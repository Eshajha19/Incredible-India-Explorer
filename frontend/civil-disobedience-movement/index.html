🧂 India · 1930 – 1934

Civil Disobedience Movement

What began as one man walking to the sea to make salt became India's largest mass movement yet — spreading through boycotts, no-tax campaigns and regional uprisings across the length of the country.

The Road from Lahore

Background and Purna Swaraj

At its December 1929 Lahore session, the Indian National Congress adopted the Purna Swaraj (complete independence) resolution, breaking with the earlier demand for dominion status. Congress fixed 26 January 1930 as the first Independence Day, to be observed with a pledge of complete independence read out in towns and villages across India.

Having declared the goal, Congress needed a method. Mahatma Gandhi proposed a nationwide campaign of civil disobedience — mass, disciplined, non-violent defiance of a specific unjust law — built around a demand simple enough for every Indian to understand: the right to make salt.

🗂️ At a Glance

  • Resolution: Purna Swaraj, Lahore session, December 1929.
  • First Independence Day: 26 January 1930.
  • Method: Mass non-violent civil disobedience.
  • Opening act: The Dandi March, 12 March – 6 April 1930.
  • Duration: 1930–1934, with a pause after the 1931 Gandhi-Irwin Pact.
Why Salt?

Salt as the Immediate Issue

A tax that touched every Indian household, regardless of class, religion or region.

🧂

A Universal Necessity

Salt was consumed by every Indian, rich or poor — making a tax on it, and a state monopoly on its manufacture, uniquely unfair and unavoidable.

⚖️

The Salt Act

British law banned Indians from producing or selling salt independently, forcing them to buy heavily taxed salt from a colonial monopoly.

🎯

A Symbol, Not Just a Tax

Gandhi chose salt precisely because breaking the law required no violence, no property destruction — only the act of picking up salt from the shore.

🌍

Global Attention

The simplicity and moral clarity of the salt issue drew international press coverage, embarrassing the colonial government on a world stage.

♟️ Elements of the Strategy

  • A single, symbolic law: Break the Salt Act publicly, disciplined and unarmed.
  • Advance notice: Gandhi wrote to Viceroy Lord Irwin beforehand, announcing his intent — denying the government the claim of being caught unaware.
  • A visible march: A long walk, gathering attention and supporters at every village along the route.
  • Replicable action: A method any Indian, anywhere, could copy without central coordination.
Discipline as Strategy

Gandhi's Strategy

Gandhi's plan combined moral clarity with practical replicability. By choosing an act as simple as scooping salt from the seashore, he gave the movement an action that villagers everywhere could repeat without needing weapons, funds, or formal organisation.

The march itself was designed as theatre with a purpose: a slow, public procession that would draw growing crowds, sympathetic press coverage, and — crucially — force the government to either ignore open lawbreaking or arrest a peaceful, elderly protester walking with a walking stick.

12 March – 6 April 1930 · 385 km

The Dandi March

Gandhi and 78 chosen followers walked from Sabarmati Ashram to the coastal village of Dandi over 24 days, gathering supporters at every stop.

Day 1

Sabarmati Ashram, Ahmedabad

Gandhi sets out on 12 March 1930 with 78 followers, having written to Viceroy Irwin days earlier announcing his intent to break the salt law.

Days 2–5

Aslali, Bareja, Navagam, Matar

The march passes through Gujarat villages, with Gandhi addressing growing crowds at each overnight halt.

Days 6–12

Nadiad, Anand, Borsad, Ras

The column swells as local volunteers join; Gandhi's daily speeches are widely reported by Indian and international press.

Days 13–20

Dabhan, Bhatgam, Dhamas, Vasna

Marchers continue toward the coast; villagers along the route greet the procession and pledge support for the wider boycott.

Days 21–23

Karadi

The march's final overnight halt before Dandi, with the crowd now numbering in the thousands.

Day 24

Dandi — 6 April 1930

Gandhi reaches the shore and picks up a lump of natural salt, formally breaking the Salt Act and launching the nationwide movement.

Regional Movements

How the Movement Spread

🌊

Dharasana Satyagraha

After Gandhi's arrest, Sarojini Naidu and Abbas Tyabji led volunteers in an unarmed attempt to occupy the Dharasana salt works, met with brutal police beatings widely reported abroad.

🏖️

Vedaranyam Salt March

C. Rajagopalachari led a parallel salt march in Tamil Nadu, from Trichinopoly to the coastal village of Vedaranyam, breaking the salt law there.

🚩

Peshawar and the Khudai Khidmatgars

Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's non-violent "Red Shirt" volunteers led mass protests in the North-West Frontier, met with firing on unarmed crowds in Qissa Khwani Bazaar.

🏭

Sholapur

News of Gandhi's arrest triggered a worker-led uprising in Sholapur that briefly took control of the city, prompting the imposition of martial law.

🌾

Bardoli No-Tax Campaign

Peasants in Bardoli, Gujarat, refused to pay land revenue in solidarity with the movement, echoing the district's earlier 1928 satyagraha.

⛰️

Manipur and Naga Hills

Rani Gaidinliu led resistance among tribal communities in Manipur and the Naga Hills, blending anti-colonial politics with regional identity movements.

Beyond Salt

Boycott Campaigns

Alongside salt law violations, the movement drew strength from an economic boycott aimed directly at colonial revenue and British commercial interests. Volunteers picketed shops selling foreign cloth and liquor, urging customers to turn back and buy Indian-made goods instead.

The boycott of foreign cloth revived demand for khadi, hand-spun cotton that Gandhi had long promoted as a tool of both economic self-reliance and quiet, everyday resistance.

🚫 Forms of Boycott

  • Foreign cloth picketing: Volunteers stood outside shops discouraging the sale of British textiles.
  • Liquor shop picketing: Aimed at cutting excise revenue, a major source of colonial income.
  • Khadi promotion: Hand-spun cloth was promoted as a patriotic, self-reliant alternative.
  • No-tax campaigns: Peasants in several regions withheld land revenue payments.
A Historic First

Women's Participation

The Civil Disobedience Movement drew Indian women into public political action on a scale never seen before.

👩‍🦱

Sarojini Naidu

Led the Dharasana Satyagraha after Gandhi's arrest, personally leading volunteers into the path of police lathis.

✊

Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay

Organised women volunteers for salt-making and picketing, and was among the first women arrested under the salt laws.

🕊️

Kasturba Gandhi

Took an active public role in the movement, courting arrest and encouraging women across Gujarat to join the picketing campaigns.

📢

Mass Mobilisation

Thousands of previously secluded women joined picket lines, made salt, and courted arrest, permanently expanding the movement's social base.

🌾 Peasant Actions

  • No-tax campaigns: Refusal to pay land revenue, most notably in Bardoli and the United Provinces.
  • No-rent campaigns: Tenants in some regions withheld rent from landlords aligned with the colonial administration.
  • Forest satyagrahas: Villagers defied forest laws restricting access to grazing land and firewood.
  • Local salt-making: Coastal peasant communities made salt independently, replicating Gandhi's example.
Rural India Joins In

Peasant Participation

The movement's reach into rural India went well beyond symbolic solidarity. In the United Provinces and parts of Gujarat, peasants withheld land revenue payments outright, directly challenging colonial finances at a moment of global economic depression, which had already driven down agricultural prices.

These agrarian campaigns fused older grievances over land revenue and forest access with the new political vocabulary of civil disobedience, broadening the movement's base far beyond the urban, educated classes who had led earlier nationalist politics.

The Colonial Response

British Repression

The scale of the movement drew a correspondingly severe official response.

🚔

Mass Arrests

Tens of thousands of participants, including Gandhi and most of the Congress leadership, were arrested over the course of the movement.

🩹

Dharasana Beatings

Police beat unarmed volunteers with steel-tipped lathis at Dharasana; American journalist Webb Miller's eyewitness report drew global condemnation.

🔫

Firing on Crowds

Police opened fire on unarmed demonstrators in Peshawar's Qissa Khwani Bazaar, killing an unconfirmed but substantial number of protestors.

📰

Press Censorship

The government restricted press coverage of the movement and repression, though foreign correspondents still carried reports abroad.

March 1931

The Gandhi-Irwin Pact

Facing mounting international criticism and a movement that showed no sign of exhausting itself, Viceroy Lord Irwin opened negotiations with Gandhi. The resulting Gandhi-Irwin Pact, signed in March 1931, was the first time the colonial government had negotiated with Congress as a political equal.

Under its terms, Gandhi agreed to suspend civil disobedience and attend the Second Round Table Conference in London, in exchange for the release of political prisoners and permission for coastal villagers to make salt for their own use.

🤝 Key Terms

  • Civil disobedience suspended by Congress.
  • Release of political prisoners not convicted of violence.
  • Permission for local, non-commercial salt production near the coast.
  • Gandhi to attend the Second Round Table Conference as Congress's sole representative.

🏛️ London, Sept–Dec 1931

  • Gandhi attended as Congress's sole official representative.
  • Talks stalled over minority representation and communal electorates.
  • No agreement was reached on the core question of self-government.
  • Gandhi returned to India in December 1931 to a harder official stance.
A Conference That Achieved Little

The Second Round Table Conference

The conference exposed deep divisions among Indian delegates over how minority communities should be represented in any future constitution — divisions the British government was in no hurry to resolve. Gandhi's insistence that Congress alone spoke for India was contested by other delegates representing separate communal and princely interests.

Gandhi returned from London without the concessions he had hoped for, and to a colonial government under a new, more confrontational Viceroy, Lord Willingdon, who moved quickly to suppress Congress once talks broke down.

1932–1934

Resumption of the Movement and Final Withdrawal

🔁

Movement Resumes

With talks broken down, Congress relaunched civil disobedience in January 1932. Gandhi and other leaders were swiftly re-arrested.

📄

Poona Pact, 1932

Amid the renewed movement, Gandhi and B. R. Ambedkar signed the Poona Pact, revising the Communal Award's provisions for depressed classes.

📉

Momentum Fades

Sustained repression, exhaustion, and leadership arrests gradually reduced the movement's intensity through 1933.

🏳️

Formal Withdrawal, 1934

Congress formally withdrew civil disobedience in April 1934, shifting focus to constructive programmes and, later, provincial elections.

🇮🇳 Lasting Impact

  • Established civil disobedience as a repeatable, mass-scale method of resistance.
  • Permanently expanded women's role in Indian political life.
  • Drew sustained international press attention to the independence cause.
  • Fed directly into the constitutional debates behind the Government of India Act, 1935.
A Movement That Changed the Terms

Long-Term Impact

Even without securing immediate independence, the Civil Disobedience Movement fundamentally changed the relationship between Congress and the colonial government. Britain had negotiated directly with Gandhi as a political equal for the first time, and could no longer treat Indian self-government as a distant, hypothetical question.

The movement's organisational reach — into villages, among women, and across regions from Peshawar to Vedaranyam — gave Congress a mass base it would draw on again in the 1937 provincial elections and, ultimately, the Quit India Movement of 1942.
