✈️ Karachi to Calcutta · January 1946

RIAF Revolt of 1946 Explorer

In January 1946, Indian airmen of the Royal Indian Air Force grounded their tools and raised their voices. What began as a mess-hall protest over food at a Karachi signals unit spread to over twenty air force stations across the subcontinent - a spark that, weeks later, would help ignite the Royal Indian Navy mutiny and reshape Britain's calculations about how much longer it could hold India.

Why the Airmen Revolted

Post-War Conditions and Grievances

The Second World War had swelled the RIAF from a token force into one carrying tens of thousands of Indian airmen. Its end brought not relief, but resentment.

🕰️

Stalled Demobilization

Airmen recruited for "hostilities only" service expected swift release once the war ended in 1945. Instead, demobilization dragged on, leaving thousands in uniform with no clear discharge date and dwindling patience.

⚖️

Racial Discrimination

Indian airmen, however skilled, were routinely denied promotion, flying duties, and technical postings given to British counterparts. Messes, clubs, and quarters were frequently segregated in practice if not in official policy.

🍚

Poor Service Conditions

Substandard rations, overcrowded barracks, and dismissive treatment by some British officers were daily irritants. At Karachi's Drigh Road signals school, a dispute over food quality became the immediate trigger.

🗞️

The INA Trials

The public trial of Indian National Army officers at the Red Fort in late 1945 had electrified opinion across the armed forces, making many rank-and-file servicemen far more receptive to open defiance of British command.

💰

Unequal Pay & Allowances

Pay scales, family allowances, and resettlement benefits for Indian personnel lagged behind those of British servicemen doing comparable work, feeding a sense that loyalty in war was not being repaid in peace.

📰

A Charged Political Climate

Nationwide agitation for independence, price inflation, and food shortages in the winter of 1945-46 meant servicemen's grievances landed on ground already primed for mass protest.

Life in the Ranks

Discrimination and Service Conditions

Although the RIAF had been formally "Indianised" in stages since the 1930s, the wartime expansion papered over deep inequalities rather than resolving them. Indian airmen who had trained on the same equipment, flown the same missions, and repaired the same aircraft as their British counterparts found promotion ceilings, separate recreational facilities, and dismissive daily treatment waiting for them once the fighting stopped.

Grievance committees set up earlier in the war had recommended reforms to pay parity and welfare that were only partially implemented. By January 1946, airmen increasingly viewed the slow pace of change not as bureaucratic delay but as a deliberate unwillingness to treat them as equals - a perception sharpened by the nationalist mood sweeping the country after the INA trials.

Day by Day

Timeline of the Revolt

Follow the protest from its first spark to its resolution.

18 January 1946

Karachi Walkout

Airmen at the signals unit in Drigh Road, Karachi, refuse duty over food quality and demobilization delays, grounding their tools in protest.

19-20 January 1946

Word Spreads

News of the Karachi stoppage travels by telegram and word of mouth to Delhi, Allahabad, and Kanpur; sympathy meetings and go-slow actions begin.

21-23 January 1946

National Spread

The protest reaches over twenty stations from Kohat to Madras. Strike committees form at major bases, drafting shared lists of demands around demobilization, pay, and equal treatment.

Late January 1946

Command Responds

Air Force commanders, wary of a full mutiny, open negotiations at several stations while quietly moving to isolate ringleaders and restore station-level control.

Late January - Early February 1946

Strike Called Off

With assurances of faster demobilization review and no mass reprisals, airmen at most stations return to duty, bringing the coordinated protest to a close within roughly a week of its start.

18 February 1946

Naval Unrest Ignites

Barely weeks later, ratings of the Royal Indian Navy in Bombay mutiny over similar grievances, drawing direct inspiration from the airmen's action and dramatically escalating the crisis.

How Authorities Reacted

Government and Command Response

🤝

Negotiation Over Confrontation

Unlike the harder line eventually taken with the Royal Indian Navy, RIAF commanders largely chose to negotiate station by station, aware that the force could not function without its Indian technical staff.

📋

Review Committees

Promises were made to review demobilization timelines and welfare grievances, with committees set up at several commands to hear airmen's representatives.

🔍

Selective Discipline

While mass punishment was avoided to prevent further escalation, identified organisers at some stations faced disciplinary action or transfer once order was restored.

🏛️

Nationalist Leaders' Caution

Congress and other nationalist leaders publicly sympathised with the airmen's grievances but urged restraint, wary of a leaderless mutiny spiralling beyond political control just as independence talks were gathering pace.

Air Force vs. Navy

Comparison with the Royal Indian Navy Mutiny

The RIAF revolt and the RIN mutiny three weeks later shared roots but diverged sharply in scale and outcome.

✈️ RIAF Revolt (Jan 1946)

  • Began at Drigh Road, Karachi over food and demobilization delays
  • Spread to 20+ land-based air stations across India
  • Largely non-violent tools-down protest and mass meetings
  • Resolved through negotiation within about a week
  • No large-scale bloodshed or fatalities reported

⚓ RIN Mutiny (Feb 1946)

  • Began aboard HMIS Talwar in Bombay over food and racist treatment
  • Spread to ships and shore establishments in Bombay, Karachi, and Calcutta
  • Escalated to seized ships, street clashes, and armed confrontation
  • Suppressed by force after several days, with negotiated surrender
  • Casualties on both sides during street violence in Bombay and Karachi

Because the RIAF revolt was resolved quietly and without violence, it is far less remembered than the RIN Mutiny - yet many historians view it as the opening act that showed the armed forces' loyalty could no longer be assumed, directly emboldening the naval ratings weeks later.

The Bigger Picture

Post-War Military Unrest of 1946

The RIAF revolt was one link in a chain of servicemen's protests that unsettled British authority in the months before independence talks began in earnest.

Nov 1945

INA Red Fort Trials Begin

Public trials of Indian National Army officers spark nationwide protests and sympathy across the armed services.

Jan 1946

RIAF Revolt

Air force stations across India strike over demobilization and discrimination.

Feb 1946

RIN Mutiny

Naval ratings mutiny in Bombay and Karachi, drawing direct inspiration from the airmen's protest.

1946

Wider Army Unease

Isolated incidents of unrest and non-cooperation are reported among Indian Army units, prompting London to reassess the reliability of Indian forces.

Feb-Mar 1946

Cabinet Mission Announced

Alarmed by the scale of unrest across the services, the British government dispatches the Cabinet Mission to negotiate a transfer of power.

Why It Mattered

Historical Significance

The RIAF revolt is often overshadowed by the more dramatic and violent RIN Mutiny that followed it, but historians increasingly treat the two as parts of a single episode of post-war military unrest that reshaped Britain's timetable for leaving India.

Together with the INA trials and the naval mutiny, the airmen's protest demonstrated that the armed forces - long considered the most reliable pillar of British rule - could no longer be counted on to remain passive. This realisation is widely credited as a key factor pushing London toward the Cabinet Mission of 1946 and, ultimately, the transfer of power in 1947.

Legacy

A Largely Forgotten First Step

Because it ended peacefully and without a single dramatic flashpoint, the RIAF revolt rarely appears in popular retellings of 1946. Yet its scale - over twenty stations and thousands of airmen - and its timing just weeks before the RIN Mutiny make it an essential first chapter for understanding how post-war military unrest, not just political negotiation, hastened the end of British rule in India.
