📜 Biography

Batukeshwar Dutt (18 November 1910 – 20 July 1965) was an Indian revolutionary and freedom fighter, best remembered for his legendary partnership with Bhagat Singh in the dramatic bomb attack on the Central Legislative Assembly on 8 April 1929.

Born in a middle-class Bengali family at Khanari, Bardhaman district, Bengal, Dutt was drawn to the nationalist and revolutionary currents of the age while still a student. His education took him from Bengal to institutions such as the National College in Lahore, where he came under the spell of revolutionary politics and the growing Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA).

By 1928–29, Dutt had become a committed member of the HSRA, sharing its ideology of complete independence achieved through revolutionary action. It was within this circle that he forged his defining partnership with Bhagat Singh and fellow revolutionaries such as Sukhdev and Chandrashekhar Azad.

On 8 April 1929, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw two smoke bombs into the well of the Central Legislative Assembly in Delhi, shouting "Inquilab Zindabad! Long Live Revolution!" and showering leaflets announcing that the bombs were aimed not at human life but at the colonial system itself. Rather than fleeing, the two deliberately courted arrest, transforming a symbolic protest into a world-famous act of political defiance.

Their subsequent trial — the Central Assembly Bomb Case — became a public spectacle of revolutionary rhetoric. Both men were sentenced to transportation for life and imprisoned in a chain of jails. Dutt endured years in Lahore, Multan, and Hazaribagh Central Jail, even during the Lahore Conspiracy Case period in which his comrade Bhagat Singh was sentenced to death.

Released in the late 1930s, Dutt lived to see independence, marrying and settling into a quieter public life, though he remained an active Congress worker and speaker. He passed away on 20 July 1965 in New Delhi. In 1967, India Post honoured him with a commemorative postage stamp — a lasting tribute to the man who made the empire's legislature hear the cry of revolution.

💥 The Central Legislative Assembly Bombing

8 April 1929 — the protest that made the deaf hear.

A Protest Against Repressive Bills

The Assembly bombing must be understood against the backdrop of the Public Safety Bill and the Trade Disputes Bill, which the colonial government was rushing through the Central Legislative Assembly to crush labour and revolutionary activity. The HSRA resolved to stage a dramatic protest that would, in Bhagat Singh and Dutt's own words, make "the deaf hear" and give the nation "a necessary stimulus."

The Act of 8 April 1929

On the afternoon of 8 April 1929, as the Assembly sat in session, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt rose from the visitors' gallery and hurled two bombs into the chamber. The bombs — deliberately small and filled with low-strength explosive — crashed with great noise but were designed not to maim. Instantly the two men began shouting "Inquilab Zindabad!" and "Long Live Revolution!" before calmly scattering revolutionary leaflets and surrendering to the guards without resistance.

"It takes a loud voice to make the deaf hear. Our bombs were such a voice. We wished to do no injury to anyone."
— Batukeshwar Dutt, from the Dock, Central Assembly Bomb Case

Why They Stood, Not Fled

The pair refused to escape. Their surrender was itself the statement: revolution was not to be hidden in shadows but proclaimed in broad daylight before the seats of power. The colonial press erupted; the government — startled and stung — resorted to draconian measures, and the event catapulted the revolutionary cause into the forefront of national attention.

🔒 Imprisonment & The Trial

From the dock of fame to years behind colonial walls.

Trial as a Platform

The Central Assembly Bomb Case trial of Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt in 1929 became one of the most electrifying political trials of colonial India. Refusing to treat the proceeding as a criminal farce, the two revolutionaries turned the courtroom into a forum for the ideals of the HSRA, delivering speeches on imperialism, exploitation, and the necessity of revolution that resonated across the country.

Sentence and Privations

On 12 June 1929, both men were sentenced to transportation for life. Thereafter Dutt was confined across a succession of prisons — Lahore, Multan, and Hazaribagh Central Jail — enduring harsh conditions. The years of the Lahore Conspiracy Case, which ended in the martyrdom of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, and Sukhdev on 23 March 1931, marked the darkest period of his imprisonment.

"We are sorry we cannot offer to it (the court) a fair exchange — of life for life, or blood for blood — because we hold human life sacred above all. But we do want the privileged order to know that as long as it exists, the poor and the exploited will have to fight for their rights."
— Excerpt from the courtroom statement of Bhagat Singh & Batukeshwar Dutt

Release and Survival

Dutt was finally released in the late 1930s. Unlike several comrades who fell to the gallows, he survived to carry the memory and ideals of the revolutionary movement into independent India, a living link between the sacrifices of the 1920s and the young republic.

🌟 Legacy & Impact

The enduring memory of the man who made the empire's legislature hear the cry of revolution.

A Stamp of Honour

In 1967, India Post issued a commemorative stamp bearing his image, honouring a revolutionary whose courage announced, in the very heart of colonial power, that British rule in India could never be secure.

Commemoration and Remembrance

The Batukeshwar Dutt Park in the Lodi Estate area of New Delhi, near the site of the old Central Secretariat, stands as a public reminder of his deed. His birthplace district of Bardhaman, as well as institutions and streets across India, also remember his name.

Place in the Revolutionary Pantheon

Though Bhagat Singh's name looms largest over the Assembly bombing, historians acknowledge that Dutt was an equal partner in the act — his presence, his clamour, and his court testimony inseparable from the event. Together the two men demonstrated that Indian revolutionaries would take responsibility for their deeds openly, defiantly, and without bloodshed.

💥 Remembering the Voice of the Assembly

When students of history recall 8 April 1929, they hear two names — Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt — and one cry, "Inquilab Zindabad!" In that cry, British India heard, for the first time in its own legislature, the unmistakable sound of an empire's end approaching.
