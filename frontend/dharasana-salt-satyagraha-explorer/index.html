Why Dharasana mattered

01 · SALT LAWS

A national grievance

02 · CIVIL DISOBEDIENCE

From Dandi to Dharasana

The Salt March opened a wider campaign in which people challenged colonial laws through organized, non-violent resistance.

→
03 · DHARASANA

A major confrontation

Volunteers attempted to approach the protected salt works while colonial forces used severe physical repression against protesters.

Gandhi's arrest changed the leadership

Gandhi was arrested on 5 May 1930 before the planned Dharasana action. Leadership subsequently passed to other national and local figures, including Abbas Tyabji and, after his arrest, Sarojini Naidu. The transition demonstrates that the campaign was larger than one individual.

Dharasana became an important test of whether disciplined non-violent action could continue under repression.

International response

Dharasana reached audiences far beyond Gujarat

Journalistic reporting

American journalist Webb Miller's reporting described the violence inflicted on volunteers and helped make Dharasana a widely discussed example of colonial repression. His dispatches contributed to international attention around India's civil-disobedience campaign.

The reporting mattered politically because it presented a contrast between the protesters' declared non-violence and the force used against them.

Why the story travelled

  • International news agencies carried reports beyond India.
  • The treatment of volunteers became part of the global discussion of British rule.
  • Visual and written accounts strengthened sympathy for India's independence movement.
  • The event helped connect a local salt action to an international political audience.

After Dandi, civil disobedience spreads through salt-making, boycotts, picketing and other local actions.

AFTER

Repression becomes international news

Dharasana demonstrates the costs of colonial enforcement while sustaining the campaign's non-violent discipline.

  • International attention grows
  • Leadership networks prove resilient
  • Pressure for political negotiations increases

Connection to Gandhi–Irwin negotiations

The wider Civil Disobedience campaign, including events such as Dharasana, formed part of the political pressure surrounding negotiations between the Indian National Congress and the colonial government. The Gandhi–Irwin Pact was signed in March 1931, leading to a temporary suspension of civil disobedience and Gandhi's participation in the Second Round Table Conference.
