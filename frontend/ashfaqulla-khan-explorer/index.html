The Kakori Hero of 1925

Ashfaqulla Khan

Revolutionary, idealist, and martyr — one of the youngest founders of the Hindustan Republican Association and a key figure in the 9 August 1925 Kakori Train Action that electrified the Indian freedom struggle.

22 October 1900 — 19 December 1927

Biography

Ashfaqulla Khan was born on 22 October 1900 in Shahjahanpur, in the United Provinces of Agra and Oudh (now Uttar Pradesh), into a respected Pathan Muslim family. His father, Shafiqullah Khan, was a police inspector, and the family was steeped in both Islamic learning and the emerging nationalist consciousness of late-19th-century north India.

Ashfaq was a brilliant student. He passed his matriculation from Mission High School, Shahjahanpur, and enrolled at Islamia College, Lucknow, before moving to the Government Commercial Institute in Kanpur. From an early age he was drawn to the broader Indian freedom movement — by his early teens he had been captivated by the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–22 led by Mahatma Gandhi.

It was in Shahjahanpur that he met Ram Prasad Bismil, a fellow resident of the town and already a hardened revolutionary. Despite the differences of religion, the two became inseparable friends. Their friendship — Bismil the Hindu, Ashfaq the Muslim — would become one of the iconic symbols of Hindu-Muslim unity in the freedom struggle, and the basis of a working partnership that would reshape the revolutionary movement.

When Gandhi withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement after the Chauri Chaura incident of February 1922, Ashfaq and Bismil were among the young nationalists who rejected the return to constitutional politics. They argued that without the pressure of an armed revolutionary underground, the British would never concede self-rule. In October 1924, they were among the founders of the Hindustan Republican Association (HRA) at Kanpur — the organisation that would later, in 1928, become the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA) under Bhagat Singh and Chandrashekhar Azad.

The HRA needed funds. The young revolutionaries were mostly students and small-town clerks; they had no weapons, no foreign backing, and no wealthy patrons. After ruling out robberies of private individuals (which they considered immoral), they decided to target government revenue itself — specifically the cash chests carried by the railways on the mail trains.

On 9 August 1925, Ashfaqulla Khan and Ram Prasad Bismil led a group of ten revolutionaries in stopping the Number 8 Down Saharanpur–Lucknow passenger train at Kakori station, about 15 miles north of Lucknow. They pulled the emergency cord, overpowered the guard, and broke open the cash chest containing government treasury funds. The action itself took less than ten minutes, but its symbolic impact was enormous — a direct armed challenge to the financial machinery of the British Indian government.

In the chaotic aftermath of the action, one passenger was killed by a stray bullet — Ahmed Ali, a lawyer. The British responded with one of the largest manhunts in Indian history. Most of the conspirators were arrested within weeks. Ashfaqulla Khan, however, escaped, travelling first to Nepal, then to Khandwa in central India, where he lived under an assumed name for several months. He was finally betrayed by a fellow traveler and arrested in Delhi in December 1925.

The subsequent Kakori Conspiracy Case (1925–1927) tried 28 revolutionaries. Of these, four were sentenced to death: Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, Rajendra Lahiri, and Thakur Roshan Singh. The case became a cause célèbre across India; the dignity and idealism of the accused during the trial won them enormous public sympathy.

Ashfaqulla Khan was hanged on 19 December 1927 at the Faizabad District Jail, aged 27. His hanging coincided almost exactly with that of Bismil at Gorakhpur Jail. To the end he refused to appeal for clemency, insisting that his actions were a legitimate act of war against a colonial ruler. His final letter, written in Urdu, declared: "I am a Muslim and proud of it. I am an Indian and proud of it. I am a revolutionary and proud of it."

    On 4 February 1922 a violent mob burns the police station at Chauri Chaura, killing 22 constables. Gandhi withdraws the Non-Cooperation Movement on 12 February. Ashfaq and Bismil, like many young nationalists, lose faith in constitutional non-violence.

    Founding of the HRA

    In October, the Hindustan Republican Association (HRA) is founded at a secret meeting in Kanpur. Founders include Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, Jogesh Chatterjee, Sachindra Sanyal, and Sachindranath Bakshi. The HRA constitution calls for an independent federal republic of India based on universal adult suffrage.

    Kakori Train Action

    On 9 August 1925, Ashfaqulla Khan and Ram Prasad Bismil lead ten HRA revolutionaries in halting the Number 8 Down passenger train at Kakori station, north of Lucknow. They break open the cash chest and seize government treasury funds worth around ₹4,600. A passenger, Ahmed Ali, is killed by a stray bullet.

  7. 1925

    Manhunt & Escape

    Within weeks most HRA members are arrested. Ashfaq escapes — travelling through Bareilly, Delhi, Banaras, and into Nepal, before returning under the alias "Hifazat Khan" to Khandwa in central India.

  8. 1925

    Arrest

    Betrayed by a fellow traveler (Pathan Qurban Hussain), Ashfaq is arrested in Delhi in December 1925, ten months after the Kakori action.

  9. 1926

    Kakori Trial

    The British government files the Kakori Conspiracy Case against 28 HRA members. The trial runs through 1926 and 1927 at the special sessions court in Lucknow.

  10. 1927

    Death Sentence

    The court sentences four men to death — Ashfaqulla Khan, Ram Prasad Bismil, Rajendra Lahiri, and Thakur Roshan Singh — and many others to transportation for life. Nationalist leaders across India appeal for clemency.

  11. 1927

    Execution at Faizabad

    On 19 December 1927, Ashfaqulla Khan is hanged at the Faizabad District Jail, aged 27. Ram Prasad Bismil is hanged the same day at Gorakhpur Jail. Rajendra Lahiri had been hanged two days earlier at Gonda Jail.

  12. 1928

    HSRA Continuation

    Chandrashekhar Azad, Bhagat Singh, and others reorganise the HRA as the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA), explicitly carrying forward the ideals for which Ashfaq and Bismil had been hanged.

  13. 1947

    Independence

    India gains independence on 15 August 1947, twenty years after the Kakori martyrs' execution.

Revolutionary Activities

Ashfaqulla Khan's revolutionary career was short but decisive. Between 1922 and 1925 he helped build the first organised armed underground aimed at overthrowing British colonial rule. The movement he helped found — the Hindustan Republican Association (HRA) — would directly evolve into the better-known HSRA of Bhagat Singh and Chandrashekhar Azad.

Founding the HRA (October 1924)

After the withdrawal of the Non-Cooperation Movement, a group of young revolutionaries from the United Provinces, Punjab, and Bengal met in Kanpur in October 1924 to form the HRA. The founders — Bismil, Ashfaq, Jogesh Chatterjee, Sachindra Sanyal, and Sachindranath Bakshi — drafted a constitution that called for the establishment of a federal republic of the United States of India based on universal adult suffrage, the abolition of all systems of exploitation, and the socialisation of industry.

The manifesto, titled The Revolutionary, was drafted largely by Bismil and was circulated in print and by hand across north India. It was the first organised attempt by Indian revolutionaries to articulate a clear political philosophy that combined anti-colonial nationalism with proto-socialist economics.

The Kakori Train Action (9 August 1925)

The HRA needed funds to publish its manifesto, buy arms, and sustain its members. After ruling out robberies of private individuals, the leaders decided to target government revenue — specifically the cash chests carried on mail and passenger trains by the railway treasury service.

On the evening of 9 August 1925, ten revolutionaries boarded the Number 8 Down passenger train running from Saharanpur to Lucknow. At Kakori station, about 24 km north of Lucknow, Ram Prasad Bismil pulled the emergency chain. Ashfaqulla Khan led the small group that entered the guard's van, overpowered the railway guard, and broke open the iron cash chest containing the government treasury funds for the Lucknow division.

The sum taken was relatively small — about ₹4,600 in cash and some railway securities. But the action's significance was never monetary. It was a direct, armed challenge to the financial apparatus of the colonial state, and the publication of the news the next morning electrified the country.

In the chaos of the action, however, a passenger — Ahmed Ali, a Lucknow lawyer — was killed by a stray bullet. The HRA's constitution explicitly forbade harm to innocents, and the killing became a moral stain on the movement that its leaders carried heavily.

Ideals and Philosophy

Ashfaqulla Khan's ideals combined three streams:

  • Anti-colonial nationalism: The belief that British rule was illegitimate and that Indians had a moral right to use force to overthrow it, since the British had themselves used force to impose it.
  • Hindu-Muslim unity: The HRA's founding core included Hindus (Bismil, Roshan Singh), Muslims (Ashfaq), and Bengali revolutionaries (Sanyal, Chatterjee). Ashfaq saw Hindu-Muslim cooperation as a precondition for any successful national revolution, and his friendship with Bismil was held up as proof that religious difference was no barrier to revolutionary comradeship.
  • Socialist republicanism: Influenced by the Russian Revolution of 1917 and by Lenin's writings, Ashfaq believed that political independence was meaningless without economic redistribution. The HRA constitution called for "the abolition of all systems which make the exploitation of man by man possible."

Underground Years (August–December 1925)

After Kakori, Ashfaq spent five months in hiding. He travelled through Bareilly and Delhi, slipped into Nepal, and finally returned to central India under the alias "Hifazat Khan", working as a clerk in Khandwa (now in Madhya Pradesh). The British announced a reward for his capture, and he was eventually betrayed by a fellow Pathan, Qurban Hussain, in Delhi in December 1925.

The Kakori Trial and Martyrdom

The British government filed the Kakori Conspiracy Case against 28 HRA members in 1926. The trial lasted nearly two years. Ashfaq defended himself with dignity, refusing to apologise or to seek mercy. In his final statement he declared that he had acted as a soldier in a war of national liberation, not as a common criminal.

On 19 December 1927, Ashfaqulla Khan was hanged at the Faizabad District Jail. He was 27 years old. His execution alongside those of Bismil, Lahiri, and Roshan Singh produced a wave of public grief across India and transformed the Kakori martyrs into enduring symbols of revolutionary sacrifice.

Legacy

The Kakori action was the model for every subsequent Indian revolutionary fundraising operation, including the HSRA's actions under Bhagat Singh and the Chittagong armoury raid under Surya Sen. The ideals of the HRA — a secular, federal, socialist republic of India — anticipated the political structure that independent India would adopt in 1950. Today, Ashfaqulla Khan is honoured as a national martyr; the Indian Posts & Telegraphs Department issued a commemorative stamp in his memory in 1997.
