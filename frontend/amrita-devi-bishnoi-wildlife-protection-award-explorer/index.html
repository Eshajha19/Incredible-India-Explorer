📜 History of the Award

The Amrita Devi Bishnoi Wildlife Protection Award is a national honour instituted by the Government of India's Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change to recognise exceptional efforts in wildlife conservation and environmental protection. It is presented every year on September 11 — National Forest Martyrs Day — the anniversary of the Khejarli massacre.

The Legend of Khejarli

In 1730, the Maharaja of Jodhpur, Abhay Singh, ordered the felling of Khejri trees (Prosopis cineraria) in the village of Khejarli near Jodhpur, Rajasthan, to obtain wood for a new palace. The Bishnoi community, bound by the teachings of their founder Guru Jambheshwar to protect trees and wildlife, refused to allow the felling.

Led by Amrita Devi, the villagers embraced the trees to shield them. Amrita Devi was beheaded as she hugged a Khejri tree, and her three daughters — Asu, Ratni, and Bhagu — followed. In all, 363 Bishnois sacrificed their lives that day. Moved by their devotion, the Maharaja later repented and decreed that the Bishnoi community and their trees would never again be harmed.

From State Honour to National Award

The governments of Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh first instituted state-level Amrita Devi Bishnoi Awards with a cash prize of ₹25,000 to honour those protecting wildlife. Later, the Ministry of Environment and Forests elevated the recognition into a national award to celebrate grassroots defenders of wildlife across India.

Why It Matters Today

The award is more than a ceremony — it is a living tribute to India's oldest recorded forest-protection movement. The Khejarli sacrifice is widely regarded as the precursor of the Chipko movement, and the award keeps the spirit of people-led conservation alive in modern environmental policy.

💡 Inspiration Behind the Award

The award draws its inspiration from the extraordinary story of Amrita Devi Bishnoi and the Bishnoi community, whose sacrifice in 1730 is considered the world's first recorded movement for the protection of trees and wildlife.

🌳 29 Principles of the Bishnoi

The Bishnoi sect, founded by Guru Jambheshwar (Jambhoji) in 1485, follows 29 principles — "Bisnoi" literally means "twenty-nine." Of these, eight principles focus directly on environmental protection, compassion for living beings, and conserving trees and wildlife.

🦌 Sacred Coexistence

For the Bishnoi, the blackbuck, chinkara, and Khejri tree are sacred symbols of a deep ecological ethic. Villages live in harmony with wildlife, treating animals as members of the family — an ethos the award seeks to propagate nationwide.

🕊️ Sacrifice Before Self

Amrita Devi's final words — that a chopped head was cheaper than a chopped tree — embodied the ultimate environmental commitment. Her courage transformed personal faith into a national conservation legacy.

🌍 Precursor to Chipko

The Khejarli movement directly inspired later people's movements, most notably Gaura Devi and the Chipko andolan of 1974 in Uttarakhand, where villagers hugged trees to stop deforestation.

📋 Eligibility Criteria

👥 Eligible Candidates

Individuals and community-based organisations from rural areas, including Gram Sabhas, who have shown exemplary courage or valour for the protection of wildlife.

🎗️ What is Recognised

Significant contributions in the field of wildlife protection — either courageous acts of valour against poaching and hunting, or exemplary long-term conservation work in the community.

✅ No Age Limit

There is no age limit for individuals, allowing young conservationists and senior community guardians alike to be honoured.

🚫 Who Cannot Apply

Government officials and government servants are not eligible for the award, keeping the focus on grassroots and community-led conservation.

📅 Annual Recognition

The award is conferred annually, with nominations invited by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change every year.

🏷️ Nature of the Award

Each awardee receives a cash prize of ₹1,00,000, along with a medallion and a citation presented by the Government of India.

🎯 Selection Process

The selection process is designed to identify genuine grassroots heroes whose work has visibly protected wildlife and inspired their communities.

1

Nomination Call

The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change issues an annual call inviting nominations from all State Governments and Union Territory administrations.

2

Public Recommendation

Any citizen of India can also recommend a deserving individual or community for the award, along with supporting documentation.

3

Documentation & Verification

Each nomination must include the candidate's name and address, area of work, and details of their significant contribution to wildlife protection.

4

Chief Wildlife Warden Review

Nominations must carry the comments and recommendations of the Chief Wildlife Warden of the respective State or Union Territory.

5

Evaluation by the Ministry

The Ministry's committee evaluates each nomination for the depth of impact, courage shown, and the conservation value of the nominee's work.

6

Award Ceremony

Awardees are honoured on 11 September, National Forest Martyrs Day, in a ceremony that pays homage to Amrita Devi and the 363 martyrs of Khejarli.

Evaluation Focus Areas

  • Exemplary Courage: Acts of valour against poaching, hunting, and wildlife crime
  • Conservation Impact: Measurable improvement in the protection of species and habitats
  • Community Participation: Grassroots involvement including Gram Sabhas and village institutions
  • Inspiration Value: The nominee's work as a model for others in the country
  • Sustainability: Long-term, consistent commitment rather than isolated acts

🦌 Wildlife Conservation & the Bishnoi Legacy

The award celebrates a conservation philosophy that puts communities at the heart of protecting India's wildlife. The Bishnoi of Rajasthan remain a living example — their villages host healthy populations of blackbuck, chinkara, and Indian gazelle, protected not by fences but by community conviction.

🦌

Blackbuck & Chinkara

Sacred to the Bishnoi, these antelopes roam freely in and around Bishnoi villages. Community guards ("vala") track herds and alert villages to poachers, even at personal risk.

🌳

Khejri & Sacred Groves

The Khejri tree is a lifeline of the Thar desert — its pods, leaves, and shade sustain people and animals alike. Bishnoi protect these trees as family, preserving entire sacred groves.

🦅

Vulture & Bird Colonies

Bishnoi hamlets host thriving colonies of migratory birds and vultures near water bodies, showing how traditional reverence translates into practical avian conservation.

🌾

Human-Wildlife Coexistence

The award recognises communities that manage human-wildlife conflict through tolerance and innovation — from crop-protection techniques to community water points for animals.

🚔

Anti-Poaching Valour

Many awardees are ordinary villagers who have confronted poachers and smugglers to save deer, tigers, and other wildlife — often risking their own lives in the process.

🌱

Grassroots Conservation Hubs

Awarded Gram Sabhas and village organisations become models of decentralised conservation, later inspiring similar efforts across other states.

Why This Matters

India is home to some of the world's most endangered wildlife. The Amrita Devi Bishnoi Wildlife Protection Award shines a light on the unsung defenders — farmers, herders, and villagers — whose everyday courage keeps India's biodiversity alive. It reminds the nation that conservation succeeds best when communities lead the way.

Impact of the Award

  • Provides national visibility to grassroots wildlife defenders who would otherwise remain unsung
  • Encourages more villages to adopt community-led protection of local species and habitats
  • Strengthens the message of the Bishnoi ethic — that humans and wildlife can thrive together
  • Celebrates rural India's role as the true custodian of the nation's biodiversity
  • Keeps the memory of Amrita Devi and the Khejarli martyrs alive in public consciousness

⏳ Timeline & Milestones

1485

Guru Jambheshwar Founds the Bishnoi Sect

In the Thar desert of Rajasthan, Guru Jambheshwar establishes the Bishnoi faith with 29 principles, including the protection of trees and wildlife.

1730

The Khejarli Sacrifice

Amrita Devi and 363 Bishnois lay down their lives to protect the sacred Khejri trees from the Maharaja of Jodhpur's woodcutters — the world's first recorded forest-protection movement.

1731

Royal Repentance

Moved by the sacrifice, Maharaja Abhay Singh orders that Bishnoi villages and their trees be left unharmed, setting a legal precedent for community-protected groves.

1974

Chipko Movement Inspired

The Khejarli legacy inspires Gaura Devi and the Chipko andolan in Uttarakhand, where villagers hugged trees to prevent deforestation.

2000

National Award Instituted

The Ministry of Environment and Forests institutes the Amrita Devi Bishnoi Wildlife Protection Award, building on the earlier state awards of Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh.

2001

First Award Conferred

On 11 September 2001, the first national award is conferred posthumously on Ganga Ram Bishnoi of Jodhpur, who died protecting chinkaras from poachers.

2013

National Award Reaffirmed

The Ministry of Environment and Forests reaffirms the Amrita Devi Bishnoi National Award, standardising the ₹1 lakh cash prize, medallion, and citation for deserving individuals and communities.

Today

Annual National Forest Martyrs Day

Every 11 September, the award honours new guardians of wildlife as the nation remembers Amrita Devi and the 363 martyrs of Khejarli.

✨ Interesting Facts

🌍 World's First Environmental Movement

The 1730 Khejarli sacrifice is widely regarded as the first recorded people's movement for environmental protection in world history — over two centuries before modern environmentalism began.

🔢 "Bishnoi" Means Twenty-Nine

The name of the community comes from the 29 principles (bis = twenty, noi = nine) laid down by Guru Jambheshwar, several of which mandate kindness to animals and protection of trees.

💚 The Khejri Tree of Life

The Khejri (Prosopis cineraria) is Rajasthan's state tree and a desert lifeline — its pods feed livestock, its bark yields gum, and its shade shelters communities.

🎖️ A Hero Honoured Twice

The first awardee, Ganga Ram Bishnoi, was also posthumously conferred the Shaurya Chakra — one of India's highest peacetime gallantry awards — for his courage against poachers.

📅 A Shared Memorial Day

September 11 is celebrated as National Forest Martyrs Day, honouring not only the Khejarli martyrs but also the many forest guards and rangers who have fallen protecting India's forests and wildlife.

🏡 Villages Without Walls

In Bishnoi villages, deer roam freely beside homes and are fed by villagers — a living demonstration that faith-based conservation can protect species more effectively than fences alone.

🤝 Government Officials Excluded

To keep the focus on grassroots effort, government officials and servants are ineligible for the award — the spotlight belongs to ordinary citizens and rural communities.

🌱 A Legacy That Grows

Beyond the ceremony, awardees' villages become conservation role models, inspiring replication of Bishnoi-style community guardianship in other parts of the country.
