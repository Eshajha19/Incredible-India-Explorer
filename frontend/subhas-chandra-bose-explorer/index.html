Subhas Chandra Bose

"Give me blood, and I shall give you freedom!" - The fiery leader of the Indian National Army.

The Defiant Nationalist

Born on January 23, 1897, in Cuttack, Odisha, Subhas Chandra Bose emerged as one of the most prominent and radical voices in the Indian independence movement. Unlike the mainstream leadership that advocated for non-violent civil disobedience, Bose believed that armed struggle and international alliances were necessary to expel the British Empire from India.

Educated in England and a brilliant civil service candidate, he resigned from the Indian Civil Service (ICS) to dedicate his life to the freedom struggle. He rose through the ranks of the Indian National Congress, serving as its president in 1938 and 1939, before ideological differences with Mahatma Gandhi led him to form the Forward Bloc.

Escape and International Alliances

Under house arrest by the British in 1941, Bose executed a daring escape, traveling through Afghanistan and the Soviet Union to reach Germany. He sought the help of the Axis powers to raise an army and eventually made his way to Southeast Asia, where he took command of the Indian National Army (INA).

Historical Timeline

The Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)

The INA was originally formed by Mohan Singh in 1942 using Indian prisoners of war captured by the Japanese. However, it was under Bose's leadership, assuming command in 1943, that the INA became a formidable fighting force and a powerful symbol of Indian unity.

Provisional Government

On October 21, 1943, Bose announced the formation of the Provisional Government of Free India (Azad Hind) in Singapore, which was recognized by several nations and declared war on Britain.

Rani of Jhansi Regiment

Bose established one of the first all-female combat regiments in Asia, led by Captain Lakshmi Sahgal, showcasing his progressive vision for gender equality in the armed struggle.

Battle of Imphal & Kohima

In 1944, the INA, alongside Japanese forces, launched an offensive into northeastern India. Though ultimately repelled by British and Commonwealth forces, the campaign severely shook British confidence in their Indian troops.
