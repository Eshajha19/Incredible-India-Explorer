🇮🇳 Gandhian Movement · 1919

Rowlatt Satyagraha

Gandhi's first all-India satyagraha — launched against the repressive Rowlatt Acts of 1919, it united the country in hartals, protests, and civil disobedience, and set the stage for the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the Non-Cooperation Movement that followed.

📜 Background

Political Conditions of 1919

The Post-War Context

India had contributed massively to the British war effort in World War I — over 1.3 million Indian soldiers served overseas, and India donated £100 million to the war. Indians expected a reward in the form of self-government (swaraj) after the war.

Instead, the British responded with repression. The wartime Defence of India Act (1915), which gave the government extraordinary powers of arrest and detention, was due to expire. The British decided not to let these powers lapse — they wanted them permanently.

The Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms

Simultaneously, the British offered the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms (Government of India Act 1919), which introduced limited "dyarchy" — partial Indian control of some provincial departments, with British control retained over key portfolios like finance, police, and land revenue.

Indian nationalists saw this as too little, too late. The Congress split — moderates accepted the reforms as a step forward; extremists like Tilak rejected them as inadequate. Gandhi, freshly returned from Champaran and Kheda, found himself poised between the two camps.

📕 The Rowlatt Acts

Main Provisions & Political Concerns

The Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919

Officially known as the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act of 1919, the law was popularly called the Rowlatt Act after Justice Sidney Rowlatt, who chaired the committee that recommended it. It was passed on 18 March 1919 despite unanimous Indian opposition in the Imperial Legislative Council.

Detention Without Trial

The Act authorised the government to imprison any person suspected of terrorism or revolutionary activity for up to two years without trial. The accused had no right to know the evidence against them.

Special Courts

Special tribunals were created to try political cases without juries. These courts' decisions could not be appealed. The Act effectively created a parallel legal system for political suspects.

Restricted Habeas Corpus

The right of habeas corpus — the fundamental protection against arbitrary detention — was suspended for suspects under the Act. A person could be held indefinitely without judicial review.

Search & Seizure

Police were given sweeping powers to search premises and seize property without warrant. Press censorship was strengthened, and seditious publications could be confiscated.

"No Vakil, No Appeal, No Dalil"

Indian members of the Legislative Council summed up the Act with the phrase: No lawyer, no appeal, no argument. It stripped away every protection that Indian subjects had under British law.

Permanence of Emergency Powers

Unlike the wartime Defence of India Act, the Rowlatt Act made emergency powers permanent in peacetime. It transformed colonial India into a permanent state of exception for political dissent.

⚖️ Opposition

Opposition from Indian Leaders

Mohandas Gandhi

Gandhi was outraged by the Act. He called it a "black act" and declared that Indians should not obey a law so unjust. He formed a Satyagraha Sabha in Bombay and called for a nationwide protest.

Muhammad Ali Jinnah

Jinnah resigned from the Imperial Legislative Council in protest, declaring the Act "obnoxious and ill-conceived." His resignation made him a national hero overnight and marked his break with moderate Congress politics.

Madan Mohan Malaviya

Malaviya and other Indian members of the Legislative Council voted against the bill but were outvoted by the official British majority. He called it a betrayal of Indian loyalty during the war.

Mazharul Haque

The Bihar leader resigned his Council seat in protest. He mobilised Muslim opinion in northern India and worked with Gandhi to ensure Hindu-Muslim unity in the opposition.

Surendranath Banerjea

A veteran moderate leader, Banerjea resigned from the Bengal Legislative Council. His resignation symbolised how even moderate Indian opinion had turned against British rule.

The Indian Press

Newspapers across India condemned the Act. The Bombay Chronicle, edited by Horniman, became a focal point of opposition. The press would play a crucial role in the protests that followed.

✊ The Satyagraha

Gandhi's Call & the Hartals

The Satyagraha Pledge

On 24 February 1919, Gandhi convened a meeting in Bombay and drafted the Satyagraha Pledge. Signatories promised to refuse civil obedience to the Rowlatt Act and other unjust laws. The pledge was signed by hundreds of Indians across the country.

Initially, Gandhi set 30 March 1919 as the day for a nationwide hartal (total strike). The date was later moved to 6 April. The hartal called for a total shutdown of business, fasting, prayer, and peaceful protest.

The 6 April Hartal

On 6 April 1919, India observed a near-total hartal. Shops closed, workers stayed home, and processions filled the streets of Delhi, Bombay, Ahmedabad, Calcutta, Madras, Lahore, and other cities. It was the first truly all-India protest in the country's history.

The hartal was largely peaceful, but in some places, the situation escalated. In Delhi, a procession clashed with police; in Punjab, the hartal coincided with the festival of Ram Navami, swelling the crowds and raising tensions.

📅 Timeline

Major Developments (Feb–Apr 1919)

Feb 6

Rowlatt Report Published

The Rowlatt Committee's report recommending permanent emergency powers is released. Indian opinion is uniformly hostile.

Feb 24

Satyagraha Sabha Formed

Gandhi convenes a meeting in Bombay and forms the Satyagraha Sabha. Members sign the satyagraha pledge to disobey unjust laws.

Mar 18

Rowlatt Act Passed

Despite unanimous Indian opposition, the Act passes the Imperial Legislative Council with the support of the British official majority.

Mar 30

First Hartal (Delhi)

Delhi observes the first hartal, originally scheduled as the main day of protest. Shops close, processions fill the streets. Minor clashes with police occur.

Apr 6

Nationwide Hartal

The main hartal day. India shuts down — shops, markets, and offices close. Peaceful processions in Bombay, Ahmedabad, Calcutta, Madras, Lahore.

Apr 7

Gandhi Invited to Punjab

Punjab leaders invite Gandhi to visit Amritsar and Lahore to lead the movement there. He begins the journey.

Apr 9

Gandhi Arrested at Palwal

Gandhi is arrested at Palwal railway station (near Delhi) while travelling to Punjab. He is escorted back to Bombay. News of his arrest sparks outrage.

Apr 10

Punjab Erupts

News of Gandhi's arrest triggers riots in Amritsar and Lahore. British officials are killed, banks and government buildings burned. Martial law is declared in Punjab.

Apr 13

Jallianwala Bagh Massacre

Brigadier General Dyer opens fire on a peaceful gathering of 10,000+ people at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar. Hundreds killed, over a thousand wounded.

Apr 18

Satyagraha Suspended

Gandhi suspends the satyagraha, acknowledging that the people were not yet ready for mass civil disobedience. He undertakes a three-day fast as penance.

🔥 Punjab

Punjab Developments

Why Punjab Exploded

Punjab had suffered disproportionately during WWI — over 400,000 Punjabis were recruited, many forcibly. Wartime taxation, inflation, and forced labour had impoverished the province. The Rowlatt Act was the final straw.

Two popular leaders, Dr. Satyapal and Dr. Kitchlew, were arrested and deported from Amritsar on 10 April. This triggered mass protests that escalated into violence. British banks, churches, and government offices were attacked.

Martial Law & Repression

Lieutenant Governor Michael O'Dwyer declared martial law across Punjab. The infamous "crawling order" forced Indians to crawl on their bellies past a British missionary who had been assaulted. Public floggings, arbitrary arrests, and aerial bombing of villages followed.

The repression was unprecedented in its severity and was later condemned by the Hunter Commission. Punjab became a symbol of British brutality and Indian resistance.

🕯️ Jallianwala Bagh

The Connection to the Massacre

The Direct Link

The Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 13 April 1919 was a direct consequence of the Rowlatt Satyagraha and the Punjab unrest that followed. On that day, approximately 10,000–20,000 people — including women and children — gathered in the enclosed garden of Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar.

Many were peaceful protesters against the Rowlatt Act; others had simply come for the Baisakhi festival, unaware of the martial law ban on public gatherings. Brigadier General Reginald Dyer marched in with 50 Gurkha and Baluchi soldiers, blocked the only exit, and ordered his men to fire into the crowd without warning. They fired for 10 minutes, emptying 1,650 rounds. The official British count was 379 dead; Indian estimates put the toll at over 1,000.

Dyer later stated that his intention was to "produce a moral effect" throughout Punjab. The massacre instead turned millions of Indians permanently against British rule and radicalised a generation of nationalists.

🏛️ Government

Government Response & Suspension

British Repression

The British responded to the protests with force. Martial law was imposed in Punjab; censorship was tightened across India; newspapers were shut down; leaders were arrested. The government refused to repeal the Rowlatt Act, insisting it was needed for security.

Gandhi's Suspension

On 18 April, Gandhi suspended the satyagraha. He acknowledged that the violence in Punjab showed Indians were not yet ready for mass civil disobedience. He undertook a three-day fast as penance for what he called "a Himalayan miscalculation."

The suspension was controversial. Many saw it as a retreat. But it established a key Gandhian principle: a satyagraha must be called off if the people cannot maintain non-violence.

🌟 Legacy

Historical Significance

First All-India Movement

The Rowlatt Satyagraha was the first truly nationwide political movement in India. It transcended regions, religions, and languages, uniting Indians across the country for the first time in a common cause.

Gandhi's National Arrival

The movement transformed Gandhi from a regional leader into India's undisputed national leader. After Champaran and Kheda, the Rowlatt Satyagraha proved he could mobilise the entire country.

Hindu-Muslim Unity

The movement saw unprecedented Hindu-Muslim unity. Muslims and Hindus fasted, protested, and faced arrest together. This unity would peak during the Khilafat-Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–22.

From Petitions to Protest

The movement marked the definitive break with the "petition politics" of the moderate Congress. Indians now took to the streets. Mass politics had arrived.

Prelude to Non-Cooperation

The Rowlatt Satyagraha was the direct prelude to the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920. Jallianwala Bagh and the Khilafat issue pushed Gandhi to launch the larger movement that would shake British rule to its foundations.

Radicalised a Generation

The repression of 1919 — Jallianwala Bagh, martial law, the crawling order — radicalised a generation. Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad, and other young revolutionaries cited 1919 as their political awakening.

📚 Sources

Historical Sources

Report of the Disorders Committee (Hunter Committee, 1920)

Official British inquiry into the Punjab disturbances of 1919, including the Jallianwala Bagh massacre. Contains testimony from witnesses, officials, and military officers.

Gandhi, M.K. (1925) — The Story of My Experiments with Truth

Gandhi's autobiography includes a detailed first-person account of the Rowlatt Satyagraha, his arrest at Palwal, and the decision to suspend the movement.

Draper, A. (1985) — The Amritsar Massacre

Comprehensive modern study of the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the Punjab disturbances, drawing on previously classified British records.

Chandra, B. et al. (1988) — India's Struggle for Independence

Seminar Marxist historiography placing the Rowlatt Satyagraha in the broader context of India's anti-colonial movement.
