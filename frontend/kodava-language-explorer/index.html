Writing & script

A spoken language, written many ways

Kodava had no dedicated script for most of its history — genealogies and rituals were once recorded on palm-leaf manuscripts called Pattole, while the language itself was mainly oral. Several scripts have been proposed since 1867; explore them below.

Also called the Muthanna script
𑙨𑙩𑙪 → ಕೊಡವ ತಕ್ಕ್

Developed in 1970 by Dr. I. M. Muthanna — a Kodagu-born writer, scholar and translator — this abugida script was adopted by the Karnataka Kodava Sahitya Academy in 2022 as the official Kodava Lipi ("Kodava script"). It is deliberately simpler than other Brahmic scripts, built to represent Kodava's distinctive vowel sounds that Kannada script cannot capture cleanly.

The most common everyday script
ಕೊಡವ ತಕ್ಕ್

Since Captain R. A. Cole's 1867 book An Elementary Grammar of the Coorg Language, Kodava has most often been written using the Kannada alphabet, sometimes with small modifications to represent extra Kodava vowel sounds. Because Kannada is the state language of Karnataka, this remains the script most Kodava speakers actually read and write in today.

In 2005, German linguist Gregg M. Cox designed the Coorgi-Cox alphabet at the community's request: a script built from straight lines for the five core vowels and circular forms for diphthongs. It exists alongside Kodava Lipi, Kannada, Malayalam and Tamil-based spellings — a reminder that a spoken-first language can carry more than one written identity at once.

👋 Say hello

The common Kodava greeting

Kodava is an overwhelmingly oral, everyday-speech language, and its greeting is shared with the wider Kannada-speaking region of which Kodagu is part — spoken with the same warmth, but carrying local custom in how it's performed.

Hello / greetings
Namaskāra
nə-məs-kaː-rə · ನಮಸ್ಕಾರ
Said with the palms pressed together at chest height (añjali mudrā) and a slight bow of the head — used for hello and goodbye alike, to elders, guests and strangers.
🙏 In Kodava homes, greeting often extends further: younger family members bow to touch the feet of elders for blessings, especially during festivals such as Puthari and Kailpodh.

Everyday exchanges

Ninga ennane ullira?

"How are you?" — a common formal check-in among Kodava speakers.

Undith aacha?

Literally "have you eaten?" — asked as a mark of hospitality, much like "how are you" in English.

🌳 Language family

Where Kodava sits in the Dravidian family

Once dismissed as a dialect of Kannada, Kodava was recognised as an independent language by philologists in the early 20th century. It belongs to the same close-knit branch as Tamil, Kannada, Tulu and Toda.

📍 The homeland

Kodagu — the land the language belongs to

Kodagu district (formerly Coorg State, merged into Mysore State in 1956) sits on the eastern slopes of the Western Ghats in southern Karnataka. Its cool, misty hills, coffee and cardamom estates, and dense forest have sheltered the Kodava-speaking communities for centuries — historians consider the Kodavas among the earliest settled agriculturists of the region.

The Kaveri river, revered across South India, rises here at Talakaveri — a site of deep spiritual significance to the Kodava people.

🏵️ Cultural heritage

Beyond the language

Kodava identity is inseparable from custom — ancestral homes, martial tradition, and festivals tied to the agricultural calendar.

Attire

Kupya & Chele

The knee-length black or white coat (kupya), secured with a gold-embroidered maroon sash (chele) and a silver dagger (peeche kathi), remains the ceremonial dress at weddings and festivals.

Faith & ancestry

Ain Mane & Kaveri reverence

Every Kodava traces descent to an ain mane (ancestral house) and clan (okka). The Kaveri river's source at Talakaveri is honoured every year at Kaveri Sankramana.

Festivals

Puthari & Kailpodh

Puthari celebrates the rice harvest; Kailpodh honours ancestral weapons — Kodavas are traditionally among the few Indian communities permitted firearms without a license.
