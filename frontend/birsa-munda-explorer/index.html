🗡️ Chotanagpur · Ulgulan 1895–1900

Birsa Munda Explorer

Dharti Aba — the Father of the Earth who shook the British Empire.

In the deep Sal forests of Chotanagpur, a young Munda farmer rose to lead one of the most stirring tribal uprisings of nineteenth-century India. Birsa Munda's Ulgulan — the Great Tumult — defied colonial landlords, missionaries and the Raj itself, demanding justice for the land, the forest and the tribe. His defiance in 1899–1900 turned an adivasi folk hero into an enduring symbol of India's freedom struggle.

Biography

The Youth Who Became a Prophet

From a village in the Khunti hills to the walls of Ranchi Central Jail — a life of barely twenty-five years that rewrote the fate of a region.

Birsa Munda was born on 15 November 1875 at Ulihatu, in the Khunti region of the Chotanagpur plateau, into a poor Munda farming family. The Mundas — khuntkattidars, the original clearers of the forest — held their land by customary right, yet by Birsa's childhood that right was collapsing under the weight of colonial revenue rules, immigrant landlords (thikadars and jagirdars), and moneylending dikus who drained tribal households dry.

Raised amid this loss of land, forest and dignity, Birsa studied briefly at the German Mission School in Chaibasa, where he learned to read and write and was drawn into Christianity — but his schooling ended early, and with it his faith in the missionaries. In 1894–95, claiming to have seen a vision of God, he began preaching a message of purification: give up superstition and witchcraft, shun intoxicants, return to the worship of the sarna sacred grove. Thousands of Mundas, Oraons and Kharias flocked to hear the "new word".

Milestones

Chronology of a Rebellion

From a shepherd boy in the Sal forest to the leadership of the Great Tumult.

1875 🌱

Born at Ulihatu

Birsa Munda is born into a poor Munda family at Ulihatu village, Khunti, on the Chotanagpur plateau — a landscape of Sal forests and ancestral khuntkatti lands under siege.

1886 📖

Schooling at Chaibasa

Birsa is admitted to the German Mission School at Chaibasa, where he learns Hindi and English, and is drawn into and then out of Christianity, beginning his search for a Munda faith.

1894 ✨

The Vision

Claiming divine inspiration, Birsa begins preaching reform: abandon witchcraft and superstition, stop intoxicants, and worship in the sacred sarna grove. The movement that will be called Birsait takes shape.

1895 ⛓️

First Arrest

Alarmed by his growing following, the British arrest Birsa on 24 August 1895 and sentence him to two years in jail. The "prophet of Chotanagpur" returns from prison more of a firebrand than before.

1897 🌾

Return & Agitation

Released on 30 November 1897, Birsa resumes the campaign — now openly political. He demands the restoration of tribal land rights and an end to forced labour (beth begari) and exploitative levies.

24 Dec 1899 🔥

The Ulgulan Begins

Around Christmas 1899 some seven thousand men and women assemble to herald the Ulgulan — the Great Tumult. Followers attack churches and loyalist strongholds across Ranchi and Singhbhum.

Jan 1900 ⚔️

Attacks on the Raj

Rebels kill constables at Etkedih on 5 January and storm the Khunti police station on 7 January. Troops under the Ranchi commissioner and deputy commissioner crush the rising at Dumbari Hill — but Birsa escapes to the Singhbhum hills.

3 Feb 1900 🪢

Captured at Jamkopai

Betrayed by informers in the Jamkopai forest, Chakradharpur, Birsa is arrested and marched to Ranchi, where hundreds of followers face trial. A reward of Rs 500 had been placed on his head.

9 Jun 1900 🕊️

Death in Ranchi Jail

While trial proceedings drag on, the twenty-four-year-old Birsa dies in Ranchi Central Jail, officially of cholera. The empire's guns could not break the Ulgulan's spirit — a century later it would still be rising.

The Ulgulan

The Great Tumult

Birsa Munda's tribal resistance — a fight for the forest, the land, and the dignity of the adivasi.

Why They Rose

Land, Forest and Forced Labour

The Mundas were the khuntkattidars — the original clearers of the forest — who held their villages by ancient customary right. Over the nineteenth century that right was steadily eroded: the British imposed new revenue systems, immigrant dikus and moneylenders swallowed village land, and peasants were compelled into beth begari — unpaid forced labour. The colonial forest department also began closing off the very woods on which the tribes depended.

Decades of petitions and the earlier Sardari Larai had won little. Birsa gave the Chotanagpur adivasis what the petition books could not: a unifying leader, a religious revival, and a vision of a world without the queen, the diku, and the thikadar.

⚔️ Movement at a Glance

  • Ulgulan — "Great Tumult", a total revolt against colonial rule.
  • Enemies named — British officials, thikadars, jagirdars, moneylenders, missionaries.
  • Weapons of the forest — bows, arrows, axes and guerrilla tactics.
  • Goal — restore khuntkatti land, end forced labour, establish Munda rule.
  • Aftermath — the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act, 1908.
🏞️

Agrarian Revolt

At heart, the Ulgulan was a peasants' war for the land. Birsa demanded the restoration of ancestral khuntkatti rights and the abolition of forced labour and extortionate rents.

🌳

Religious Revival

Birsa fused resistance with faith, purging Munda religion of foreign influence and calling tribes back to the sarna sacred grove — a spiritual foundation for political unity.

🏹

Guerrilla Resistance

From the Sal forests, Birsa's warriors struck police stations and loyalist strongholds with bows, arrows and axes — inflicting real blows on the machinery of colonial power.

📜

A Decade of Defiance

Born from a century of Chotanagpur uprisings — from the Tamar rising of 1789 to the Kol Revolt of 1831–32 — the Ulgulan was the greatest of them all, and the last great armed rising of the region.

Legacy

The Dharti Aba Who Built a State

Birsa Munda died in a colonial jail, but his memory marched on — until the map of India itself was redrawn.

🌍

Father of the Earth

To the Mundas, Birsa is not merely a hero but a deity — Dharti Aba, "Father of the Earth", honoured in folk songs sung across Chotanagpur to this day.

🏛️

Jharkhand is Born

On 15 November 2000 — Birsa's 125th birth anniversary — Jharkhand was carved out of Bihar, fulfilling the tribal dream of a homeland that Birsa had fought for.

⚖️

Land Rights Secured

The Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908) banned the transfer of tribal land to non-tribals — a direct legislative echo of the Ulgulan's core demand.

📬

A Stamp and a Portrait

India Post issued a commemorative stamp in 1988, and Birsa's portrait hangs in India's Parliament — a tribesman, remembered in the halls of power.

🗓️

Tribal Pride Day

Since 2021, his birth anniversary is celebrated nationwide as Janjatiya Gaurav Divas — Tribal Pride Day — honouring the adivasi contribution to the nation.

✈️

A Region Named for Him

Ranchi's airport, universities, hospitals and the Khunti district bear his name — from Birsa Munda Airport to the Birsa Munda University, his identity is woven into Jharkhand's present.

🪶

The Spirit of the Ulgulan

"Friends, go back to your villages and pledge to fight against the British. Keep up the flame of revolution." — Birsa Munda, as he was led into Ranchi Jail, 1900.

References & Further Reading

Sources Behind the Story

The classic studies of the Munda rebellion and the colonial record, together with the modern works that frame Birsa in the wider story of India's freedom struggle.

📖

Singh, Kumar Suresh (1966)

The Dust-Storm and the Hanging Mist: A Study of Birsa Munda and His Movement in Chhotanagpur (1874–1901). Calcutta: Firma K. L. Mukhopadhyay. The standard scholarly account of Birsa and the Ulgulan.

📖

Hallett, M. G. (1917)

Bihar and Orissa District Gazetteers: Ranchi. Patna: Superintendent of Government Printing. A colonial gazetteer documenting the suppression of the rebellion.

📖

Hoffmann, J. & Van Emelen, A. (1930)

Encyclopaedia Mundarica. Patna: Government Printing. The monumental Jesuit lexicon of Munda life, a key contemporary source on Birsa's movement.

📖

Zide, Norman & Munda, Ram Dayal (1969)

A Bibliographical Introduction to Modern Indian Literature — and the collected Munda folk songs of the Ulgulan era, which keep Birsa's memory alive in the oral tradition.

📖

Guha, Ranajit (1999)

Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India. Durham: Duke University Press. Situates tribal uprisings like the Ulgulan within the history of peasant resistance.

📖

Wikipedia

Birsa Munda and Munda Rebellion. General reference for chronology, names and the colonial record of the rising.
