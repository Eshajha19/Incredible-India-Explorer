⚔️ Bihar · 22 October 1764

Battle of Buxar Explorer

On the banks of the Ganges at Buxar, a small, disciplined army of the East India Company routed the combined forces of the Nawab of Bengal, the Nawab of Awadh, and the Mughal Emperor himself. The victory sealed Company rule in Bengal and opened the road to British supremacy over the subcontinent.

After Plassey, a New Storm

A Battle That Changed the Fate of India

Seven years after Robert Clive's triumph at Plassey, the East India Company faced a far sterner test. Mir Qasim, the new Nawab of Bengal, resented the Company's demand for free trade and its officials' misuse of dastaks (trade passes). When the Company refused to end its privileged commerce, Mir Qasim clashed with his own council and marched against Calcutta.

After defeats at Katwa, Murshidabad, and Patna, Mir Qasim fled to Awadh, where he forged an unprecedented alliance with Nawab Shuja-ud-Daulah and the fugitive Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. In October 1764, their combined host confronted Major Hector Munro's Company army near Buxar — and this time, there was no treachery or bribery to decide the day.

🏛️ At a Glance

  • 22 October 1764 — Fought on the banks of the Ganges at Buxar, Bihar.
  • Company Victory — A genuine field battle won by discipline, not conspiracy.
  • Triple Alliance — Bengal, Awadh, and the Mughal Empire united against the Company.
  • Treaty of Allahabad (1765) — Diwani rights over Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.
Forces in the Field

The Belligerents

Two very different armies met at Buxar: a compact, professional Company force against a vast but loosely coordinated coalition.

🛡️

British East India Company

  • Commander — Major Hector Munro
  • Strength — ~7,072 men (857 Europeans, 5,297 sepoys, 918 Indian cavalry) with 9 guns
  • Tactics — Disciplined volley fire, concentrated artillery, and light field guns
🛡️

Combined Indian Alliance

  • Leaders — Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah, Shah Alam II
  • Strength — ~40,000+ men, including heavy cavalry and numerous guns
  • Tactics — Large-scale cavalry charges and artillery, but poor coordination

Why it mattered: Unlike Plassey (1757), Buxar was won on the battlefield itself. It proved the Company's disciplined sepoys and gunpowder tactics could defeat the largest native armies of North India without the aid of betrayal.

⚔️ The Verdict of Buxar

  • Decisive British Victory — Munro's compact force broke the allied line and routed the coalition.
  • Casualties — Company: ~847 killed and wounded; Alliance: roughly 2,000–6,000 dead.
  • Alliance Collapse — Mir Qasim fled and died an exile; Shuja-ud-Daulah and Shah Alam II sued for peace.
  • Paramount Power — The Company became the strongest military force in northern India.
Company Supremacy Secured

Why Buxar Was the Turning Point

Plassey had given the Company the treasury of Bengal through a conspiracy; Buxar gave it legitimacy through arms. The defeat of the Mughal emperor and two great nawabs shattered the old political order of North India and left no indigenous power capable of challenging the Company's growing might.

The Mughal court, in one stroke, lost its last leverage. The Company, in one afternoon, acquired a status it had never formally held — the arbiter of India's destiny.

The Pen After the Sword

The Treaty of Allahabad (12 August 1765)

Robert Clive followed the victory at Buxar with two treaties signed at Allahabad that reshaped the subcontinent.

1765

With Shah Alam II (Mughal Emperor)

The Company received the Diwani — the right to collect land revenue — of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, in exchange for an annual tribute to the emperor and the return of the Allahabad and Kora districts.

1765

With Shuja-ud-Daulah (Nawab of Awadh)

Awadh was returned to the nawab after he paid a large indemnity and entered a defensive alliance — making Awadh a buffer state and a future source of Company revenue and troops.

1772

Diwani in Practice

When Warren Hastings became Governor-General, the Company directly administered Bengal's revenue. The wealth of Bengal funded the conquests of Mysore, the Marathas, and the Sikhs — the engine of British expansion.

📜 Lasting Implications

  • Company as Ruler — Revenue rights made the Company a landholder and ruler, not merely a trader.
  • Start of Colonial Era — The Regulating Act (1773) and later reforms formalised British governance.
  • Mughal Twilight — The emperor became a pensioner of the Company, ending Mughal sovereignty in practice.
  • Road to 1857 — The fiscal exploitation that began with the Diwani fed the grievances behind the Revolt of 1857.
Milestones

Chronology of the Battle of Buxar

From Plassey to the Treaty of Allahabad — the road to Company supremacy.

23 June 1757

Battle of Plassey

Robert Clive's victory over Siraj-ud-Daulah installs Mir Jafar as Nawab of Bengal and gives the Company its first grip on Bengal's treasury.

1760

Mir Qasim Becomes Nawab

After deposing Mir Jafar, the Company places Mir Qasim on the throne of Bengal — expecting a pliant ally, and receiving instead a defiant ruler.

1763

Conflict Erupts

Mir Qasim resists the Company's free-trade privileges. He is defeated at Katwa, Murshidabad, and Patna, then flees westward toward Awadh.

22 October 1764

The Battle of Buxar

Major Hector Munro's ~7,000 Company troops decisively rout the allied army of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah, and Shah Alam II.

12 August 1765

Treaty of Allahabad

Shah Alam II grants the Company the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa; Shuja-ud-Daulah regains Awadh as a client state.

1772

Diwani Enforced

Warren Hastings takes direct control of revenue collection. Bengal's wealth becomes the engine of British conquest across India.
