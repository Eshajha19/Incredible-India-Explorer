Understanding Wetlands

What Are Wetlands?

Wetlands are ecosystems where water covers the soil or is present at or near the surface for varying periods, creating unique habitats that support extraordinary biodiversity.

Under the Ramsar Convention, wetlands are defined as "areas of marsh, fen, peatland or water, whether natural or artificial, permanent or temporary, with water that is static or flowing, fresh, brackish or salt."

India's wetlands range from the icy marshes of the Himalayas to the sun-drenched mangroves of the Sundarbans, from the floating phumdis of Loktak to the man-made tanks of Tamil Nadu.

These ecosystems are among the most productive on Earth — rivalling rainforests and coral reefs in the diversity of life they support.

  • Cover only 6% of Earth's surface but support 40% of all species
  • Store 3-5x more carbon per hectare than tropical rainforests
  • Provide freshwater, food, and livelihoods to billions
  • Act as natural water purifiers and flood buffers

Global Recognition

Ramsar Convention & India's Commitment

The Ramsar Convention is an international treaty for wetland conservation. India, a signatory since 1982, now has more Ramsar Sites than any other Asian country.

The Convention on Wetlands of International Importance (Ramsar Convention) was adopted in 1971 in the Iranian city of Ramsar. It provides the framework for national action and international cooperation for the conservation and wise use of wetlands.

India's journey from 2 Ramsar Sites in 1981 to 80 sites today reflects a growing recognition of wetland values. Each Ramsar Site is designated based on criteria including representative wetland types, support for vulnerable species, and provision of ecosystem services.

Global Challenge

Climate Change Impact

Wetlands are both victims of and solutions to climate change. Their protection is essential for climate adaptation and mitigation.

How Climate Change Affects Wetlands

Climate change poses existential threats to wetland ecosystems through rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, sea-level rise, and increased frequency of extreme weather events.

  • Sea-level rise threatens coastal wetlands — a 1m rise could inundate 50% of India's mangroves
  • Glacial melt reduces water supply to Himalayan wetlands and downstream riverine systems
  • Changing rainfall disrupts flood-pulse timing essential for wetland breeding cycles
  • Higher temperatures increase evapotranspiration, drying out shallow wetlands
  • Intense cyclones cause saltwater intrusion into freshwater wetlands

Wetlands as Climate Solutions

Despite covering only 6% of land, wetlands store 20-30% of Earth's soil carbon. Peatlands alone store twice as much carbon as all the world's forests combined.

Mangroves along India's coast protect over 40 million people from storm surges and reduce wave height by up to 66%, making them one of the most cost-effective natural climate defenses.

Restoring 15% of degraded wetlands could reduce global flood damage by $24 billion annually.
