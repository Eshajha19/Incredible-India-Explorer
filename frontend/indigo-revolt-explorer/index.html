🌿 Bengal · 1859 – 1860

Indigo Revolt

Neel Bidroho: when Bengal's cultivators refused, for the first time on a mass scale, to sow the crop that had trapped them in debt and coercion for generations — and forced the colonial government to respond.

A Forced Cash Crop

The Indigo Cultivation System

From the late 18th century, European planters established indigo plantations across Bengal to meet growing global demand for the blue dye. Two systems of cultivation operated side by side: "nij" cultivation on land directly controlled by planters, and the far more widespread "ryoti" system, under which peasant cultivators (ryots) were compelled to grow indigo on their own land.

Under the ryoti system, planters advanced cash loans, called "dadon", to cultivators before the sowing season. Accepting a dadon bound a ryot — and often his descendants — to keep growing indigo on a fixed portion of his land, season after season, regardless of whether it was profitable.

🌱 Plantation Structure

  • Nij cultivation: Indigo grown directly on planter-controlled land using hired or forced labour.
  • Ryoti cultivation: Peasants forced to grow indigo on their own holdings under contract.
  • Dadon: Cash advances that bound cultivators into recurring, often multi-generational, obligation.
  • Fixed low prices: Planters set purchase prices well below the cost of cultivation.
Debt, Coercion, Dispossession

Economic Exploitation and Cultivator Conditions

Indigo cultivation was structurally designed to keep peasants dependent and indebted, using both financial and physical coercion.

💰

Below-Cost Prices

Planters paid far less for indigo than it cost cultivators to grow it, while rice or other food crops on the same land would have earned far more.

📜

Coerced Contracts

Agreements were frequently signed under duress, in languages cultivators could not read, and renewed year after year through fresh dadon.

🏚️

Lost Foodgrain Land

Land forced into indigo could not be used to grow rice, pushing families toward food insecurity even as they remained in debt.

🥊

Planter Violence

Planters employed armed retainers, "lathiyals", to enforce cultivation, seize crops and property, and punish resistance.

✊ Forms of Resistance

  • Collective oaths: Villagers swore, often in temples or mosques, not to sow indigo again.
  • Refusing dadon: Cultivators declined new cash advances, breaking the debt cycle at its source.
  • Social boycott: Communities refused labour and services to planters and their agents.
  • Legal petitions: Educated cultivators and sympathisers filed complaints and lawsuits against planters.
  • Armed self-defence: Some villages physically resisted lathiyals sent to enforce cultivation.
Unity Across Communities

The Role of Local Communities

What distinguished the Indigo Revolt was the breadth of its social base. Hindu and Muslim cultivators organised together at the village level, pooling resources to withstand planter reprisals and sustain the boycott across multiple growing seasons.

Local zamindars and moneylenders sometimes supported the cultivators — occasionally out of genuine sympathy, and occasionally because indigo planters competed with their own claims on peasant produce — lending the revolt useful, if uneven, local backing.

The Power of the Press

Journalists, Writers and Intellectuals

Bengal's emerging press and educated middle class, the bhadralok, turned local unrest into a matter of wider public conscience.

Editor

Harish Chandra Mukherjee

Editor of "The Hindoo Patriot", he published detailed, sustained reporting on planter atrocities, shaping Calcutta's educated public opinion against the indigo system.

Playwright

Dinabandhu Mitra

His 1860 Bengali play "Nil Darpan" (The Indigo Mirror) dramatised cultivator suffering, becoming one of the revolt's most powerful pieces of protest literature.

Translator

Reverend James Long

An English missionary who translated "Nil Darpan" into English, he was prosecuted and fined for libel over the translation — turning his trial into a further public controversy.

Peasant Leaders

Digambar & Bishnu Biswas

Brothers from Nadia district credited with leading the earliest organised refusal to cultivate indigo in 1859, inspiring resistance across neighbouring villages.

Official Inquiry

British Response and the Indigo Commission

As unrest spread and press coverage intensified, Governor-General Lord Canning's government could no longer treat the disturbances as purely local. In March 1860, it appointed the Indigo Commission to investigate conditions in the indigo districts, taking testimony directly from cultivators, planters and local officials.

The Commission's report substantiated many of the cultivators' complaints, acknowledging that coercion was widespread. Its findings fed directly into Act XI of 1860, which confirmed that no cultivator could lawfully be compelled to grow indigo against their will.

🏛️ Indigo Commission, 1860

  • Appointed by: Governor-General Lord Canning, March 1860.
  • Method: Direct testimony collected from cultivators and planters across Bengal.
  • Finding: Confirmed widespread coercion in the ryoti indigo system.
  • Result: Act XI of 1860 barred forced indigo cultivation.
What Changed

Immediate Outcome and Long-Term Impact

📉

Collapse of Forced Cultivation

Freed from compulsion, cultivators shifted land back to rice, and indigo production in Bengal fell sharply within a few years.

🏭

Planters Relocate

Many indigo planters shut Bengal factories or shifted operations to Bihar, where forced cultivation continued for decades longer.

✊

A Template for Peasant Action

The revolt demonstrated that organised, non-violent refusal could force concessions from a powerful colonial-commercial nexus.

🌾

A Line to Champaran

The pattern of forced indigo cultivation and cultivator resistance recurred in Bihar, culminating in Gandhi's 1917 Champaran Satyagraha.
