Subterranean Architecture

Architecture of Ancient Stepwells

Known as Baolis, Baoris, Vavs, and Vapis, stepwells are marvels of ancient Indian engineering. Scroll down to descend into the depths and uncover their structural brilliance.

Descend

The Geometry of Descent

Unlike traditional wells, stepwells were designed as inverted temples. Their construction required precise mathematical planning to ensure structural stability against the massive pressure of the surrounding earth.

The stepped corridors (often in a cross or L-shape) create a mesmerizing visual symmetry, guiding light down to the deepest levels while providing structural reinforcement.

Pavilions and Structural Support

As you descend, you encounter multi-story pavilions. These pillared galleries served a dual purpose: they buttressed the retaining walls and provided cool resting places for travelers.

Because the temperature at the bottom of a stepwell can be up to 10 degrees cooler than the surface, these pavilions acted as subterranean oases during the scorching Indian summers.

More Than Just Water

Stepwells were the centers of community life. They were places for socializing, festivals, and religious ceremonies.

Many stepwells feature intricate carvings of deities, effectively serving as subterranean temples. For example, Rani ki Vav in Gujarat features over 500 principal sculptures celebrating the sanctity of water.

The Aquifer

At the very bottom lies the deep well shaft that taps directly into the unconfined groundwater aquifer. The water level fluctuates with the seasons, rising during the monsoons to submerge the lower steps and falling during the dry months.

This dynamic architecture allowed people to access water year-round, representing a profound understanding of hydrology and sustainable water conservation.
