🗡️ Palayakkarar Chieftain of Panchalankurichi · Early Anti-Colonial Resister

The Chieftain Who Refused to Pay Tribute to the British

📜 Biography

Veerapandiya Kattabomman (3 January 1760 – 16 October 1799) was a Palayakkarar (poligar, or local chieftain) of Panchalankurichi in the Tirunelveli region of present-day Tamil Nadu. He belonged to a line of poligars who governed the area as semi-autonomous military-administrative chiefs under the wider authority of the Nawab of Arcot.

Following the British East India Company's expanding control over South Indian revenue collection through arrangements with the Nawab of Arcot, Kattabomman refused to recognize the Company's demand for tribute and taxes from his territory, insisting that the Company had no legitimate authority over Panchalankurichi.

His confrontation with the Company escalated after a tense meeting with the British collector of Ramnad, Jackson, in 1799, during which Kattabomman's guards are said to have clashed with Company officials — an episode that hardened both sides and set the stage for open conflict.

The Company mounted a military campaign against Panchalankurichi. Kattabomman's fort was besieged and eventually destroyed after fierce fighting. He evaded capture for a period by fleeing into the forests, reportedly aided by neighbouring chieftains, before being handed over to the British by the Raja of Pudukkottai.

Kattabomman was tried and sentenced to death. He was hanged on 16 October 1799 at Kayathar, near Tirunelveli — a site now marked by a memorial. His younger brother, Oomaithurai, and other poligars of the region continued resistance against the Company in what came to be called the Second Poligar War (1800–1801).

⚔️ Military Resistance

Refusing the Company's Tribute

At the heart of the conflict was Kattabomman's refusal to accept that the East India Company — acting through its arrangements with the Nawab of Arcot — had any legitimate right to tax Panchalankurichi. He treated Company tax demands as an infringement on the traditional autonomy of the Palayakkarar chieftaincies of the Tirunelveli region.

Kattabomman's confrontation with Collector Jackson at Ramnad in 1799 is remembered as the flashpoint that turned a tax dispute into open armed conflict.
— Popular historical accounts of the 1799 meeting

The Fall of Panchalankurichi

When negotiations broke down, the Company assembled a military force to subdue Panchalankurichi. Kattabomman's forces, though vastly outmatched in weaponry and numbers, mounted a determined defence of the fort using guerrilla tactics suited to the region's terrain before the stronghold was ultimately stormed and razed.

Flight, Betrayal, and Capture

Following the fort's fall, Kattabomman fled into the surrounding forests, reportedly sheltered for a time by sympathetic local chieftains. He was eventually captured after being handed over to British forces by the Raja of Pudukkottai — an act of betrayal that features prominently in regional folk memory of the episode.

The Second Poligar War

Kattabomman's execution did not end the resistance. His brother Oomaithurai, together with other poligars of the Tirunelveli region, continued the armed struggle against the Company in the Second Poligar War (1800–1801), extending the legacy of resistance he had begun.

🏛️ Historical Significance

Why Kattabomman's resistance matters in the wider story of India's freedom struggle.

An Early Act of Anti-Colonial Defiance

Kattabomman's stand against the East India Company came nearly six decades before the widely recognised 1857 uprising, making him one of the earliest South Indian chieftains to resist colonial taxation and authority through armed defiance rather than negotiation.

Symbol of Regional Sovereignty

His resistance is remembered in Tamil Nadu as an assertion of the Palayakkarar system's traditional autonomy against an expanding colonial revenue apparatus — a theme that resonates through the broader history of the Poligar Wars fought across the region in this period.

Enduring Cultural Memory

Kattabomman's story has been kept alive through Tamil folk ballads, oral tradition, and — most famously — the 1959 Tamil film Veerapandiya Kattabomman starring Sivaji Ganesan, which cemented his place as a celebrated folk hero and martyr in South Indian popular memory.

Commemoration

A memorial at Kayathar marks the site of his execution, and Panchalankurichi remains a site of pilgrimage and remembrance, with statues and institutions across Tamil Nadu bearing his name.

🗡️ Remembering the Chieftain of Panchalankurichi

Long before the wider subcontinent rose against colonial rule, Veerapandiya Kattabomman stood his ground against a tax he refused to legitimise. His execution at Kayathar did not silence the resistance — it passed the flame to his brother and to generations of Tamil memory that followed.
