🐸 Western Ghats Endemic Amphibian

Malabar Tree Toad Explorer

Meet Pedostibes tuberculosus, a small, secretive toad that spends most of its life in the tree canopy rather than on the forest floor — a rare trait among toads, and one that makes it one of the Western Ghats' most distinctive amphibians.

🌿 Introduction

The Malabar Tree Toad (Pedostibes tuberculosus), also called the Warty Asian Tree Toad, is a small amphibian found only in the Western Ghats mountain range along India's western coast. Unlike most toads, which are ground-dwellers, the Malabar Tree Toad is largely arboreal — spending much of its life on trees and descending mainly during the monsoon breeding season.

First described by British zoologist Albert Günther in 1876 from a specimen collected in the historic Malabar region, this toad remained one of the least-recorded amphibians of peninsular India for over a century, owing to its cryptic, canopy-dwelling habits.

Today, citizen-science projects such as Frogwatch and Mapping the Malabar Tree Toad are helping researchers document its distribution and ecology, making it a fascinating symbol of grassroots amphibian conservation in India.

🔬 Scientific Name

The species' binomial name is Pedostibes tuberculosus Günther, 1876. It is the only species in the monotypic genus Pedostibes, which is why it is sometimes referred to collectively as an "Asian tree toad."

  • Binomial name: Pedostibes tuberculosus Günther, 1876
  • Common names: Malabar Tree Toad, Warty Asian Tree Toad, Western Ghats Tree Toad
  • Synonym: Nectophryne tuberculosa (Boulenger, 1882)
  • Type locality: "Malabar", South India

Note: The genus Pedostibes once included several Southeast Asian tree toad species, but these were reclassified into a new genus, Rentapia, in 2016 — leaving P. tuberculosus as the sole remaining member of Pedostibes.

🗺️ Distribution

The Malabar Tree Toad is endemic to the Western Ghats, a UNESCO World Heritage biodiversity hotspot running along India's western coast. Confirmed sightings span a fragmented range across Karnataka, Kerala, Goa, and Maharashtra.

  • Sharavathi, Aghanashini, Bedti, and Kali river basins, Karnataka
  • Neyyar Wildlife Sanctuary and Silent Valley National Park, Kerala
  • Parambikulam Tiger Reserve and Indira Gandhi National Park (Kerala–Tamil Nadu border region)
  • Cotigao and Chandoli Wildlife Sanctuaries, Goa and Maharashtra

Its known range covers only a small fraction of the Western Ghats, and its patchy, scattered occurrence makes it one of the rarer amphibians documented in the region.

🌳 Habitat

This species favours evergreen to moist-deciduous forests at elevations roughly between 50 and 1,800 metres above sea level. It is typically found near perennial streams, where humidity stays high year-round.

True to its name, the toad spends much of its life 1–10 metres above the ground, sheltering inside tree hollows, leaf axils, and water-filled cavities — a lifestyle known as being phytotelmatic (living in plant-held water bodies). It descends closer to streams mainly to breed during the monsoon.

👀 Behaviour

The Malabar Tree Toad is largely nocturnal and arboreal, making it difficult to spot despite its wide elevational range. Its skin is warty and cryptically coloured, allowing it to blend in with tree bark and leaf litter.

During the monsoon, males call from perches on trees near streams to attract mates — one of the few times the species becomes more conspicuous. Outside the breeding season, individuals are rarely seen, and much of the species' day-to-day behaviour is still poorly documented.

🍽️ Diet

Like most bufonids, the Malabar Tree Toad is insectivorous. It feeds opportunistically on small invertebrates such as ants, beetles, and other insects it encounters while foraging on tree trunks and branches at night.

By controlling insect populations in its forest habitat, it plays a small but meaningful role in maintaining the balance of the canopy ecosystem it inhabits.

🥚 Reproduction

Breeding is closely tied to the monsoon season, when the toads descend from the canopy toward streams. Females are known to lay large clutches — over a thousand small, translucent eggs per clutch — suspended just below the water's surface.

The tadpoles develop in streams and phytotelmata (water-filled tree cavities and reed culms) before metamorphosing into terrestrial, tree-climbing juveniles. Much of the species' larval biology was only formally documented in the last two decades, reflecting how elusive this toad remains.

🌍 Ecological Importance

As an amphibian restricted to undisturbed evergreen forest with clean streams, the Malabar Tree Toad acts as an indicator species — its presence signals a healthy, well-forested watershed, while its absence can flag habitat degradation.

It also contributes to natural pest control through its insect diet and forms part of the food web for forest predators such as snakes and birds, linking canopy and stream ecosystems within the Western Ghats.

🛡️ Conservation Status

The species was first assessed by the IUCN in 2004 as "Endangered" due to its small, fragmented range of under 5,200 sq. km. Following further field surveys and citizen-science records expanding its known range, the 2023 IUCN Red List reassessment moved it to "Least Concern."

Even so, the IUCN notes that its overall population trend remains declining, and it is protected under Schedule IV of India's Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972. Its long-term survival still depends on the health of Western Ghats forests.

⚠️ Threats

  • Habitat loss: Deforestation and conversion of evergreen forest for plantations and agriculture
  • Stream alteration: Dams and water diversion changing the flow needed for breeding
  • Fragmentation: Roads and human settlements isolating populations from one another
  • Climate change: Research modelling suggests shifting rainfall and temperature patterns may shrink suitable habitat in the coming decades
  • Road mortality: Vehicle strikes during monsoon migration toward breeding streams

✨ Interesting Facts

🌳 A Toad That Prefers Trees

Unlike almost all other toads, which stay on the ground, the Malabar Tree Toad spends most of its life climbing trees — an unusual adaptation within the toad family.

🧬 One of a Kind

It is the only living species in its genus, Pedostibes, after related Southeast Asian species were moved into a separate genus, Rentapia, in 2016.

🔍 Rediscovered Through Persistence

For decades after its 1876 description, the species was rarely recorded — it took dedicated field surveys in the 2000s to confirm it was more widespread than once believed.

🤝 A Citizen-Science Success Story

The "Mapping the Malabar Tree Toad" initiative invites the public to submit sightings, helping researchers track this hard-to-find species across the Western Ghats.

💧 A Life Tied to Water in the Trees

Its tadpoles have been recorded developing inside water-filled reed stems, showing how closely this species' life cycle is tied to tiny, tree-borne pools of water.
