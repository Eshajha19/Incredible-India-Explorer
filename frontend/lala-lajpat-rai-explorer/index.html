📜 Biography

Lala Lajpat Rai (28 January 1865 – 17 November 1928) was an Indian nationalist revolutionary, writer, educationist, and prominent member of the Indian National Congress. Popularly hailed as "Punjab Kesari" (The Lion of Punjab), he was one of the three members of the Lal-Bal-Pal triage whose assertive brand of extremist nationalism shaped the early phase of the independence movement.

Born at Dhudike in the Faridkot district of Punjab (British India), into an Agrawal Jain family, Lajpat Rai was the eldest son of Munshi Radha Krishna, a Urdu and Persian teacher, and Gulab Devi. After completing his education at the Government College in Lahore, he qualified as a lawyer and began legal practice in Hisar and later Lahore.

An early follower of Swami Dayanand Saraswati, he joined the Arya Samaj in 1882 and became a leading voice of its progressive wing. He established the Dayanand Anglo-Vedic School in Lahore (1885) to blend traditional Indian learning with modern education, and later founded the Punjab National Bank (1895), the Laxmi Insurance Company, and the National College in Lahore.

He served as the President of the Indian National Congress at its Special Session in Kolkata (1920), where Mahatma Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement — a testament to his standing among his peers. During the First World War he toured abroad (Japan, England, the United States) and founded the Indian Home Rule League of America in New York City (1917), mobilising the overseas Indian diaspora.

Lajpat Rai's journalism — most notably the Punjabi-language weekly Punjabee (1905) — carried nationalist ideas to the masses. His fearless oratory and organizing made him the face of militant nationalism in North India, earning the British label of "the most dangerous of the extremist trio."

He breathed his last on 17 November 1928, succumbing to the grievous head injuries inflicted by a British lathi charge during the protest against the Simon Commission in Lahore. His martyrdom became a turning point, fusing the Gandhian path of non-violence with the revolutionary fire that would ignite the 1928–30 struggle.

🛡️ Simon Commission Protest & Martyrdom

A Protest That Lit the Nation

In 1928, the British government appointed the Simon Commission to review the implementation of the Government of India Act, 1919. In an insult to Indian political sentiment, the commission was constituted entirely of British members — not a single Indian was included. Nationwide boycotts and protests erupted under the rallying cry "Simon Go Back!"

On 30 October 1928, the Commission arrived in Lahore. Lala Lajpat Rai, though already in poor health, led a peaceful procession of workers, students, and elders to the viceregal lodge where the commission was meeting. Bearing black flags and shouting the defiant slogan, the protesters presented a stark symbol of national rejection.

"The blows struck at me today will be the last nail in the coffin of British rule in India."
— Lala Lajpat Rai, addressing the crowd at Mochi Gate, 30 Oct 1928

The Lathi Charge

The police superintendent of Lahore, James A. Scott, ordered a forceful lathi charge into the unarmed crowd. Rai was struck repeatedly on the head, fracturing his skull. Even as blood streamed down his face, he addressed the gathered crowd that evening at Mochi Gate and delivered the immortal words above.

Rai was admitted to the Lady Willingdon Hospital but never fully recovered. He died on 17 November 1928 at the age of 63. Contemporary doctors opined that the force of Scott's blows had accelerated his death through a cerebral haemorrhage. When the matter was raised in the British Parliament, the government denied any culpability.

A Flame Passed to Revolutionaries

The assassination of Rai's assailant — J. P. Saunders, an assistant superintendent of police — by Bhagat Singh, Shivaram Rajguru, and Sukhdev Thapar of the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA) on 17 December 1928, transformed Rai's martyrdom into the spark that ignited a new generation of revolutionary struggle.

🦁 Legacy & Impact

The enduring imprint of the Punjab Kesari across institutions, education, and the nationalist ethos.

Institutional Legacy

Punjab National Bank, which he co-founded in 1895, has grown into one of India's largest public sector banks. His vision of indigenous enterprise also lives on through the Laxmi Insurance Company and the National College in Lahore.

Education & Social Reform

He established over a dozen Dayanand Anglo-Vedic Schools, seeded the Lal-Bal-Pal National College, and championed widow remarriage and women's education through Arya Samaj. His belief that "education is the weapon of the future" continues to guide institutions named in his honour.

Political Inspiration

His martyrdom fused the Gandhian strategy of satyagraha with revolutionary urgency — directly inspiring Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad and the HSRA. To this day, the slogan "Dagar to door tak khol do, Lala Lajpat Rai!" echoes at nationalist gatherings.

Naming Honours

Lala Lajpat Rai University (Moga), Lala Lajpat Rai Institute of Engineering & Technology, numerous schools, hospitals, roads, and the Punjab Kesari War Memorial at his birthplace Dhudike bear testament to his enduring fame.

🦁 Remembering the Lion of Punjab

From the streets of Lahore to the corridors of nationalism, Lala Lajpat Rai lived for the nation and died for it. At the stroke of 11:30 a.m., whenever a lamp is lit in his honour, remember: every lathi blow that sought to silence him only amplified the drums of independence.
