Introduction

The Hump-backed Mahseer is a giant freshwater fish endemic to the Cauvery (Kaveri) river basin in South India. Renowned among anglers for generations as one of the world's great freshwater game fish, it is also, today, one of India's most urgent freshwater conservation priorities.

Long shrouded in taxonomic confusion, the species was only formally and conclusively identified in 2018, just as scientists confirmed it to be sliding rapidly toward extinction in the wild.

Scientific Name

Tor remadevii Kurup & Radhakrishnan, 2007

Also known as the orange-finned mahseer, the species was first formally described in 2007 from juvenile specimens collected in the Pambar river, a Cauvery tributary. It was only in 2018 that researchers confirmed these juveniles were the same species as the legendary giant "hump-backed mahseer" of the main Cauvery river, resolving decades of taxonomic confusion with other mahseer species.

Distribution Map

The Hump-backed Mahseer is endemic to the Cauvery river basin, which flows through the states of Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu in the Western Ghats region of South India. It is not found naturally in any other river system in the world.

🗺️ Distribution Map: Cauvery River Basin, Karnataka & Tamil Nadu

Today, self-sustaining populations are known from only a couple of isolated stretches of the river system, a dramatic contraction from its once much wider range across the Cauvery.

Habitat

  • Prefers fast-flowing, clean, well-oxygenated stretches of large rivers with rocky or gravel beds.
  • Depends on free-flowing river conditions, making it highly sensitive to dam construction and flow regulation.
  • Historically ranged widely through the main Cauvery channel and its major tributaries before dams and habitat change fragmented its range.

Physical Characteristics

  • Size: The largest known mahseer species, capable of growing beyond 1.5 metres in length and up to around 45-55 kg in weight.
  • Body: A large, deep-bodied cyprinid with a distinctive humped back, giving the species its common name.
  • Colouration: Golden to orange-tinged fins, which also give it the alternate name "orange-finned mahseer."
  • Scales: Large, robust scales typical of the genus Tor, adapted for a powerful, fast-swimming lifestyle in strong river currents.

Diet

Like other mahseer species, the Hump-backed Mahseer is broadly omnivorous, feeding on a mix of algae, aquatic vegetation, insects, crustaceans, and smaller fish, adjusting its diet as it grows from a juvenile into the river's dominant apex-level fish.

Behaviour

The species is renowned among anglers for its exceptional strength and fighting ability when hooked, a trait that has made it one of the world's most sought-after freshwater game fish for over a century.

It shows migratory behaviour, moving between different stretches of the river system in response to seasonal flow patterns, particularly around the monsoon.

Reproduction

Mahseer species typically migrate upstream to specific gravel-bed stretches to spawn, timing their reproduction around monsoon-driven increases in river flow. This reliance on specific, undisturbed spawning grounds makes the species especially vulnerable to dams and barriers that block migratory routes within the Cauvery system.

Ecological Importance

  • As one of the largest fish in the Cauvery system, it plays an apex-level role in the river's freshwater food web.
  • Its presence is considered an indicator of a healthy, free-flowing, well-oxygenated river ecosystem.
  • Recognised as one of the world's top freshwater megafauna species, giving it significant value as a flagship species for river conservation in South India.

Conservation Status

The Hump-backed Mahseer was assessed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List in November 2018 - the only species in the genus Tor to be placed in this highest-risk category.

  • Long-term angler catch data recorded a dramatic decline: at one well-studied fishing camp, the ratio of hump-backed to introduced blue-finned mahseer fell from roughly 1 in 4 in 1998 to about 1 in 218 by 2012.
  • Researchers estimate the population has fallen by around 90% over recent decades.
  • Only a small number of isolated, self-sustaining populations are believed to remain in the wild.

Threats

  • Dam Construction: Dams across the Cauvery have altered natural flow rates and blocked migratory routes needed for spawning.
  • Water Abstraction: Over-extraction of river water for irrigation and other uses has reduced habitat availability.
  • Pollution: Declining water quality has degraded suitable habitat in parts of the river system.
  • Destructive Fishing Methods: Unsustainable and illegal fishing practices have directly reduced numbers.
  • Invasive Species Competition: Introduction of non-native mahseer and other fish species for angling purposes has led to competition and hybridisation pressure on native populations.

Conservation Programs

  • Researchers at Bournemouth University and the Mahseer Trust have led long-term studies into the species' taxonomy, population trends, and conservation needs.
  • The Wildlife Association of South India (WASI) has worked for decades to document remaining populations and promote river conservation in the Cauvery basin.
  • Organised angling programs have doubled as valuable long-term population monitoring tools, providing decades of catch-rate data used to track the species' decline.
  • Conservationists have called for coordinated river-management action across Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu to protect the species' remaining habitat.

Interesting Facts

  • It is popularly known as the "Tiger of the Cauvery" and ranks among the world's top 20 freshwater megafauna species.
  • It is the largest known species of mahseer, with historic records of individuals exceeding 1.5 metres and 45 kg.
  • For decades it was mistakenly linked to other fish, including the Himalayan golden mahseer and a separate river carp species, before its true identity was resolved in 2018.
  • At some riverside temples along the Cauvery, mahseer gather to be fed by pilgrims, giving the fish a place in local cultural and religious tradition.
  • It was first recorded in scientific literature in 1849 and first appeared in angling records in 1873, making it one of the longest-documented freshwater fishes in India.
