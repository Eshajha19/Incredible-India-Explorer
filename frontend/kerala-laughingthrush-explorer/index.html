Introduction

The Kerala Laughingthrush - also known as the Palani Laughingthrush - is a songbird endemic to the high-elevation shola forests of the southern Western Ghats, found only in the hills south of the Palghat Gap. It belongs to a small group of birds so tightly bound to these isolated montane forest patches that scientists refer to their habitat as the "Shola Sky Islands."

With its soft grey bib, rufous belly, and bold white eyebrow stripe, it is one of the more distinctive endemic birds of the Anaimalai and Palani hill ranges.

Scientific Name

Montecincla fairbanki (Blanford, 1869)

The species was originally described under other genus names, including Garrulax jerdoni fairbanki, Strophocincla fairbanki, and Trochalopteron fairbanki, before a 2017 genetic study placed it in the newly created genus Montecincla, established specifically for the laughingthrushes endemic to the Western Ghats' high-elevation "Sky Islands."

Distribution Map

The Kerala Laughingthrush is restricted to the hills of the Western Ghats south of the Palghat Gap, principally the Anaimalai and Palani hill ranges spanning parts of Kerala and Tamil Nadu. A closely related southern population in the Ashambu Hills, south of the Achankovil Gap, is now increasingly treated as a distinct species.

Habitat

  • Found in shola forests - dense, stunted evergreen forest patches confined to sheltered valleys at high elevation, interspersed with rolling montane grasslands.
  • Occupies the understorey and forest edges of these isolated shola "islands," rarely venturing far into open grassland.
  • Because shola forests exist as fragmented patches rather than continuous forest, the species' habitat is naturally scattered across the high hills, giving rise to the "Shola Sky Islands" concept used to describe this ecosystem.

Physical Characteristics

  • Crown & Face: Dark grey-brown crown with a narrow dark eyestripe, set off by a broad white supercilium (eyebrow) extending behind the eye.
  • Underparts: Grey throat and upper breast, faintly streaked with brown, transitioning to a rusty, rufous-brown lower breast and belly.
  • Upperparts: Olive-brown back and wings.
  • Bill: Noticeably lighter brown than the closely related black-chinned laughingthrush, useful for field identification.

Diet

Like other laughingthrushes, the Kerala Laughingthrush forages mainly in the leaf litter and dense undergrowth of shola forests, feeding on a mix of insects and other small invertebrates, along with fruits and berries available in its forest habitat.

Behaviour

The species is known to be territorial, defending patches of shola forest habitat, and is typically encountered in pairs or small family groups moving through dense undergrowth rather than in the open. Its calls, characteristic of the laughingthrush group, help researchers detect and monitor its presence in the field even when the bird itself stays hidden in cover.

Breeding

Detailed breeding biology of the Kerala Laughingthrush remains relatively poorly documented. As with other shola-dwelling laughingthrushes, nesting is understood to take place within the dense cover of shola forest undergrowth, offering protection for eggs and chicks from the region's frequent mist and rain.

Conservation Status

The Kerala Laughingthrush is currently assessed as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List. Its restricted range, confined to fragmented shola forest patches south of the Palghat Gap, makes it especially sensitive to further habitat loss.

The related, more southerly Ashambu population - increasingly recognised as a separate species - is assessed at a higher risk level of Vulnerable, underlining how precarious these Western Ghats shola-forest specialists are as a group.

Threats

  • Man-Made Fires: Recurring fires, particularly around peaks such as Agasthyarkoodam, damage shola forest patches that take decades to recover.
  • Illegal Tree Felling: Logging within shola forest fragments reduces available habitat and degrades forest structure.
  • Invasive Undergrowth: Disturbed shola patches are prone to being overtaken by dense reed thickets, which can alter habitat quality for forest-dependent birds.
  • Tourism & Plantation Pressure: In other parts of its range, expanding eco-tourism infrastructure and tea plantations have been flagged as emerging concerns for related shola bird species.

Interesting Facts

  • The genus Montecincla was created in 2017 specifically for the Western Ghats' shola-dwelling laughingthrushes, following genetic research showing they diverged from their Himalayan relatives millions of years ago.
  • The same 2017 study found a strikingly similar pattern of diversification in an unrelated bird group, the Sholicola shortwings, both shaped by the same ancient fragmentation of Western Ghats forest.
  • It was historically grouped with the grey-breasted subspecies of the black-chinned laughingthrush and known simply as the "grey-breasted laughingthrush."
  • Its closest relative, the Ashambu population further south, is now often treated as its own species due to subtle plumage differences, including a shorter white eyebrow stripe.
  • Along with other shola specialists, it is considered a strong indicator species for the health of the Western Ghats' rare high-elevation forest ecosystem.
