Himalayan Glaciers of India

Why It Matters

The Water Towers of Asia

Glaciers are not just scenery at the top of a catchment. They are a stored buffer that releases water exactly when rainfall does not.

A glacier is a reservoir with a very slow release valve. Snow that fell decades or centuries ago is compressed into ice, carried downhill, and melts at the bottom. The useful part is the timing: the melt peaks in late spring and summer, in the months before the monsoon arrives, when rivers would otherwise be at their lowest.

That is why glacier loss is a water-security problem and not only a climate statistic. The Indus depends heavily on snow and glacier melt. The Ganga and Brahmaputra get proportionally more of their volume from the monsoon, but glacier melt still carries their headwaters through the dry season, and it is the headwater reaches where hydropower and mountain settlements sit.

There is a counter-intuitive middle stage. As a glacier retreats it first releases more water, not less, because there is more exposed ice melting. Basins cross a point sometimes called peak water, after which flow declines permanently. Some Himalayan basins are believed to be near or past that point.

  • Melt is concentrated in the pre-monsoon and summer months, when rainfall is scarce
  • The Indus basin is the most glacier-dependent of the three
  • Retreat produces more water first, then permanently less
  • Retreat also leaves unstable lakes behind — the hazard grows as the resource shrinks

A Note on the Numbers

Retreat rates on this page are approximate. Published values for the same glacier differ depending on the measurement period, the method, and where exactly the snout is defined — debris-covered tongues are especially hard to measure. Each glacier card carries a note on the basis of its figures. Treat them as indicative of scale, not as precise measurements.
