🧶 Gujarat Handloom Heritage

Patola Silk & Double Ikat Weaving

Step into Patan's ancient loom houses to experience double ikat — a weaving technique so complex that both warp and weft threads are tie-dyed with mathematical precision before being woven.

900 Years of Craftsmanship

The Royal Patrons and Salvi Guilds of Patan

Patola weaving has been nurtured in Patan, Gujarat, since the 12th century CE. Historical chronicles recount that King Siddharaj Jaisinh of the Solanki dynasty invited over 700 weaver families from Maharashtra's Jalna region to settle in Patan. The King desired pure Patola textiles for daily temple rituals, as their colors and threads never fade.

Today, only a handful of Salvi families guard the master secrets of authentic Patan Patola. The craft is secured under Geographical Indication (GI) status. Traditionally passed down through oral training, creating a single Patola saree can take a team of four weavers up to six months of meticulous effort, making it a coveted family heirloom.

✨ Patola Highlights

  • Unfading Colors: Natural dyes extracted from marigold, indigo, madder root, and pomegranate peel preserve hues for centuries.
  • Reversible Elegance: Because warp and weft colors are aligned perfectly, the front and back patterns of a double-ikat Patola are identical.
  • Auspicious Motifs: Patterns represent guardians, prosperity, and connection to nature (elephants, parrots, girls, flowers).
The Mathematics of Thread

Understanding Double Ikat

Unlike single ikat (where only warp or weft yarns are dyed), Patola uses the complex Double Ikat technique.

🧵

1. The Warp (Vertical)

Vertical silk strands are mapped out on a wooden frame, grouped, and tied with cotton threads at calculated intervals to shield specific areas from the dyes.

🪡

2. The Weft (Horizontal)

Horizontal strands are mapped and tied independently to match the vertical pattern. Both sets are repeatedly tie-dyed in color sequences.

📐

3. Precise Grid Math

During handloom weaving, the dyed segments of the warp and weft must intersect perfectly. An error of even 1 millimeter throws the pattern off-grid.

Seven Steps of Mastery

The Journey of Patola Weaving

Discover the journey from raw mulberry silk to the completed double-ikat textile.

1

Yarn Degumming

Raw silk skeins are boiled in soap solution to remove natural gums, creating a soft white surface suitable for color absorption.

2

Design Mapping

The pattern is drafted onto graph paper. Computations determine the exact points on each thread bundle where dye must be blocked.

3

Resist Tying (Bandhana)

Weavers tie the silk thread bundles using cotton threads. Tightly wrapped cotton shields the silk from subsequent dye baths.

4

Color Layering

Threads are dyed starting from light yellow, then red, blue, and green. Cotton ties are cut and re-tied for each color phase.

5

Loom Dressing

The dyed warp threads are stretched across the rosewood handloom (Salvi loom), which is tilted downwards at an angle of 30 degrees.

6

Shuttle Winding

Weft threads are wound onto bamboo bobbins and loaded into the wooden shuttle, ready to meet their warp counterparts.

7

Interlocking Weft

Weavers slide the shuttle, aligning every single weft intersect using a steel needle. Only 6 to 8 inches of fabric are woven per day.
