Cabinet Mission Plan Explorer

In May 1946, a three-member British delegation proposed a last, intricate plan to keep India united — a three-tier federation balancing Congress's vision of a strong centre against the Muslim League's demand for Muslim-majority autonomy. Its collapse, within months, set India firmly on the road to Partition.

🌍 Post-War Situation & British Objectives

By early 1946, several pressures converged to force Britain's hand on India. Post-war Britain was economically exhausted after six years of war, with little appetite or capacity to hold on to an increasingly ungovernable empire. The Labour Party's landslide victory in July 1945, under Prime Minister Clement Attlee, brought to power a government more sympathetic to Indian self-rule than its predecessors.

On the ground, colonial authority was visibly weakening: the Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny of February 1946 showed that even Britain's own armed forces in India were no longer reliable, and public fury over the Red Fort trials of Indian National Army officers had united Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs in shared outrage. An earlier attempt at a settlement, the Cripps Mission of 1942, had already failed to satisfy either the Congress or the Muslim League.

Attlee's British objective was narrow and urgent: transfer power to Indian hands as quickly as possible, while trying — one last time — to keep India united rather than partitioned, since London feared partition would leave large religious minorities stranded on the wrong side of any new border. To achieve this, Attlee dispatched a delegation of senior cabinet ministers — the Cabinet Mission — to negotiate directly with Indian political leaders and devise machinery for framing a new constitution.

👥 Cabinet Mission Members

The Cabinet Mission arrived in New Delhi on 24 March 1946, working alongside Viceroy Lord Wavell. It comprised three senior British ministers:

Lord Pethick-Lawrence

Secretary of State for India

Headed the mission and led formal negotiations with Indian political leaders on behalf of the British government.

Sir Stafford Cripps

President of the Board of Trade

Brought experience from leading the earlier, unsuccessful Cripps Mission of 1942, and played a central role in drafting the plan's proposals.

A. V. Alexander

First Lord of the Admiralty

The third member of the delegation, contributing to talks alongside Pethick-Lawrence and Cripps.

Their mandate was to devise a mechanism for framing an Indian constitution and to help form an interim government — all without conceding the Muslim League's demand for a fully sovereign Pakistan.

📜 Major Proposals

After the tripartite Simla Conference collapsed in May 1946, the Mission issued its own proposals on 16 May 1946. It rejected both a fully unitary India and an outright partition into Pakistan, and instead proposed a three-tier federal structure:

1

The Union Centre

A loose Union of India covering British India and the princely states, holding authority over only three subjects: foreign affairs, defence, and communications — plus the finances needed to run them. All other powers, or "residuary powers," rested with the provinces.

2

Provincial Groups

Provinces were to be organized into three sections (A, B, and C) that could frame their own group constitutions, covering matters the provinces chose to share regionally.

3

The Provinces

Individual provinces retained wide autonomy and, crucially, an "opt-out clause" allowing them to leave their assigned group after the first general election and a ten-year review period.

The plan also proposed an Interim Government composed almost entirely of Indian leaders — every member except the Governor-General was to be Indian — to govern until the new constitution was finalized.

🏛️ Constituent Assembly

To draft India's new constitution, the plan proposed a Constituent Assembly of 389 members, elected indirectly by the existing provincial assemblies using proportional representation across three communal voting groups — General, Muslim, and Sikh.

  • 292 members elected from British India's provinces
  • 93 members nominated by the princely states
  • 4 members representing the Chief Commissioners' provinces

Under the plan's design, Sections A, B, and C would first meet separately to frame their own provincial and group constitutions, before all sections convened together to frame the Union Constitution. Any communal matters raised in the central legislature would require a majority of both major communities present and voting — a safeguard meant to reassure the Muslim League.

The Assembly finally convened on 9 December 1946 at the Constitution Hall in New Delhi. Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha served as its first temporary chairman before Dr. Rajendra Prasad became its permanent President — but the Muslim League boycotted this first session entirely, a sign of how badly the plan had already unravelled.

Congress welcomed the rejection of Pakistan and the plan's long-term constitutional machinery, but read the grouping of provinces as optional, not mandatory — provinces should be free to opt out immediately rather than only after ten years. Congress accepted participation in the Constituent Assembly while formally reserving its objections to compulsory grouping, a position that would prove decisive in the plan's collapse.

The Muslim League, under Muhammad Ali Jinnah, initially accepted the plan — it saw the grouping scheme as a route to a strong, quasi-autonomous Muslim-majority bloc that could later evolve toward full statehood. When Congress rejected compulsory grouping and insisted on provincial autonomy, Jinnah treated this as a repudiation of the plan, withdrew the League's acceptance, and reverted to demanding a fully sovereign Pakistan outright.

The princely states, though invited to send 93 nominated members to the Constituent Assembly, remained cautious and largely non-committal. Valuing their existing autonomy under British paramountcy, most rulers showed no urgency to align firmly with either the Congress or the Muslim League while the plan's fate remained uncertain.

🤝 Negotiations & Breakdown

The Mission's failure did not happen overnight — it unfolded through a chain of cause, negotiation, and eventual breakdown:

⚡

Cause

Post-war exhaustion, the RIN Mutiny, and the failed Cripps Mission make a constitutional settlement urgent.

→
💬

Negotiation

The Simla Conference fails; the Mission issues its own 16 May plan. The League conditionally accepts; Congress accepts participation but disputes compulsory grouping.

→
💥

Breakdown

Congress and the League cannot reconcile their reading of "grouping" as optional versus compulsory. The League withdraws acceptance and calls Direct Action Day.

An Interim Government was nonetheless formed on 2 September 1946, dominated by Congress with Jawaharlal Nehru at its head — though Congress maintained its opposition to mandatory grouping throughout. On 26 October 1946, Viceroy Lord Wavell brought the Muslim League into this Interim Government too, hoping to encourage cooperation — but League ministers largely joined to obstruct proceedings from within, and the two parties clashed on nearly every decision.

🔥 Direct Action Developments

With negotiations deadlocked, the Muslim League called for "Direct Action Day" on 16 August 1946, a general strike and mass demonstration intended to show the strength of Muslim demand for a separate Pakistan, concentrated in Calcutta.

The protests spiralled rapidly into widespread communal violence, remembered as the Great Calcutta Killings. Over roughly three days, several thousand people — estimated at around 4,000 dead and thousands more wounded, drawn from both Hindu and Muslim communities — were killed, with the unrest soon spreading to other regions including Bengal and Bihar.

Direct Action Day shattered whatever residual trust remained between Congress and the League, and convinced British authorities that continued delay in transferring power risked still greater violence — accelerating the timetable that would culminate in Partition less than a year later.

🏁 Political Consequences & Connection to Partition

Although the Cabinet Mission Plan itself collapsed, its consequences shaped everything that followed:

  • Accelerated British withdrawal: The violence and deadlock convinced London that a prolonged transition was untenable, paving the way for a much faster handover of power
  • Lord Mountbatten's arrival in March 1947 quickly shifted British policy from "preserve unity" to managing a negotiated partition, culminating in the Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947
  • The Constituent Assembly endured: Despite the League's boycott and the plan's failure, the Assembly born from the Cabinet Mission went on to draft the Constitution of independent India
  • A federal blueprint: The three-tier model's debates over centre-state power influenced later discussions of Indian federalism and states' rights
  • Direct line to Partition: The unresolved grouping dispute and Direct Action Day's violence are widely seen by historians as the point after which a united India became politically unworkable, making the eventual Partition of British India into India and Pakistan in August 1947 all but inevitable

The Cabinet Mission Plan is often remembered as history's great "what if" moment for India — the last serious, detailed proposal for a united, federal India that was ultimately overtaken by mutual distrust, communal violence, and the hardening of political positions on both sides.
