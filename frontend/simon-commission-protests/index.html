✊ 1928 · Nationwide Protest Movement

Simon Commission Protests

In 1928, an all-British Commission arrived to decide India's constitutional future without a single Indian member. The response — "Simon, Go Back" — united rival political factions in a nationwide boycott, and turned deadly on the streets of Lahore.

Why the Commission Was Formed

The Statutory Commission of 1927

The Government of India Act of 1919 required a review, after ten years, of how far Indians were ready for self-government. In November 1927, the British government appointed a seven-member Statutory Commission, chaired by Sir John Simon, to conduct that review. It landed in Bombay on 3 February 1928 to begin its tour of the country.

The Commission was tasked with examining the working of the 1919 reforms — including dyarchy in the provinces — and recommending whether and how India should move toward further constitutional change.

🗂️ At a Glance

  • Appointed: November 1927, by the British government.
  • Chair: Sir John Simon, a British Member of Parliament.
  • Members: Seven British MPs — no Indian member.
  • Arrival: Bombay, 3 February 1928.
  • Mandate: Review the Government of India Act, 1919 and recommend further reforms.
"All White, No Indian"

Why Indians Opposed the Commission

The Commission's composition, more than its mandate, provoked near-universal condemnation across India's political spectrum.

🚫

No Indian Members

All seven members of the Commission were British; not a single Indian was included in a body deciding India's constitutional future.

🤝

Rare Political Unity

The exclusion united the Indian National Congress, the Muslim League, the Hindu Mahasabha, and other groups in common opposition.

📜

An Insult to Self-Rule

Many saw the all-white panel as proof that Britain did not consider Indians capable of judging their own readiness for self-government.

📢

The "Simon, Go Back" Slogan

The protest cry became a rallying slogan nationwide, chanted at every stop of the Commission's tour.

✊ Forms of Protest

  • Hartals: Shops, schools and businesses shut down wherever the Commission travelled.
  • Black flag demonstrations: Crowds greeted the Commission with black flags and black balloons.
  • Boycotted proceedings: Most provincial legislatures and political parties refused to cooperate with or appear before the Commission.
  • The Nehru Report: An all-party conference, led by Motilal Nehru, drafted India's own constitutional blueprint in August 1928 as a direct counter-response.
A Boycott Without Precedent

India Responds With One Voice

As the Commission moved from city to city between February 1928 and its final visit in 1929, it was met almost everywhere by hartals, black-flag processions, and chants of "Simon, Go Back." The scale of the boycott — cutting across Congress, League, and regional parties — was unusual for a period otherwise marked by political division.

In parallel, Indian leaders moved to answer the Commission's implicit challenge — that Indians could not agree on a constitution of their own — by convening an all-party conference that produced the Nehru Report, proposing dominion status and a detailed constitutional framework drafted entirely by Indians.

Lahore

30 October 1928

The Commission's Lahore visit drew the movement's largest and most consequential procession, led by Lala Lajpat Rai. Police met the marchers with a lathi charge that left Lajpat Rai severely injured; he died seventeen days later.

"Punjab Kesari" — The Lion of Punjab

Lala Lajpat Rai and the Commission Protests

Lala Lajpat Rai was already one of India's most senior nationalist leaders — a founder of the Lal-Bal-Pal triumvirate alongside Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Bipin Chandra Pal, and a former Congress president — when he led the Lahore protest against the Simon Commission on 30 October 1928.

He personally led the march to the railway station, and was singled out and struck by police lathis during the ensuing charge. Though he initially seemed to recover, his health declined rapidly, and he died on 17 November 1928. His death is widely regarded as a direct consequence of the injuries he sustained that day, and it transformed him into a martyr of the independence movement.

🦁 Lala Lajpat Rai

  • Born: 28 January 1865, Punjab.
  • Known as: "Punjab Kesari" (Lion of Punjab).
  • Political legacy: Part of the Lal-Bal-Pal trio of assertive nationalist leaders; former Indian National Congress president.
  • Lahore protest: Led the 30 October 1928 march against the Commission.
  • Death: 17 November 1928, from injuries sustained in the police charge.
30 October 1928

The Lahore Protest and the Police Charge

The single most consequential confrontation of the entire boycott movement.

🚶

The Procession

A large, disciplined march set out toward Lahore railway station to greet the Commission's arrival with black flags and slogans, led by Lala Lajpat Rai.

🛑

The Police Cordon

Police, under Superintendent James A. Scott, moved to block the procession's path near the station, escalating a peaceful demonstration into confrontation.

💥

The Lathi Charge

Police baton-charged the marchers. Lala Lajpat Rai was struck directly and severely injured, along with several other leaders and demonstrators.

⚖️

Official Silence

No disciplinary action was taken against the officers responsible, deepening public outrage in the weeks that followed.

🏛️ How Leaders Responded

  • Indian National Congress: Condemned the police action and used the outrage to press for full independence rather than dominion status.
  • All-Party Conference: Convened under Motilal Nehru, producing the Nehru Report (August 1928) as India's own constitutional counter-proposal.
  • Muslim League: Divided over the Nehru Report's stance on separate electorates, a split that widened over the following years.
  • Congress, Calcutta 1928: Gave the British government a one-year ultimatum to grant dominion status, setting the stage for the 1929 Lahore session.
From Outrage to Ultimatum

Political Reactions Across India

News of the Lahore lathi charge and Lajpat Rai's death travelled fast, hardening political opinion across the country. Younger Congress leaders, including Jawaharlal Nehru, argued that dominion status was no longer enough, and pushed the party toward a more assertive demand for complete independence.

The Congress's December 1928 Calcutta session responded with an ultimatum: grant dominion status within a year, or face a countrywide civil disobedience movement. When the deadline passed unmet, the party's 1929 Lahore session adopted the Purna Swaraj (complete independence) resolution, with 26 January 1930 observed as the first Independence Day pledge.

Beyond Constitutional Politics

Revolutionary Consequences

For a younger generation of nationalists, Lajpat Rai's death was not just a political grievance — it was a call to direct action.

🔥

The HSRA Responds

Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, Chandrashekhar Azad and the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association resolved to avenge Lajpat Rai's death.

🎯

The Saunders Shooting

On 17 December 1928, HSRA members shot dead Assistant Superintendent J. P. Saunders in Lahore, believing him responsible for the fatal charge.

💣

The Assembly Bombing

On 8 April 1929, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw a non-lethal bomb inside the Central Legislative Assembly to protest repressive laws, then courted arrest.

⚰️

Trial and Execution

Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev were tried for Saunders's killing and executed on 23 March 1931, becoming enduring symbols of revolutionary sacrifice.

🇮🇳 Lasting Impact

  • Fused constitutional politics and revolutionary action against a shared grievance.
  • Produced the Nehru Report, India's first Indian-drafted constitutional blueprint.
  • Hardened Congress's demand from dominion status to full independence (Purna Swaraj).
  • Turned Lala Lajpat Rai and, later, Bhagat Singh into enduring symbols of sacrifice.
A Turning Point

Impact on the Independence Movement

The Simon Commission protests did more than register opposition to one Commission — they demonstrated that Indians across parties could unite around a shared cause, and that colonial repression would be met with an increasingly assertive response, both political and revolutionary.

The chain of events set in motion in 1928 — the Nehru Report, the Calcutta ultimatum, the Lahore Purna Swaraj resolution, and the trials and executions of the young revolutionaries who avenged Lajpat Rai — fed directly into the Civil Disobedience Movement and the Dandi March of 1930, marking 1928 as a pivotal year on the road to independence.
