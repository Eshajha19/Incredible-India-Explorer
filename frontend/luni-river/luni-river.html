The Luni River

The Lifeline of the Thar Desert

↓ Scroll to Explore

Origin & Name

The Luni River rises in the Pushkar valley of the Aravalli Range, near Ajmer. Originally known as Sagarmati, it takes the name Luni after meeting its tributary Sarasvati.

The name "Luni" is derived from the Sanskrit word Lavanavari, which translates to "Salt River". This is due to its high salinity, gathered as it flows through the salt-rich soils of the Thar Desert.

Etymology

  • Sanskrit: Lavanavari
  • Meaning: Salt River
  • Initial Name: Sagarmati

Desert Geography & Seasonal Nature

Unlike most major rivers, the Luni is endorheic. It does not flow into an ocean but terminates in the marshy lands of the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat. It is an ephemeral river, meaning it flows primarily during the monsoon season.

The soil along its banks is heavily alkaline. While the water is fresh near the origin in Ajmer, it turns increasingly saline as it reaches Balotra, making it unsuitable for irrigation beyond that point.

The Tributaries

The Luni basin is supported by several key tributaries, almost all of which join from the left bank (originating in the Aravalli hills). Only the Jojari river joins from the right.

Historical & Cultural Importance

The Luni River basin has been a critical lifeline for the Marwar region for centuries. In ancient times, it supported early settlements in a harsh arid climate and provided water for the historic trade routes traversing the Thar Desert. The river was essential for the survival of both wildlife and the nomadic pastoralist communities of Rajasthan.

Ancient Era

Early settlements along the upper fresh-water reaches.

Medieval Marwar

Essential water source for fort cities and trade caravans.

Modern Day

Focus of arid-zone hydrology and desertification studies.

Wildlife & Desert Ecology

The intermittent pools formed by the Luni River serve as vital oases for desert wildlife. During the dry months, these remaining water bodies sustain local populations of blackbuck, chinkara, and numerous migratory birds.

The high salinity of the surrounding soil creates unique micro-ecosystems where halophytic (salt-tolerant) plants thrive, preventing further desertification of the Marwar region.

Key Species

  • Mammals: Blackbuck, Chinkara (Indian Gazelle)
  • Birds: Demoiselle Cranes, Sandgrouse
  • Flora: Halophytes, Khejri trees
