01 · Geographic Setting

On the upper Sarasvati, between three capitals

Banawali sits on the left bank of the now-dried Sarasvati (Ghaggar) river, on its upper-middle valley — unlike Kalibangan, which occupied the lower-middle valley of the same dead river. That position placed the town within reach of three major Harappan centres without belonging fully to any one cluster.

The mound rises roughly 10 m above the surrounding plain, a visible marker of the site's long, repeated rebuilding — each generation raising floors and walls on the ruins of the last.

02 · Phase-by-Phase City Map

How the town itself changed

Three tabs, three excavated horizons. Town planning, fortification, houses and streets all shifted between phases — select a period to see its plan and read what changed.

Sub-phases: IA pre-defence → IB defence wall raised → IC transitional (Proto-Harappan)
  • Town planning. An unwalled farming settlement at first (Period IA), already showing well-planned houses rather than a random cluster.
  • Fortification. A defence wall of moulded mud brick is added in Period IB — the town's first perimeter, well before the Mature Harappan citadel existed.
  • Houses. Simple mud-brick houses, modest room counts, hand-finished floors.
  • Pottery. Sothi–Siswal-related painted wares; motifs grow simpler and sparser through the phase, with white pigment used less and less over time.
  • Continuity. Excavators read this as one continuous population, not a replacement — the same community that later builds the Mature Harappan town.
Trapezoidal fortified town, c. 200 × 500 m, with apsidal citadel
  • Town planning. A fully walled, chess-board-influenced town divided into two adjoining sectors — a raised citadel for the ruling/elite class and a lower town for everyone else. Unusually for a Harappan site, the citadel here is apsidal (semicircular-ended) rather than rectangular, and the lower town is trapezoidal — a real deviation from the classic grid.
  • Fortification. A defence wall over 105 m long, 4.5 m high and 6 m wide has been traced; late in this period a broad moat was dug around the town, likely as flood defence against the Sarasvati — the wall itself may ultimately have failed to water damage.
  • Streets. The lower town has north–south thoroughfares cutting at right angles, linked by east–west lanes — genuinely urban street planning. Near the citadel gateway complex the layout curves into a radial pattern, a direct consequence of the semicircular citadel.
  • Houses. Kiln-burnt and moulded brick construction, rammed-earth floors, mud-plastered walls, multiple rooms, kitchens and toilets. One multi-room house with seals and weights suggests a merchant's residence; a larger house with gold beads, lapis lazuli, carnelian and a gold-streaked touchstone suggests a jeweller's.
  • Drainage. No public street-drain network here (unlike Mohenjo-daro) — households instead used soak jars sunk at fixed points along the streets to absorb waste water.
Contraction and reoccupation — Banawali-Bara horizon
  • Town planning. Occupation contracts and reorganises; the strict citadel/lower-town division loses its earlier force.
  • Culture. This period is represented by the Bara culture — sometimes described as post-Harappan or a late contemporary of Harappa itself, with a distinct pottery style overlying the Mature Harappan levels.
  • Decline. Urban life at both Banawali and Kalibangan appears to end abruptly rather than fade gradually — a pattern excavators still debate.
  • Brick ratios shift. Earlier bricks follow the Kalibangan 3:2:1 ratio; later bricks shift to 4:2:1 — a small but telling sign of changing building convention over the town's lifetime.

06 · Trade Connections

A node between metropolises

Weights, seals and imported raw materials — gold, lapis lazuli, carnelian, marine shell — show Banawali plugged into both regional exchange with nearby Harappan towns and long-distance networks reaching toward the coast and the northwest highlands. Marine shell found this far from any coastline is itself evidence of active internal trade during the early Indus period.
