Introduction

Impatiens denisonii is a delicate flowering herb found only in the upper Nilgiri Hills of the Western Ghats. First described in 1862 by the British naturalist Richard Henry Beddome, it was once so abundant on the rocks and trees of the Nilgiris that it was considered one of the most beautiful plants of the region.

The species later vanished from scientific record for well over a century, and its rediscovery in 2002 made it one of the most remarkable botanical comebacks associated with the Nilgiri Hills.

Scientific Name

Impatiens denisonii Bedd.

The species name honours a colonial-era dedication by Beddome, and the abbreviation "Bedd." credits Richard Henry Beddome as the botanist who first formally described the plant.

Distribution

Impatiens denisonii is endemic to the Nilgiri Hills in the Western Ghats of South India, and is found nowhere else in the world. It is counted among roughly eighteen Impatiens species endemic to the Nilgiris, and is regarded as one of the rarest of them.

Historically, it was recorded as especially abundant along the western slopes of the Nilgiris near the Sispara Ghat, at elevations of roughly 900 to 1,500 metres.

Habitat

  • Grows in montane grasslands, along hedges, and in wastelands of the upper Nilgiri Hills.
  • Historically found growing directly on rocks and tree trunks on the steep western slopes near Sispara Ghat.
  • Restricted to a narrow elevation band of roughly 900-1,500 metres, closely tied to the shola-grassland mosaic characteristic of the upper Nilgiris.

Flower Characteristics

The plant produces numerous pink or purple flowers held on very long, slender, leafless flowering stalks (scapes) that rise well above the foliage.

  • The leaf base is deeply two-lobed with a broad sinus, typical of many Nilgiri balsams.
  • The petal "wings" are three-parted and bearded on the inner face.
  • A thread-like appendage is tucked inside a long, curved nectar spur - a hallmark feature used to distinguish this species from its close relatives.
  • Leaves are egg-shaped with a heart-like base and finely toothed, bristly edges, hairy above and smooth below.

Pollination

Like other members of the genus Impatiens, the flowers of I. denisonii are built around a long nectar spur that rewards visiting insects, typically bees and other long-tongued pollinators capable of reaching the nectar stored deep within it.

The plant's brightly coloured, upward-facing flowers and elevated scapes are thought to improve visibility to pollinators moving through the dense montane grasslands where it grows, a strategy shared with many of its Nilgiri relatives.

Ecological Importance

  • Contributes to the exceptional plant diversity of the Nilgiri shola-grassland ecosystem, one of the most biologically distinctive habitats in the Western Ghats.
  • As a nectar source, it supports pollinating insects within its narrow high-elevation habitat.
  • Its extreme rarity and narrow range make it an indicator species for the health of Nilgiri montane grasslands, which are themselves under pressure from land-use change.

Conservation Status

Impatiens denisonii is widely described in regional botanical literature as an endangered and critically rare species. After being recorded as abundant in the 19th century, it went unseen by botanists for well over a hundred years before being rediscovered in 2002.

Its extremely narrow distribution, restricted to a small elevation band of the Nilgiri Hills, places it among the most vulnerable of the region's endemic balsams.

Threats

  • Habitat Loss: Conversion and degradation of Nilgiri montane grasslands, including historical planting of exotic wattle and eucalyptus plantations, has reduced suitable habitat.
  • Extremely Narrow Range: A distribution limited to a small elevation band makes the species highly vulnerable to any local disturbance.
  • Low Visibility: The plant flowers only briefly each year, making it easy to overlook and difficult to monitor - a key reason it went unrecorded for so long.
  • Grazing & Trampling: Grassland habitats in the Nilgiris face ongoing pressure from grazing and human activity.

Interesting Facts

  • It was not seen by botanists for over 140 years after its original description by Beddome in 1862, before being rediscovered in 2002.
  • It was rediscovered by researcher Tarun Chhabra and the Edhkwehlynawd Botanical Refuge team, who spent three consecutive August-September flowering seasons searching for it before finally sighting it.
  • It is considered one of the rarest of roughly eighteen Impatiens species endemic to the Nilgiri Hills.
  • In the 19th century it was described as one of the most beautiful and abundant plants of the Nilgiri plateau - a striking contrast to its rarity today.
  • Its long, recurved nectar spur and thread-like floral appendage are distinguishing features used by botanists to separate it from closely related Nilgiri balsams.
