Fort William

The historic British fort in Kolkata, still an active military establishment.

Overview

Fort William is a historic fortress on the eastern bank of the River Hooghly in Kolkata (Calcutta), West Bengal. It is named after King William III of England and was the seat of British power in Bengal for over two centuries.

Uniquely, Fort William remains an active military establishment to this day - it serves as the headquarters of the Eastern Command of the Indian Army. The fort's grounds include the sprawling Maidan, the city's vast open parkland, which was originally created as a clear field of fire for the fort's cannons.

History

The first Fort William was built between 1696 and 1702 by the British East India Company near the settlement of Calcutta. It was a modest structure, built partly of brick and partly of mud.

In 1756, the fort was captured by Siraj-ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Bengal, in a dramatic episode that included the infamous Black Hole of Calcutta incident. The following year, Robert Clive defeated the Nawab at the Battle of Plassey (1757) and ordered the construction of a much stronger fort - the second Fort William, completed in 1773, which stands today.

Builder

The original Fort William was built by the British East India Company in the late 17th century. After the fort fell to Siraj-ud-Daulah in 1756, Robert Clive oversaw the construction of a far more formidable replacement following the Battle of Plassey.

The second fort was designed along the lines of a European star fortress, engineered to be virtually impregnable. It cost the East India Company a staggering sum (reportedly around two million pounds) and took over a decade to complete.

British East India Company

Fort William was the nerve centre of the British East India Company in India. From this fort, the Company administered Bengal, its most valuable province, and conducted trade, diplomacy, and warfare across the subcontinent.

The fort housed the Company's governor, treasury, and garrison, and served as the launch point for campaigns that eventually brought most of India under British control. Its capture and recapture in the 1750s directly shaped the course of British expansion in India, culminating in the establishment of the British Raj.

Architecture

The second Fort William is a masterpiece of 18th-century military engineering.

  • Octagonal Plan: The fort is built in the shape of an irregular octagon, designed to eliminate blind spots.
  • Massive Ramparts: Thick bastioned walls and ramparts, several metres high, encircle the fort.
  • Protected Harbour: The fort faced the Hooghly river, allowing it to be supplied and reinforced by sea.
  • Gates Named After Generals: Its gates bear the names of British generals, such as St. George's Gate and Plassey Gate.
  • St. Peter's Church: A historic church within the fort, serving the garrison.

Unlike the first fort, the second Fort William was never attacked or captured.

Military Importance

Fort William's military significance has endured for over 250 years. It served as the headquarters of the British Indian Army's Bengal command and later became a key garrison of independent India.

  • It is currently the headquarters of the Eastern Command of the Indian Army.
  • The fort remains a restricted military zone, not open to general public access.
  • St. Peter's Church inside the fort opens to the public on one day each year.
  • The surrounding Maidan was deliberately kept open to provide the fort with a clear field of fire.

Interesting Facts

  • Fort William is named after King William III of England.
  • The first fort was captured by Siraj-ud-Daulah in 1756, an event tied to the Black Hole of Calcutta.
  • The second fort, completed in 1773, was built at the direction of Robert Clive.
  • The fort has never been attacked since its completion.
  • It remains an active military base - the HQ of the Indian Army's Eastern Command.
  • The Maidan parkland was created by clearing the surrounding jungle for the fort's defence.
  • The Victoria Memorial and the Race Course stand on the opposite edge of the Maidan.
