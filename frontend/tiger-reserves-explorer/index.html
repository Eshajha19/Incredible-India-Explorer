Tiger Reserves of India

India began with nine reserves and roughly 18,000 km². It now runs the largest tiger conservation programme in the world — and holds about seventy per cent of the tigers left alive in the wild. This is what a tiger reserve actually is, how the animals in it are counted, and where the programme has succeeded and failed.

The Category

A Reserve Is Not a National Park

The two are used interchangeably in everyday speech and they are not the same thing. The difference is structural, and it drives almost everything about how a reserve is run.

A tiger reserve is notified under Section 38V of the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, on the recommendation of the National Tiger Conservation Authority. That notification does two things a national park notification does not: it imposes a mandatory two-zone structure, and it brings the area under a Tiger Conservation Plan that the NTCA has to approve.

The two zones are the point. The core, or critical tiger habitat, is meant to be inviolate — kept free of human settlement so that tigers can breed undisturbed. The buffer is deliberately not inviolate. It is multiple-use land where people live, graze and collect, and where dispersing young tigers are expected to turn up. Managing a reserve is largely the work of managing that second zone, because that is where tigers and people actually meet.

A reserve is also the larger unit. Corbett Tiger Reserve contains Corbett National Park; it is not a renaming of it. The same is true almost everywhere — a reserve typically wraps around one or more parks and sanctuaries and adds buffer around them.

  • Notified centrally on NTCA recommendation, not by the state alone
  • Core must be inviolate; relocation requires informed consent and a settled package
  • Buffer is multiple-use by design, not by concession
  • Governed by a Tiger Conservation Plan, and centrally funded through Project Tiger

On the Numbers

Tiger figures on this page come from the four-yearly All India Tiger Estimation, which publishes a range with confidence limits rather than a count. The midpoints quoted here are convenient, not precise, and reserve-level figures carry wider bands than the national one. Areas change whenever a fresh notification is issued. Treat everything here as indicative and dated.

Zoning

Core, Buffer, Corridor

Three pieces of land with three different jobs. Only one of them is legally inviolate, and the one with no legal status at all may matter most.

🎯

Core / Critical Tiger Habitat

Inviolate by law

Kept free of human settlement and resource use so that breeding females can raise cubs without disturbance. Tourism is capped and restricted to designated routes. This is the part of the reserve that produces tigers.

🌾

Buffer / Peripheral Area

Multiple use, by design

People live and work here. Dispersing sub-adult tigers move through it, and increasingly breed in it. Nearly all human–tiger conflict happens in the buffer, which makes it the hardest zone to manage and the one that decides whether a reserve is tolerated locally.

🔗

Corridor

Largely without legal status

The forest, farmland and scrub connecting one reserve to the next. Corridors are what stop a reserve becoming a genetic island, and most of them have no protected-area designation at all — which is precisely why highways, canals and mines keep being routed through them.

How Tigers Are Counted

The All India Tiger Estimation

Six steps, and one important caveat: the answer is a range, not a number.

⚠️

Why the 2006 figure looked like a collapse. Until 2005, tigers were counted by tracing pug marks and treating each distinct trace as an animal. The method over-counted badly and could not be checked. When camera-trap-based sampling replaced it, the national estimate fell from over 3,500 to about 1,411. Nothing had happened to the tigers that year — the earlier number had simply never been right.
