Nilgiri Pipit

📖 Introduction

The Bird of the Shola Skies

What is the Nilgiri Pipit?

The Nilgiri Pipit is a small passerine bird endemic to the southern Western Ghats of India. It belongs to the pipit family (Motacillidae) and is one of only a handful of bird species found exclusively in this biodiversity hotspot. Unlike its widespread relatives, the Nilgiri Pipit has never been recorded outside its narrow high-altitude home.

Its scientific name, Anthus nilghiriensis, directly references the Nilgiri Hills where it was first described. The bird is closely associated with the unique shola-grassland ecosystem — a mosaic of stunted evergreen forest patches (sholas) separated by rolling montane grasslands that exists only above approximately 1,800 metres in the Western Ghats.

Why is it Special?

The Nilgiri Pipit is a sky island specialist. Its habitat — the high-altitude grasslands of the Western Ghats — forms isolated patches separated by valleys that are too warm or too dry for the bird to cross. This makes each population effectively an island, with limited gene flow between mountaintops.

Because its entire range is confined to a narrow altitudinal band, climate change poses an existential threat. As temperatures rise, the bird's suitable habitat shifts upward until it simply runs out of mountain. This makes the Nilgiri Pipit one of India's most climate-vulnerable endemic birds.

Relationship to Other Pipits

The Nilgiri Pipit is most closely related to the Paddyfield Pipit (Anthus rufulus) and the Long-billed Pipit (Anthus similis). However, genetic and morphological studies confirm it is a distinct species that diverged from its lowland relatives millions of years ago as the Western Ghats rose.

It was once considered a subspecies of the Richard's Pipit but is now universally recognised as a full species based on its unique plumage, vocalisations, and high-altitude adaptation.

Distinguishing Features

The Nilgiri Pipit is a stout, heavy-billed pipit with strongly streaked upperparts and a distinctly marked breast. It is larger and heavier than the widespread Paddyfield Pipit, with a longer bill adapted for probing in high-altitude grass tussocks.

Adults have brown upperparts with bold dark streaks, pale underparts with heavy breast streaking that forms a partial band, and a pale supercilium (eyebrow stripe). The bill is noticeably longer and heavier than in sympatric pipit species.

🗺️ Distribution

Where the Nilgiri Pipit Lives

Geographic Range

The Nilgiri Pipit is endemic to the southern Western Ghats, occurring in the high-altitude grasslands of Tamil Nadu and Kerala. Its core range centres on the Nilgiri Hills (Tamil Nadu) and extends south through the Anaimalai, Palani, and Cardamom Hills (Kerala).

The species is entirely confined to montane grasslands above approximately 1,800 metres. Key strongholds include the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, Eravikulam National Park (home of the Nilgiri Tahr), Mukurthi National Park, and the grassy ridges around Ooty, Kodaikanal, and Munnar.

Altitudinal Range

The bird is typically found between 1,800 and 2,600 metres above sea level. Below 1,800 metres, the habitat transitions to lower-elevation forest or plantation that the pipit cannot use. Above 2,600 metres, the grassland becomes too sparse and rocky to support its foraging needs.

This narrow altitudinal band means the species occupies what ecologists call a "sky island" — an isolated habitat patch on a mountaintop, surrounded by unsuitable lower-elevation land that acts as a barrier to dispersal.

🏔️ Habitat

Montane Grasslands of the Shola Mosaic

Shola-Grassland Mosaic

The Nilgiri Pipit's habitat is the unique shola-grassland ecosystem — a naturally occurring mosaic of stunted evergreen forest patches (sholas) nestled in valleys, separated by rolling montane grasslands on ridges and plateaus. The pipit is strictly a grassland species and avoids the shola forest interior.

High-Altitude Specialist

Found above approximately 1,800 metres, the bird requires open, short-grass habitats with scattered rocks and tussocks. These conditions exist only in the high-altitude "sky islands" of the southern Western Ghats, making it one of India's most altitudinally restricted birds.

Microhabitat Needs

The pipit needs short grass for foraging, tussocks for cover and nesting, and exposed rocks for perching and scanning for predators. It avoids dense or tall grass, recently burned areas, and areas with heavy livestock grazing.

Climate Dependence

The shola-grassland mosaic is maintained by a specific combination of cool temperatures, high rainfall (2,000–4,000 mm annually), and seasonal fire. The pipit depends on this equilibrium — too dry and the grassland degrades; too wet and forest invades.

Protected Areas

Key protected habitats include Eravikulam National Park, Mukurthi National Park, and the grasslands within the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve. These reserves protect the shola-grassland mosaic from plantation conversion and heavy grazing.

Habitat Fragmentation

The bird's habitat is naturally fragmented — each mountaintop grassland is an "island" separated by warmer valleys. This makes the species especially vulnerable to further fragmentation from plantations, roads, and settlements.

🍽️ Diet

What the Nilgiri Pipit Eats

Insectivorous Forager

The Nilgiri Pipit is primarily insectivorous, feeding on small invertebrates found in the high-altitude grasslands. Its diet includes beetles, ants, small grasshoppers, caterpillars, spiders, and other arthropods that live among the grass tussocks and in the soil.

The bird forages by walking or running along the ground, using its long heavy bill to probe into the grass base and soil. It is a methodical forager, moving in short bursts and pausing to scan for prey.

Seasonal Variation

Diet composition shifts with seasonal invertebrate availability. During the monsoon (June–September), when insect activity is highest, the pipit feeds on a wider variety of prey. In the dry winter months, it relies more on ants and beetles that remain active.

Seeds are occasionally consumed but are not a significant part of the diet. The bird does not visit feeders or human habitations for food.

🐦 Behaviour

Life in the High-Altitude Grasslands

Terrestrial Lifestyle

The Nilgiri Pipit is almost entirely terrestrial. It spends most of its time on the ground, running rather than hopping. It perches on rocks and grass tussocks but rarely flies to trees. When disturbed, it runs quickly through the grass rather than taking flight.

Song and Display

Males sing from exposed perches such as rocks or the tops of grass tussocks. The song is a thin, high-pitched tseep-tseep call, repeated frequently. During the breeding season, males perform short song flights — rising a few metres, singing, then descending.

Breeding Season

Breeding occurs during the pre-monsoon and early monsoon period (March–July). The nest is a cup of grass placed on the ground, often sheltered by a tussock or rock. Clutches typically contain 2–3 eggs. Both parents feed the chicks.

Social Structure

Outside the breeding season, Nilgiri Pipits are often seen in small groups of 3–6 birds foraging together. During breeding, they are territorial and defend foraging and nesting areas against other pipits.

Anti-Predator Behaviour

When threatened, the pipit freezes and relies on its streaked plumage for camouflage against the grass. If the predator approaches, it flushes with a short burst of flight, then drops back into the grass. Raptors and shrikes are the main predators.

Thermoregulation

At high altitudes, temperatures can drop sharply. The pipit conserves heat by fluffing its plumage and sheltering in grass tussocks during the coldest parts of the day. It is active during the warmer mid-morning and afternoon hours.

🛡️ Conservation

Conservation Status

Vulnerable (VU)

The Nilgiri Pipit is classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Its extent of occurrence is estimated at approximately 2,000 km², and its population is believed to be declining due to habitat loss, degradation, and climate change.

Legal Protection

The species is listed under Schedule IV of the Indian Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, which provides it with legal protection against hunting and trade. Its key habitats fall within protected areas including national parks and biosphere reserves.

Conservation Efforts

Conservation of the Nilgiri Pipit centres on protecting the shola-grassland mosaic from conversion to monoculture plantations (wattle, eucalyptus, pine). Grassland restoration programmes in Eravikulam and Mukurthi National Parks directly benefit the species. Monitoring programmes track population trends.

⚠️ Threats

What Endangers the Nilgiri Pipit

Critical

Climate Change

As temperatures rise, the bird's altitudinal band shifts upward. Eventually the species runs out of mountain — there is simply no higher habitat to move to. This "escalator to extinction" is the most severe long-term threat.

Critical

Plantation Invasion

Montane grasslands have been extensively converted to wattle (Acacia mearnsii), eucalyptus, and pine plantations. These plantations are dark and ecologically dead for the pipit, replacing its open foraging habitat with impenetrable monoculture.

High

Livestock Grazing

Overgrazing by cattle and buffalo in grasslands outside protected areas degrades habitat structure. Heavy grazing removes grass tussocks needed for nesting and compact the soil, reducing invertebrate prey availability.

High

Invasive Weeds

Invasive species such as Scotch broom (Cytisus scoparius) and mistflower (Ageratina riparia) invade grasslands, altering structure and displacing native vegetation. These invasions are particularly severe in degraded areas.

Moderate

Tourism Pressure

High tourist traffic in hill stations like Ooty and Kodaikanal leads to trampling of grassland habitat, litter, and disturbance during the breeding season. Off-road vehicle use destroys nests and foraging grounds.

Moderate

Habitat Fragmentation

Roads, settlements, and agricultural expansion further fragment the already isolated sky island populations, reducing genetic diversity and making local extinctions more likely.

✨ Facts

Interesting Facts

1

The Nilgiri Pipit is one of only about 30 bird species endemic to the Western Ghats biodiversity hotspot.

2

Its entire global range is smaller than many city metropolitan areas — confined to a thin band of mountain grassland.

3

The bird shares its habitat with another Western Ghats endemic, the Nilgiri Tahr — and both depend on the same shola-grassland mosaic.

4

Unlike most pipits, the Nilgiri Pipit has a heavier bill adapted for probing in dense high-altitude tussocks.

5

It is considered an "escalator to extinction" species — climate change pushes it higher until there is no more mountain.

6

The species was first scientifically described in 1885 by the British ornithologist Richard Bowdler Sharpe.

7

It is sometimes called the "Nilgiri Grassbird" in older literature, though it is a true pipit, not a grassbird.

8

The pipit's song is unusually high-pitched for a pipit, an adaptation to the thin, cool mountain air.
