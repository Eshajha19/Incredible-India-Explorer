Jallianwala Bagh and the making of a nation's resolve

AMRITSAR, PUNJAB · 13 APRIL 1919

A walled garden, a festival crowd, and ten minutes of gunfire that changed the direction of India's freedom movement. This explorer sets the massacre inside the wider story of the Rowlatt Act, the unrest in Punjab, and the inquiry and reckoning that followed.

Background

The Rowlatt Act

By early 1919, the emergency powers Britain had used against India during the First World War were about to be made permanent.

Wartime India had lived under the Defence of India Act, which let the colonial government detain people without trial and try political cases without a jury. In March 1919, the Imperial Legislative Council passed the Rowlatt Act, extending many of those powers into peacetime. It allowed detention without trial, gave the government sweeping authority to search and arrest, and let political cases be heard by judges alone, with no right of appeal.

Every elected Indian member of the Council voted against it. Gandhi, until then a supporter of the war effort in hope of eventual self-rule, called for a nationwide hartal — a day of business closure and fasting — as peaceful protest. The response across Indian cities, including Amritsar, was immediate and large.

What the Act actually changed

From wartime measure to standing law

Critics named it the "Black Act": for the first time, emergency powers built for a war would apply with no war and no end date, aimed squarely at political dissent rather than external threat.

Context

Punjab's political situation

Punjab had given the war effort more than almost any other province: a disproportionate share of India's wartime military recruits, and heavy war-loan contributions extracted under pressure that bordered on coercion. Lieutenant Governor Michael O'Dwyer ran the province with a firm hand, suspicious of the nationalist movement and quick to treat public gatherings as sedition rather than grievance.

That combination — wartime sacrifice without wartime reward, a Rowlatt Act that felt like betrayal, and an administration inclined to see conspiracy in protest — made Punjab, and Amritsar especially, the most volatile province in India that spring.

Amritsar

Protests against the Rowlatt Act

In Amritsar, the hartal was organised by two young local leaders: Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal, a Muslim and a Hindu physician who worked together and were seen locally as a symbol of Hindu-Muslim unity in the anti-Rowlatt campaign. Their meetings drew large, disciplined crowds and stayed explicitly non-violent in their stated aims.

O'Dwyer's administration read the popularity of Kitchlew and Satyapal as the real danger — not the Rowlatt Act itself — and moved to remove them from the city rather than negotiate.

9–10 April 1919

Arrests and the collapse of order

9 April 1919 — Ram Navami
Kitchlew and Satyapal are summoned and deported

On O'Dwyer's orders, Deputy Commissioner Miles Irving had the two leaders picked up and secretly removed from Amritsar without public announcement or trial.

10 April 1919
A protest march is fired on; the city erupts

A crowd marching to the Deputy Commissioner's residence to demand the leaders' release was stopped and fired on. Rioting followed: banks and government buildings were attacked, telegraph lines cut, and five British men were killed. A British missionary, Marcella Sherwood, was assaulted by a mob while cycling through a lane later sealed off by martial law.

11 April 1919
Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer takes military command of Amritsar

With civil authority effectively lost, the city passed to military control. Dyer issued a proclamation banning public gatherings and processions — but it was posted in only a few locations and read aloud at just a handful of points in a city of hundreds of thousands.

13 April 1919, afternoon

The gathering at Jallianwala Bagh

13 April was Baisakhi, the Punjabi harvest festival and Sikh new year, and thousands of visitors from surrounding villages were already in Amritsar for the Golden Temple. Many drifted into Jallianwala Bagh, a walled patch of open ground about six acres in size, ringed by the backs of houses and reached mainly through one narrow lane.

A political meeting was also planned there that afternoon — to protest the 10 April firing and to call for the leaders' release — and the two crowds overlapped. Because Dyer's proclamation had barely circulated, many present, especially the festival visitors, had no idea an assembly ban was even in force.

On the space itself: Jallianwala Bagh was not a park in the modern sense — an enclosed, largely walled ground with buildings on most sides and very limited ways out, one of them a narrow lane barely wide enough for a few people abreast.
13 April 1919, ~5:15pm

The events of 13 April

This account is presented factually and without graphic detail, in keeping with the gravity of what happened.

Dyer marched a detachment of around fifty riflemen into the Bagh through the narrow entrance, positioned them on raised ground, and — without ordering the crowd to disperse or giving any warning — commanded them to open fire directly into the gathering. The shooting continued for roughly ten minutes, an estimated 1,650 rounds fired, aimed toward the densest parts of the crowd and the exits rather than into the air.

There was no way out for most of those present. The single main exit was blocked by troops, and people trying to escape over the walls or through the well in the grounds added to the casualties. Dyer later testified to the Hunter Commission that he had gone to the Bagh intending to produce a "moral effect," and withdrew his troops without arranging medical aid for the wounded.

Casualty figures — contested to this day

No single agreed number

The Hunter Commission's official figure was 379 dead. The Indian National Congress's own inquiry, led in part by Motilal Nehru, put the toll above 1,000. A 2019 study by Amritsar district authorities, drawing on hospital and family records alongside British dispatches, estimated more than 1,500 killed and wounded combined. No figure is treated as final by historians.

"I fired and continued to fire until the crowd dispersed." — Reginald Dyer, testimony to the Hunter Commission, November 1919 (paraphrased from committee records)
April–June 1919

Immediate aftermath

Martial law spread across much of Punjab in the weeks after the massacre. Public floggings, collective fines on villages, and aerial bombing of a crowd near Gujranwala followed. Newspapers were censored and movement was restricted, extending the punitive reach of the crackdown far beyond Amritsar itself.

In Amritsar, Dyer introduced the so-called "crawling order" for the lane where Marcella Sherwood had been attacked: Indians passing through it were made to crawl on all fours, and public whipping was used against those accused of involvement in the 10 April violence. These measures, alongside the massacre itself, became central evidence in the case against the administration's conduct.

October 1919 – March 1920

The Hunter Commission

Facing public pressure, the government appointed the Disorders Inquiry Committee, chaired by Lord William Hunter, to investigate the massacre and the wider Punjab disturbances. It heard testimony in Amritsar and elsewhere from November 1919, including from Dyer himself, and reported in March 1920.

The majority report criticised Dyer's decision as a grave "error of judgement" but stopped short of recommending criminal or disciplinary proceedings. The committee's Indian members issued a separate, sharper minority report. The Army Council later relieved Dyer of his command and he returned to Britain on half pay — but the House of Lords voted to endorse his actions, and a public fund organised by the Morning Post newspaper raised roughly £26,000 in his support.

1919–1920

Political reactions

In Britain

A divided response

Winston Churchill, then Secretary of State for War, condemned the shooting in the House of Commons as a singular, monstrous event with no parallel in modern British history — while much of the press and the House of Lords treated Dyer as a man who had saved the Raj.

In India

Moderates turn nationalist

The massacre and the whitewash many Indians saw in the Hunter Commission's findings pushed a generation of moderate, cooperative Indian leaders toward the nationalist cause, hardening opinion across the political spectrum.

A personal protest

Tagore renounces his knighthood

In May 1919, Rabindranath Tagore wrote to the Viceroy renouncing the knighthood he had received in 1915, saying it was no longer bearable to hold an honour beside people subjected to what he called inhuman treatment.

1919 onward

Long-term impact on the freedom movement

Jallianwala Bagh convinced Gandhi that continued cooperation with the colonial government was untenable. Within a year he launched the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–22, the first mass, countrywide campaign of the independence struggle, drawing directly on the loss of faith the massacre had caused.

The site itself became a place of memory. The Government of India built a formal memorial there in 1951, and the Yaad-e-Jallian museum opened nearby in 2019, on the massacre's centenary. In 1997, Queen Elizabeth II visited and called it a distressing example of history; in 2019, the British Prime Minister formally expressed regret in Parliament, though successive British governments have stopped short of an official apology.

Full chronology

From the Rowlatt Act to remembrance

18 March 1919
Rowlatt Act passed

Emergency wartime powers of detention without trial are extended into peacetime, over the objection of every elected Indian member of the Legislative Council.

6 April 1919
Nationwide hartal

Gandhi's call for a countrywide day of protest against the Rowlatt Act draws a large response, including in Amritsar.

9 April 1919
Kitchlew and Satyapal deported

The two local protest leaders are arrested and secretly removed from the city on O'Dwyer's orders.

10 April 1919
Amritsar riots

A march demanding the leaders' release is fired on; rioting follows, five British men are killed, and Marcella Sherwood is assaulted.

13 April 1919
Jallianwala Bagh massacre

Troops under Brigadier-General Dyer open fire without warning on the crowd gathered in the Bagh.

15 April 1919
Martial law declared across Punjab

Punitive measures, including public floggings and the crawling order, follow in Amritsar and beyond.

May 1919
Tagore renounces his knighthood

Rabindranath Tagore writes to the Viceroy resigning the honour in protest.

Oct 1919 – Mar 1920
Hunter Commission inquiry and report

The Disorders Inquiry Committee hears testimony and censures Dyer's judgement without recommending prosecution.

1920
Non-Cooperation Movement launched

Gandhi leads the Indian National Congress into its first mass campaign against British rule.

13 March 1940
Udham Singh kills Michael O'Dwyer

Twenty-one years after witnessing the massacre as a young man, Udham Singh shoots O'Dwyer in London.

1951
Jallianwala Bagh memorial completed

The Government of India dedicates a formal memorial and garden on the site.

2019
Centenary and the Yaad-e-Jallian museum

On the hundredth anniversary, a dedicated museum opens near the site and the British Prime Minister expresses regret in Parliament.
