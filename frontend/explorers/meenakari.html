Historical Overview

From Safavid Persia to the Pink City

Meenakari's name comes from the Persian mina — a heaven-blue enamel used to decorate palace ceilings. The craft travelled with courts, conquerors and craftsmen, settling permanently in Rajasthan.

15th – 16th Century · Safavid Iran

Origins in Persian palace art

Enamelling of metal and ceramic tiles flourished under the Safavid dynasty, prized for the way fired glass held colour permanently under Persian sun and candlelight alike.

16th Century · Mughal Court

Carried east by Mughal patronage

As Mughal power expanded, Persian minakars were drawn into imperial workshops, where enamelling was applied to jewellery, weapons, and vessels for the Mughal nobility.

16th Century · Amber & Jaipur

Raja Man Singh brings artisans from Lahore

Raja Man Singh of Amber invited skilled minakars from Lahore to settle in Rajasthan, laying the foundation for what would become the art's most celebrated home.

18th Century · Sawai Jai Singh II

Jaipur becomes the enamel capital

The founder of Jaipur actively patronised local artisans, and the city's gold enamelling — especially its signature ruby-red ground — became internationally renowned.

Today · Regional Schools

A living craft with distinct dialects

Jaipur is known for gold and its rich red; Lucknow for blue-and-green work on silver; Varanasi for pink gulabi meena; Bikaner and Udaipur for fine silver enamelling; Pratapgarh for glass ename­lling on gold foil.

The Enameling Process

How colour is fused, not painted

Meenakari is a relay between several craftsmen — engraver, minakar, and polisher — and every colour is a separate act of fire.

01

Engraving the design — चित्रकारी / chitai

A goldsmith cuts the motif's outline into the metal, then a specialist engraver deepens the grooves that will hold each colour of enamel, keeping every compartment separate.

02

Grinding the enamel — मीना घोटना

Solid enamel — coloured glass fused from metal oxides — is ground with a mortar and pestle into a fine paste, one colour at a time, mixed to the right consistency with water.

03

Filling the grooves

Using a fine needle-tipped tool, the minakar packs the enamel paste into each engraved cell, working from the colour that needs the highest firing temperature down to the lowest.

04

Firing in the kiln — भट्टी

The piece is fired between roughly 750–850°C. The powdered glass melts and fuses permanently to the metal — this step repeats for every colour, hottest first.

05

Cooling & polishing

Once cooled, the surface is polished to a glassy shine with fine abrasives, revealing the depth and translucency that makes fired enamel glow from within.

06

Setting the stones — जड़ाई

If the piece is kundan jewellery, the jadiya sets refined gold and gemstones on the front face — meaning the enamel now hides, unseen, on the reverse.
