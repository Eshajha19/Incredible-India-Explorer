Tribal Resistance · 1855–1856

The Santhal Hul

Hul — "the great uprising". The first organized tribal revolt against the British East India Company, zamindars, and moneylenders in the forests of Jharkhand and Bengal.

60,000+ Santhal warriors

1856 Santhal Parganas formed
🌾 The Uprising

What was the Santhal Hul?

Background

The Santhals are one of India's largest tribal communities, originally inhabiting the regions of present-day Jharkhand, Odisha, Bihar and West Bengal. They had lived a semi-nomadic agrarian life for centuries, governed by their own manjhi-pargana system of village councils, with deep ties to forests and land.

When the British introduced the Permanent Settlement of 1793 in Bengal, zamindars and moneylenders (known as dikus or "outsiders") began encroaching on Santhal lands, pushing them into debt bondage and forced labour.

The Spark

By the 1850s, the Santhals had been driven into the Rajmahal hills, where they cleared forests for cultivation. But even there, zamindari agents and moneylenders followed — charging usurious interest (often over 50%), using forged documents, and seizing land.

The final trigger came when petitions to the British colonial authorities at Bhagalpur went unheard. Convinced that their survival was at stake, the Murmu brothers declared Hul — "revolution" — on 30 June 1855.

⚖️ Root Causes

Why the Santhals Rose

🏛️

Zamindari Exploitation

Under the Permanent Settlement, zamindars extracted crushing rents. Santhals, unfamiliar with written contracts, found their customary land rights erased. Revenue demands soared, and the forests they relied upon were declared government property.

💰

Moneylenders (Dikus)

The mahajans lent money at extortionate rates (50–200% annually). Illiterate Santhals were tricked into signing bonds they couldn't read, and debts were inherited across generations, creating a cycle of bondage.

🇬🇧

Colonial Administration

The British courts, police, and revenue officials sided with zamindars and moneylenders. Santhal petitions were routinely rejected. Their customary laws were disregarded, and the colonial state became the instrument of their oppression.

📜

Intermediaries & Fraud

Middlemen used forged documents, false weights and measures, and illegal cesses (abwabs) to extract more from cultivators. The Santhals' lack of literacy made them especially vulnerable to manipulation.

🌲

Loss of Forest & Land

The British reserved forests and banned traditional shifting cultivation (jhum). Santhals were displaced from ancestral lands, losing not just livelihood but cultural and spiritual ties to their territory.

🔍

Denial of Justice

Courts were distant, expensive, and conducted in languages Santhals did not speak. When they presented their grievances at Bhagalpur in 1855, the Collector dismissed them. The Hul became a last resort.

🗡️ The Murmu Brothers & Allies

Leaders of the Hul

SM

Sidhu Murmu

Principal Leader · Visionary

Sidhu claimed divine revelation: that Thakur (God) had commanded him to free the Santhals from the dikus. He proclaimed the end of Company rule and urged a return to self-governance under manjhi-pargana councils.

KM

Kanhu Murmu

Principal Leader · Strategist

Kanhu organised the military mobilisation. He led the march from Bhognadih to Calcutta, aiming to present the Santhals' grievances to Lord Dalhousie. His tactical leadership kept the uprising coordinated across hundreds of villages.

CM

Chand Murmu

Military Commander

Chand took command in battle, leading the Santhal forces at the engagements of Pirpainti and Kahalgaon. He was captured by the British in early 1856 and executed alongside his brothers.

BM

Bhairav Murmu

Military Commander

Bhairav was the fourth Murmu brother and a key battle commander. He coordinated attacks on zamindari agents and moneylender strongholds across the Rajmahal hills before his death in combat.

Ph

Phulo & Jhano Murmu

Sisters · War leaders

Sidhu and Kanhu's sisters Phulo and Jhano fought alongside them, leading women's contingents. They were captured and executed by the British — among the first known women martyrs of tribal resistance in India.

Leadership Network

The Hul was not a spontaneous riot but an organized movement. The Murmu brothers coordinated with manjhis (village headmen) and parganaits (regional leaders) across the Santhal region. Messengers carried sealed sal leaves as summons; drums signaled assembly points.

📅 Chronology

Timeline of the Hul

1793

Permanent Settlement introduced

Lord Cornwallis's Permanent Settlement in Bengal empowers zamindars, beginning the displacement of Santhal cultivators from their traditional lands.

1832

Santhals settle in Rajmahal hills

The British encourage Santhal migration to clear the jungle of Daman-i-Koh, creating a buffer against Paharia tribes. But soon, dikus follow.

1854

Grievances intensify

Santhal elders begin gathering testimonies of exploitation. Petitions to British magistrates are ignored or dismissed.

30 Jun 1855

Hul declared at Bhognadih

Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu gather 60,000 Santhals and declare rebellion. They vow to march to Calcutta to present their grievances to the Governor-General.

Jul 1855

Battle of Pirpainti

Santhals defeat a force led by the Darogha of Pirpainti. Zamindari agents flee the region; moneylenders' records are burned.

Aug 1855

Martial law imposed

The British declare martial law across the Santhal region. Army units armed with Enfield rifles are deployed against warriors wielding bows and arrows.

Nov 1855

Death of Sidhu Murmu

Sidhu is captured and hanged at Bhagalpur. Kanhu takes over leadership, vowing to continue the struggle.

Jan 1856

Death of Kanhu Murmu

Kanhu is captured while attempting to reach Calcutta. He is hanged alongside Chand and Bhairav at Bhagalpur.

Dec 1856

Santhal Parganas Act

The British create the Santhal Parganas as a non-regulation district, prohibiting the transfer of Santhal land to non-Santhals — a direct result of the Hul.

⚔️ Consequences

British Response & Suppression

Military Response

The British mobilized modern army regiments with muskets and artillery against Santhal warriors armed with bows, arrows, and spears. Major engagements at Narayanpur, Pirpainti, and Kahalgaon ended in brutal massacres — between 15,000 and 30,000 Santhals were killed.

Martial law was imposed from November 1855 to January 1856, allowing summary executions. Villages were burned and leaders hunted down.

Administrative Consequences

Despite the violent suppression, the Hul forced the British to acknowledge systemic injustice. The Santhal Parganas Act of 1856 created a separate administrative unit, with Deputy Commissioner W.G. Archer overseeing protections for Santhal land.

The Act banned the sale of Santhal land to outsiders and exempted the region from ordinary laws — a rare colonial admission that injustice had occurred.

🌟 Long-Term Significance

Legacy of the Hul

Inspiration for Tribal Movements

The Hul set the template for later tribal uprisings: the Kherwar movement, Birsa Munda's Ulgulan (1899), and the Tana Bhagat movement. It proved that organized tribal resistance could challenge imperial power.

Land Rights Precedent

The Santhal Parganas Act became a legal model for tribal land protection. It influenced the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908) and later constitutional safeguards like Schedule V of the Indian Constitution.

Cultural Memory

The Hul is commemorated annually on 30 June as Hul Diwas across Jharkhand. The site of Bhognadih is now a memorial. Sidhu-Kanhu-Bhairav-Chand University bears their names.

Jharkhand Statehood

The spirit of the Hul fed into the 20th-century Jharkhand movement. When Jharkhand became India's 28th state in 2000, the Murmu brothers were hailed as its foundational heroes.

Related Tribal Movements

The Hul connects to broader patterns: the Kol Uprising (1831), the Ho revolt, the Munda Ulgulan, and the Alluri Sitarama Raju Rampa rebellion (1922–24).

National Symbol

In 2002, India Post issued a commemorative stamp honouring the Santhal Hul. The Hul is now taught as one of India's earliest anti-colonial uprisings, predating the 1857 Revolt by two years.
