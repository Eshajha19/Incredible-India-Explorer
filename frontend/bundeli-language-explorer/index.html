✒️ Writing system

Devanagari script

Bundeli is written in Devanagari, the same script as Hindi and Sanskrit. Historic Bundeli land records, treaties, and manuscripts — including correspondence attributed to regional rulers — were also composed in Devanagari, giving the region a long documentary tradition.

🗺️ Classification & region

Where Bundeli fits

Language family

Indo-European→ Indo-Iranian→ Indo-Aryan→ Western Hindi→ Bundeli

A descendant of Sauraseni Apabhramsha, Bundeli was classified under the Western Hindi group by linguist George Abraham Grierson in his Linguistic Survey of India. It is closely related to Braj Bhasha, once the leading literary language of north-central India, and neighbours Bagheli, Kannauji, and Malvi.

The Bundelkhand region

Bundelkhand spans southern Uttar Pradesh and northern Madhya Pradesh, roughly bounded by districts including:

  • Jhansi, Banda, Hamirpur, Mahoba, Lalitpur, Chitrakoot (UP)
  • Sagar, Damoh, Panna, Chhatarpur, Tikamgarh, Datia (MP)

Census figures often merge Bundeli speakers with Hindi, so the ~5.6 million counted natively in 2011 is considered an undercount by regional researchers.

🎭 Folk & literary heritage

A language of ballads and forts

Alha-Khand epic

The earliest known Bundelkhandi literature: heroic verses about the 12th-century warriors Alha and Udal, still preserved and sung by Banaphari bards across the region.

Rai & Faag folk traditions

The Rai folk dance and Faag (Holi) songs remain central to Bundelkhand's festival calendar, sung in Bundeli verse passed down through generations of village performers.

Court literature

Formal Bundeli literary works date from the reign of Emperor Akbar; the poet Keshavdas (16th century) and later Padmakar Bhatt (19th century) are among its best-known writers.

Bundela forts

Orchha and Jhansi's terracotta-red forts, built under Bundela rule, remain the region's most visible cultural landmarks and a backdrop to much of Bundeli's oral history.

Bundeli is not officially recognised in the Eighth Schedule of the Indian Constitution, and census returns are frequently merged with Hindi — regional linguists and cultural groups continue to campaign for its formal recognition, which makes documentation projects like this one part of active conservation.
