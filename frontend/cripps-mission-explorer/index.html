📜 War Cabinet to Quit India · March – August 1942

The Cripps Mission & the Road to Quit India

In March 1942, with Singapore fallen and a Japanese invasion of India feared, the British War Cabinet sent Sir Stafford Cripps to negotiate India's wartime cooperation. Ten days later, talks had collapsed — and the failure set the freedom struggle on its final, uncompromising course.

Why Britain Came to the Table

A Mission Born of Crisis

By early 1942, the war had turned sharply against Britain in Asia. Singapore had fallen in February and Rangoon soon after, bringing Japanese forces to India's eastern doorstep. The Allies — the United States, the Soviet Union, and China — were pressing Winston Churchill's government to secure India's full cooperation in the war effort, something increasingly difficult without a political concession to Indian self-rule.

Churchill, long opposed to Indian independence, reluctantly agreed to send Sir Stafford Cripps — a Labour politician and War Cabinet member known for his sympathy to Indian aspirations — to negotiate directly with Congress, the Muslim League, and other Indian political leaders. Cripps arrived in Delhi on 22 March 1942 and announced his proposals on 30 March.

🕰️ At a Glance

  • Mission arrives: 22 March 1942, led by Sir Stafford Cripps.
  • Proposals announced: 30 March 1942.
  • Congress rejects proposals: Working Committee resolution, early April 1942.
  • Quit India Resolution: 14 July 1942, movement launched 8 August 1942.
🎩
The Envoy

Sir Stafford Cripps

A senior Labour politician and Leader of the House of Commons, Cripps sat in Churchill's coalition War Cabinet despite his party's traditional sympathy for Indian self-government. He drafted the proposals largely himself, and arrived believing a genuine settlement was within reach. His personal standing with Congress leaders — built over years of correspondence — briefly raised hopes that this mission, unlike earlier British overtures, might succeed. Those hopes did not survive the fortnight.

What Was On the Table

The Two Core Proposals

Cripps's draft declaration rested on two linked offers — both conditional on the war ending first.

Proposal One

Dominion Status After the War

India would become a self-governing Dominion, free to decide its own relationship with the Commonwealth and to join the United Nations as an equal — but only once hostilities ended, with no fixed timetable in the meantime.

Why it fell short: Congress wanted immediate self-government, not a promise deferred to an uncertain future. Gandhi is widely remembered for dismissing it as "a post-dated cheque on a crashing bank."
Proposal Two

An Elected Constituent Assembly

After the war, an assembly — elected by provincial legislatures, with princely states nominating their own representatives — would frame India's new constitution. Any province unwilling to accept it could opt out and negotiate a separate arrangement.

Why it fell short: Congress saw the opt-out clause as an invitation to break up the country, and objected to unelected princely nominees. The Muslim League wanted an explicit guarantee of Pakistan, not an indirect opening.

How Ten Days Changed the Freedom Struggle

Filter the chronology by phase, or read straight through from crisis to collapse.

Feb 1942 Cause

Singapore Falls

The fall of Singapore, followed by Rangoon, brings the Japanese advance to India's border and forces Britain to seek genuine Indian cooperation in the war.

22 Mar 1942 Negotiation

Cripps Arrives in Delhi

Sir Stafford Cripps lands in India to open direct talks with Congress, the Muslim League, and other political leaders.

30 Mar 1942 Negotiation

Proposals Announced

The draft declaration — Dominion Status after the war, plus an elected Constituent Assembly — is published for Indian leaders to consider.

Early Apr 1942 Failure

Congress and the League Reject the Offer

The Congress Working Committee rejects the proposals as vague and incomplete; the Muslim League rejects them for not explicitly guaranteeing Pakistan. Cripps returns to Britain without an agreement.

14 Jul 1942 Consequence

The Quit India Resolution

Convinced that a negotiated settlement is no longer possible, the Congress Working Committee passes the Quit India Resolution, demanding an immediate end to British rule.

8 Aug 1942 Consequence

"Do or Die"

Gandhi delivers his Quit India speech at Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay, launching a mass movement. Within hours, the British arrest nearly the entire Congress leadership.

⚖️ Why the Mission Failed

  • No immediate transfer of power — everything was conditional on the war ending first, with no fixed date.
  • Defence remained British-controlled — Congress wanted a real share of wartime authority, not a promise for later.
  • The opt-out clause divided opinion — Congress feared it would fracture the country; the League felt it stopped short of guaranteeing Pakistan.
  • Deep mutual distrust — years of broken British promises left Indian leaders unwilling to accept assurances without immediate substance.
From Breakdown to Uprising

The Mission's Real Legacy

The Cripps Mission's collapse convinced Gandhi and the Congress leadership that further negotiation with Britain was futile while the war continued. With Japanese forces advancing and no credible offer of immediate self-rule on the table, Gandhi concluded that only British withdrawal could make India safe — and that the time for compromise had passed.

That conviction became the Quit India Resolution of July 1942 and the mass movement Gandhi launched in August, with the call to "Do or Die." The British response — mass arrests of the Congress leadership within hours of the speech — is widely described as one of the most serious challenges to British rule in India since the revolt of 1857, and the final turning point on the road to independence in 1947.

🔗 What Changed

  • Trust collapsed: Indian leaders stopped expecting good-faith offers from wartime Britain.
  • Congress radicalised: Negotiation gave way to demands for immediate withdrawal.
  • Mass arrests: Nearly the entire Congress leadership imprisoned within hours of Quit India.
  • Point of no return: The last major attempt at a negotiated wartime settlement before 1947.
