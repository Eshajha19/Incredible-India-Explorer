INA Red Fort Trials Explorer

The court-martial of three INA officers at the Red Fort became a turning point in India's freedom struggle — uniting political opinion across party lines and igniting nationwide protest.

Background

From the end of the war to the courtroom

Japan's surrender in August 1945 ended the Indian National Army's fight, but not its story. As INA personnel were captured or surrendered across Southeast Asia, the British government in India faced a fateful choice — one that would reshape the final phase of the independence movement.

End of the War, Capture of the INA

With Japan's defeat, tens of thousands of INA soldiers were taken into custody by advancing Allied forces across Burma, Singapore, and Malaya, and repatriated to India for processing.

The Decision to Prosecute

The British government chose to court-martial INA officers for offences including "waging war against the King-Emperor" — a decision meant to reassert authority but one that badly misjudged public sentiment.

The Red Fort as Venue

Delhi's Red Fort, the historic seat of Mughal power, was chosen as the trial venue — a symbolic setting that only sharpened the trials' resonance with the independence movement.

The Defendants

The three officers of the first INA trial

The first and most prominent court-martial, beginning in November 1945, tried three INA officers together — deliberately drawn from three different faiths to underline the INA's claim to represent a united India.

Shah Nawaz Khan

INA Officer · Punjab

A former British Indian Army officer who joined the INA and rose to command troops on the Burma front. He faced charges of waging war against the King-Emperor.

Prem Kumar Sahgal

INA Officer · United Provinces

A close aide to Subhas Chandra Bose within the Azad Hind command structure, tried alongside Khan and Dhillon in the same joint court-martial.

Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon

INA Officer · Punjab

An INA officer whose joint trial with Khan and Sahgal became known simply as "the Red Fort Trial," the case that galvanized public opinion nationwide.

Charges & Legal Proceedings

The case against the officers

The three officers were charged under the Indian Army Act with "waging war against the King-Emperor," alongside charges of murder and abetment of murder relating to the INA's own internal discipline in the field. The proceedings opened at the Red Fort on 5 November 1945 before a military court.

The trial drew enormous public and press attention from its first day, with daily reporting turning legal arguments over sovereignty, allegiance, and wartime necessity into a national conversation about the legitimacy of the freedom struggle itself.

Trial Facts

  • Proceedings opened on 5 November 1945 in the Red Fort's Diwan-i-Aam.
  • Charges included waging war against the King-Emperor.
  • The trial was the first of several INA court-martials held in 1945–46.
Defence Team

A defence that united India's political leadership

The Indian National Congress organised the INA Defence Committee, assembling one of the most formidable legal teams of the era to defend the officers.

Bhulabhai Desai

Lead Defence Counsel

A senior Congress leader and lawyer who led the defence, arguing the case for the INA's legitimacy as a wartime national army.

Jawaharlal Nehru

Defence Counsel

Donned legal robes for the first time in over two decades to join the defence, underscoring the trial's political weight.

Tej Bahadur Sapru

Defence Counsel

A veteran constitutional lawyer and statesman who lent his standing and expertise to the defence committee.

Public Protests

A nation responds

Student Participation

Students across Delhi, Calcutta, and Bombay organised marches, strikes, and "INA Day" observances in solidarity with the accused officers.

Worker Participation

Trade unions and industrial workers joined demonstrations and hartals, linking the INA cause to wider economic and political grievances.

Nationwide Reach

Protests and public meetings spread well beyond Delhi, reaching Calcutta, Bombay, and towns across the subcontinent through late 1945 and into 1946.

Timeline

The trials and their aftermath

Jan 1946Facing continued mass protest and pressure from Indian political leaders, the British Commander-in-Chief remits the sentences and the officers are released.
1946Further INA trials proceed on a smaller scale, but public pressure leads to reduced sentences and releases in most cases.
Political Reactions

A rare moment of political unity

The trials drew support from across India's political spectrum — the Congress, the Muslim League, and other parties that were otherwise deeply divided found common cause in defending the INA officers, framing the case as a matter of national honour rather than partisan politics.

The British government's response evolved under this pressure: initial firmness gave way to remission of sentences as the risk of wider unrest — including disaffection within the British Indian Army itself — became impossible to ignore.

Why It Mattered

The Red Fort Trials showed the British government that the INA's wartime challenge had not ended with Japan's surrender — it had been transformed into a unifying political symbol that reduced Britain's room to govern India by force alone.

Verdict & Consequences

Impact on the independence movement

Although convicted, the three officers were released within weeks amid overwhelming public pressure — a rare instance of the colonial government reversing a major legal decision in the face of mass mobilisation.

Historians widely regard the trials as a catalyst that accelerated the final phase of the independence movement: they helped spark the RIAF revolt of January 1946 and the Royal Indian Navy Mutiny weeks later, and hardened the British government's assessment that continued rule in India had become untenable.
