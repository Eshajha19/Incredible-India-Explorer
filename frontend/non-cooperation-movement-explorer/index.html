🇮🇳 Mass Movement · 1920–1922

Non-Cooperation Movement

India's first truly mass movement under Gandhi — a nationwide campaign of boycott, non-cooperation, and khadi promotion that mobilised millions across every region, religion, and class, and transformed Indian politics forever.

📜 Context

The Aftermath of Jallianwala Bagh

The Political Storm of 1919

The year 1919 had left India seething. The Rowlatt Act, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre, the martial law atrocities in Punjab, and the Hunter Commission's whitewash had convinced Indians that British rule could not be reformed — it had to be resisted.

Gandhi, who had suspended the Rowlatt Satyagraha in April 1919, spent the next year rebuilding the movement. He toured Punjab, visited Jallianwala Bagh, and concluded that constitutional methods were finished. What was needed was a mass movement.

The Montagu-Chelmsford Disappointment

The Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms) had introduced limited "dyarchy" — partial Indian control of provincial subjects. The Congress, under Gandhi's growing influence, rejected the reforms as inadequate. The moderates accepted them; the nationalists boycotted the 1920 elections.

The British had promised self-government in return for Indian war support — but delivered only a half-measure. This disillusioned even the most loyal Indians and prepared the ground for non-cooperation.

☪️ Khilafat

The Khilafat Movement Connection

The Ottoman Caliphate Issue

After WWI, the victorious Allied Powers planned to dismember the Ottoman Empire and abolish the institution of the Caliphate (Khilafat). For Indian Muslims, the Caliph was the spiritual head of the worldwide Muslim community, and the proposed abolition was seen as an attack on Islam itself.

The Ali brothers — Maulana Muhammad Ali and Maulana Shaukat Ali — launched the Khilafat Movement to pressure the British. They approached Gandhi for support, and Gandhi saw an opportunity to cement Hindu-Muslim unity.

The Hindu-Muslim Alliance

Gandhi threw the Congress behind the Khilafat cause. He made non-cooperation the common programme for both Khilafat and Indian political grievances. This was a masterstroke — it united Hindus and Muslims in a common movement for the first time on such a scale.

The Khilafat-Conference at Allahabad in June 1920 gave Gandhi a mandate to launch non-cooperation. The Muslim League also endorsed the movement. For a brief moment, India seemed genuinely united across religious lines.

🎯 Strategy

Gandhi's Strategy

Progressive Non-Cooperation

The movement was to be launched in stages — first, surrender of titles and honorary offices; then, resignation from government service; then, boycott of courts and schools; finally, refusal to pay taxes. Each stage escalated the pressure.

Constructive Programme

Alongside boycotts, Gandhi insisted on constructive work: spinning khadi, promoting Hindu-Muslim unity, abolishing untouchability, setting up national schools (Vidyapiths). He believed Indians must build the institutions of swaraj while destroying colonial ones.

Strict Non-Violence

Gandhi made non-violence an absolute condition. Any violence would be grounds for suspending the movement. He believed that non-violent non-cooperation was more powerful than armed resistance because it exposed the moral bankruptcy of the state.

Mass Mobilisation

For the first time, the movement explicitly targeted peasants, workers, students, and women — not just the educated elite. Gandhi toured the country speaking in local languages, drawing millions into politics for the first time.

Congress Endorsement

At the Calcutta special session (September 1920) and the Nagpur session (December 1920), the Congress formally adopted non-cooperation. The goal was redefined as "swaraj" (self-rule) to be achieved by "peaceful and legitimate means."

The Promise of Swaraj

Gandhi famously promised swaraj within one year if the movement was carried through with discipline and non-violence. This bold promise galvanised millions but also set an impossible deadline that would later haunt him.

🚫 Boycott

Boycott of Institutions & Foreign Goods

Institutional Boycott

Students left government schools and colleges in thousands. Over 800 national schools and colleges (Vidyapiths) were set up, including the Gujarat Vidyapith, Bihar Vidyapith, Kashi Vidyapith, and Jamia Millia Islamia in Delhi.

Lawyers gave up their practices. Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das, Asaf Ali, and Rajendra Prasad abandoned lucrative legal careers. Courts were boycotted, and parallel "people's courts" were set up to settle disputes.

Foreign Goods Boycott

Foreign cloth was picketed and publicly burned. The sale of British mill cloth fell by over 50% between 1920 and 1922. Liquor shops were picketed. The boycott was remarkably effective — it hit the British economy where it hurt most.

Khadi became the uniform of the movement. Spinning charkhas became a political symbol. Every Congress member was expected to spin a prescribed amount of yarn daily. Khadi transformed from a simple cloth into a symbol of national identity.

Before vs. During the Movement

Before (1919)

  • British cloth imports: ₹100 crore/year
  • Government school enrolment: 9.5 million
  • Court cases in British courts: normal
  • Titles and honours widely accepted

During (1921)

  • British cloth imports: ₹45 crore/year
  • 800+ national schools with 100,000+ students
  • Lawyers boycotting courts; people's courts set up
  • Titles returned; honours rejected

👥 Leaders

Major Leaders of the Movement

MG

Mahatma Gandhi

Supreme Leader

The architect of the movement. Gandhi toured the country mobilising millions, setting the programme of non-violent non-cooperation and suspending it after Chauri Chaura.

AN

Motilal Nehru

Congress President

Gave up a flourishing legal practice at the peak of his career. As Congress President, he steered the organisation through the movement and was imprisoned multiple times.

CR

C.R. Das

Bengal Leader

Known as Deshbandhu, Das resigned his legal practice and led the movement in Bengal. He later broke with Gandhi to form the Swaraj Party (1923) to enter the councils.

MA

Muhammad Ali Jauhar

Khilafat Leader

Along with his brother Shaukat Ali, he led the Khilafat wing of the movement. His fiery oratory drew millions of Muslims into the struggle. Imprisoned for most of the movement.

RP

Rajendra Prasad

Bihar Leader

Gave up his legal practice and led the movement in Bihar. Later became India's first President. His Champaran roots made him a natural Gandhian lieutenant.

JB

J.B. Kripalani

Sindh & UP

Active in Sindh and the United Provinces. A close Gandhian associate from the Champaran days, he organised constructive work and later became Congress President (1946).

CR

C. Rajagopalachari

Madras Leader

Led the movement in the Madras Presidency. Known as Rajaji, he was Gandhi's closest southern associate and later became India's first Indian Governor-General.

SP

Sardar Patel

Gujarat Organiser

Organised the movement in Gujarat. The Bardoli satyagraha (1928) would build on this foundation. Patel's organisational skills were crucial to the movement's infrastructure.

📅 Timeline

Major Phases (1920–1922)

1 Aug 1920

Movement Launched

Gandhi launches the Non-Cooperation Movement on 1 August 1920, the day Bal Gangadhar Tilak died. He returns the Kaiser-i-Hind medal awarded by the British for his war service.

Sep 1920

Calcutta Special Session

The Congress special session in Calcutta, presided over by Lala Lajpat Rai, formally adopts the non-cooperation programme. Gandhi's strategy wins over the party.

Dec 1920

Nagpur Session

The regular Congress session at Nagpur confirms the programme. The goal is redefined as swaraj. The Congress constitution is rewritten to allow mass membership (4 annas fee).

1921

Peak of the Movement

Mass participation across India. Students leave schools, lawyers leave courts, foreign cloth is burned. Khadi becomes the movement's uniform. The Prince of Wales' visit is boycotted with empty streets.

Jan 1922

Mass Arrests

Gandhi and other leaders are arrested in large numbers. The movement continues under local leaders. The British respond with repression but cannot stem the tide.

5 Feb 1922

Chauri Chaura

Violence at Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur district (UP) — protesters set fire to a police station, killing 22 policemen. Gandhi is horrified and decides to suspend the movement.

12 Feb 1922

Movement Withdrawn

Gandhi formally withdraws the movement at Bardoli, citing the people's failure to maintain non-violence. The Congress Working Committee reluctantly agrees.

🔥 Chauri Chaura

The Chauri Chaura Incident

The Incident

On 5 February 1922, a group of protesters in Chauri Chaura (a small town in Gorakhpur district, Uttar Pradesh) clashed with police. The police fired on the crowd, wounding several protesters. In retaliation, the crowd set fire to the police station where the constables had taken shelter.

Twenty-two policemen — including the inspector — were burned alive or hacked to death as they tried to escape the flames. The incident horrified Gandhi, who had always insisted on strict non-violence.

The Consequence

Five days later, on 12 February 1922, Gandhi withdrew the entire movement. He called Chauri Chaura "the last straw" and said the people were not yet ready for mass civil disobedience. He undertook a five-day fast as penance.

The withdrawal shocked the nation. Young nationalists like Nehru were bitterly disappointed; revolutionary terrorists like Bhagat Singh saw it as a betrayal. The incident would haunt Gandhi for the rest of his life.

⛔ Withdrawal

The Government Response & Withdrawal

British Response

The British responded with mass arrests, censorship, and repression. Over 30,000 satyagrahis were imprisoned during the movement. The press was censored, and nationalist publications were banned. But the government could not crush the movement by force — it had become too widespread.

The British also tried conciliation: the Viceroy promised further reforms. Gandhi rejected these offers, insisting on swaraj. The government's dual strategy of repression and half-measures failed to halt the movement.

The Bardoli Resolution

On 12 February 1922, the Congress Working Committee met at Bardoli (Gujarat) and reluctantly agreed to withdraw the movement. The resolution declared that civil disobedience could not be carried on without violence and therefore must be suspended.

The withdrawal was a massive anti-climax. Many Congress leaders — C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, Subhas Bose — opposed the decision. They formed the Swaraj Party in 1923 to enter the councils and continue the struggle from within.

🌟 Impact

Long-Term Impact on Indian Politics

Mass Politics Arrives

For the first time, peasants, workers, students, and women became active political agents. The movement transformed Congress from an elite body into a mass organisation. Politics would never be the same again.

Hindu-Muslim Unity Peak

The Khilafat-Non-Cooperation alliance produced unprecedented Hindu-Muslim unity. Muslims and Hindus marched, were imprisoned, and were beaten together. This unity would not be matched again until 1947.

Khadi as Identity

Khadi became a permanent part of Indian political identity. Even after the movement ended, the charkha remained the Congress symbol. Khadi defined nationalist dress for generations.

Leadership Pipeline

The movement launched the careers of a generation of leaders — Nehru, Patel, Rajendra Prasad, Kripalani, Rajaji, and dozens more who would lead India to independence in 1947.

Revolutionary Resurgence

The withdrawal disillusioned many young people, driving them toward revolutionary terrorism. The HRA, later HSRA, and revolutionaries like Bhagat Singh and Chandrashekhar Azad emerged directly from this disillusionment.

Blueprint for Future Movements

The Non-Cooperation template — mass mobilisation, boycott, constructive work, non-violence — was used again in Civil Disobedience (1930) and Quit India (1942). Every future movement built on 1920-22.
