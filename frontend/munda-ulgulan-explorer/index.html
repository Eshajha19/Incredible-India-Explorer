🌾 Munda World

Munda Society Before the Ulgulan

Khuntkatti System

The Mundas had a unique khuntkatti system of joint tribal land ownership. Entire village communities held collective rights over land, forests and water — no individual could alienate land to outsiders. The munda, manki and parha councils governed this system for centuries.

Sacred Groves & Sarna

The Mundas followed the Sarna faith, worshipping Singbonga (the Sun God) in sacred groves (sarna sthal). Their rituals, harvest festivals (Sarhul), and ancestor worship were deeply tied to the forest landscape.

Causes of the Movement

Cause → Resistance → Outcome

Land Alienation

British policies allowed zamindars and dikus to seize khuntkatti land, destroying the Munda economy.

→

Ulgulan

Birsa mobilises Mundas to attack dikus, burn records, reclaim land, and reject colonial authority.

CNT Act 1908

The Chotanagpur Tenancy Act is passed, protecting tribal land from sale to non-tribals.

Colonial Land Policies

The British introduced individual land settlements, replacing communal khuntkatti tenure. Outsiders (dikus) could now purchase tribal land, and Mundas lost their ancestral holdings.

Exploitation by Intermediaries

Zamindars, jagirdars and thikedars imposed illegal cesses (abwabs) and forced labour (begari). Mundas were reduced to tenants on their own land.

Missionary Activities

Christian missionaries gained converts by siding with colonial authorities. They encouraged abandonment of Munda customs and supported land alienation, dividing the community.

Cultural Erosion

Missionary schools taught Mundas to despise their traditions. The loss of cultural pride and social cohesion created a spiritual vacuum that Birsa sought to fill.

Forced Labour & Taxes

The colonial state imposed new taxes and forced labour demands (road building, forest clearance). Mundas had to work without pay, losing control of their time and land.

Legal Dispossession

British courts consistently upheld zamindari and moneylender claims. Illiterate Mundas could not contest forged documents, losing lands that had belonged to their families for generations.

Birsa Munda: The Leader

BM

Birsa Munda (1875–1900)

Dharti Aba · "Father of the Earth"

Born on 15 November 1875 in Ulihatu (Khunti district), Birsa grew up witnessing the exploitation of his people. Educated briefly at a German Mission school in Chaibasa, he left after seeing how missionaries collaborated with colonial authorities.

From 1893 onwards, Birsa emerged as a prophet and social reformer. He claimed visions from Singbonga, urging Mundas to return to their traditional faith, give up liquor, and resist the dikus. By 1895, he had thousands of followers and was briefly imprisoned.

After his release in 1897, Birsa's message radicalised into open rebellion. He declared the end of Queen Victoria's rule and the beginning of Munda Raj — a Munda kingdom governed by tribal law.

Religious Dimension

Birsa founded the Birsait faith, a purified form of Sarna religion that rejected missionary Christianity and colonial Hindu influences. He declared himself a prophet, performed miracles, and became the spiritual centre of the movement. The religious fervor gave the rebellion its deep emotional power.

Social Reform

Birsa attacked not just outsiders but internal corruption: liquor addiction, witchcraft practices, and caste-like divisions. He urged Mundas to purify their communities through abstinence, prayer, and solidarity — a moral foundation for political resistance.

📅 Chronology

Timeline of the Ulgulan

1875

Birth of Birsa

Birsa Munda is born on 15 November in Ulihatu village, Khunti. His early life exposes him to the exploitation of his people by dikus and colonial officials.

1886

Mission School at Chaibasa

Birsa enrolls at the German Lutheran Mission school in Chaibasa. He witnesses how missionaries collaborate with colonial authorities and leaves disillusioned.

1893

First visions

Birsa begins claiming divine revelations. He starts preaching a return to Sarna faith, abstinence from liquor, and resistance to dikus. His following grows rapidly.

1895

First arrest

Alarmed by Birsa's growing influence, the British arrest him on charges of inciting unrest. He is imprisoned for two years, which only increases his stature.

1897

Release and radicalisation

Birsa is released in 1897. His message now becomes openly anti-colonial: he declares the end of British rule and proclaims Munda Raj under his leadership.

Dec 1899

The Ulgulan begins

On Christmas Eve 1899, Birsa's followers attack churches, police stations, and zamindari offices across Chotanagpur. The Ulgulan ("Great Tumult") is now open warfare.

Jan 1900

Battle of Dumbari Hill

The British deploy troops with modern rifles against Munda warriors armed with bows, arrows, and swords. Hundreds of Mundas are killed in a one-sided battle.

Feb 1900

Arrest at Jamkopai

On 3 February 1900, Birsa is captured in the Jamkopai forest along with his close followers. He is taken to Ranchi jail.

Jun 1900

Death in Ranchi Jail

On 9 June 1900, Birsa Munda dies in Ranchi Jail under mysterious circumstances, officially from cholera. He is only 25 years old. His death ends the Ulgulan.

1908

Chotanagpur Tenancy Act

Eight years after Birsa's death, the British pass the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (CNT), prohibiting the transfer of tribal land to non-tribals. It is Birsa's greatest victory.

⚔️ The Uprising

British Response & Suppression

Military Crackdown

The British responded with overwhelming force. Modern rifles, cavalry, and artillery were deployed against Mundas armed with traditional weapons. The Battle of Dumbari Hill (January 1900) became a massacre, with over 400 Mundas killed.

Mass Arrests

After Birsa's capture, hundreds of his followers were arrested. 482 were put on trial, with 63 convicted and sentenced to long prison terms. The movement's leadership was dismantled.

Suppression of Birsait Faith

The British banned Birsait gatherings, destroyed sacred groves, and persecuted Birsa's followers. Yet the faith survived underground, and Birsa's legend grew in his absence.

Administrative Concessions

Despite the repression, the British recognised systemic injustice. The Chotanagpur Tenancy Act of 1908 and subsequent laws protected tribal land — a direct consequence of Birsa's sacrifice.

🌟 Long-Term Significance

Legacy of the Ulgulan

CNT Act 1908

The Chotanagpur Tenancy Act remains the cornerstone of tribal land protection in Jharkhand. It prohibits transfer of tribal land to non-tribals and is still invoked in court battles today.

Jharkhand Statehood

Birsa became the symbolic father of the Jharkhand movement. When Jharkhand was carved from Bihar on 15 November 2000 — his birthday — it was seen as the fulfilment of his dream of Munda Raj.

Janjatiya Gaurav Divas

Since 2021, Birsa's birthday (15 November) is celebrated nationally as Janjatiya Gaurav Divas — Tribal Pride Day — honouring his contribution to India's freedom struggle.

Related Tribal Movements

The Ulgulan connects to broader tribal resistance: the Santhal Hul (1855), Kol Uprising (1831), Tana Bhagat movement (1914), and Alluri Sitarama Raju's Rampa rebellion (1922).

Folk Legend

Birsa is revered across eastern India as a folk hero, celebrated in songs, plays, and oral traditions. He has become a symbol of tribal pride, self-respect, and resistance against exploitation.

Modern Politics

Birsa's legacy is claimed by political parties across the spectrum. The CNT Act remains politically sensitive, invoked in debates over mining, land acquisition, and tribal rights in Jharkhand.
