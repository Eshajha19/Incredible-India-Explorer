Daimabad: A Deccan Settlement Across Civilizations

On the left bank of the Pravara River in Ahmednagar (Ahilyanagar) district, Maharashtra, Daimabad preserves a continuous 5-metre cultural deposit spanning five Chalcolithic phases — and the chance 1974 discovery of a hoard of bronze sculptures that reopened the question of how far south Harappan influence reached.

Geographic Setting

A river-bank site on the Deccan's black-cotton plain

Daimabad sits on the left bank of the Pravara River, a tributary of the Godavari, in Shrirampur taluka of Ahmednagar district, Maharashtra — now part of the revenue village of Ladgaon.

The surrounding plain is heavy black-cotton soil, with a patch of shallow, coarse soil about 5 km away that would have supported pasture — a combination well suited to the mixed farming-and-herding economy archaeologists have reconstructed for the site.

Multi-Period Timeline · Settlement Chronology

Five phases, c. 2300–1000 BCE

Scroll to move through the sequence excavators identified in Daimabad's cultural deposit.

Phase IBefore c. 2300 BCE

Savalda culture

The earliest farming community: trilateral mud-walled houses, a surrounding village wall, black-on-red painted pottery, and early copper objects alongside stone tools.

Phase IIc. 2300–1800 BCE

Late Harappan culture

Ceramics resembling Indus styles, mud-brick structures, and terracotta button-seals carrying Indus script — the strongest evidence tying Daimabad to the wider Harappan world.

Phase IIIc. 1800–1600 BCE

Daimabad culture

A locally distinct black-on-buff/cream ware phase, following a occupation gap of roughly half a century after Phase II ends.

Phase IVc. 1600–1400 BCE

Malwa culture

Fine orange-slipped ware with channel-spouted bowls and high-necked storage jars; permanent farming villages become established across the region.

Phase Vc. 1400–1000 BCE

Jorwe culture

Daimabad reaches its peak of roughly 30 hectares, with a fortified mud wall, planned housing, and craft-specialist quarters before the site is abandoned.

Phase V

Jorwe — the settlement's peak

25 excavated houses across five structural sub-phases, with quarters identified for a butcher, lime-maker, potter, bead-maker and merchant, plus traces of a mud fortification wall with bastions. Of 48 burials found in this phase, 44 were urn burials.

Phase IV

Malwa — orange-slipped ware

Channel-spouted bowls and storage jars in a fine orange slip mark this phase, alongside an expanded farming repertoire of wheat, barley, pulses and lentils.

Phase III

Daimabad culture — a local recovery

Named for the site itself: a black-on-buff/cream ware distinct from both the Savalda and Late Harappan traditions that preceded it.

Phase II

Late Harappan — the Indus connection

Mud-brick structures and Indus-style black-on-red ceramics, plus two terracotta button-seals and three potsherds bearing Indus script symbols.

Phase I

Savalda — first farmers

Mud-walled trilateral houses with hearths and storage pits, protected by a surrounding wall; pottery painted with arrowheads, harpoons and animal figures.

Cultural transition: excavators identified a break in occupation of roughly half a century between the end of Phase II (Late Harappan) and the start of Phase III (Daimabad culture) — a gap, not a gradual blend.

Harappan-Related Evidence

How far south did the Indus world reach?

Ceramic and structural links

Phase II pottery is a painted black-on-red ware whose forms — including the dish-on-stand — closely echo Indus ceramic traditions, appearing alongside mud-brick structures rather than Phase I's simpler mud-wall construction.

Script and seals

Two terracotta button-seals and three potsherds bearing Indus script symbols gave the clearest confirmation of Harappan affinity, prompting decades of scholarly debate over whether Daimabad represents a genuine southward expansion of Indus civilization or a zone of strong indirect influence.

Chalcolithic Cultures

Five communities, five ways of living on the same mound

Savalda

Before c. 2300 BCE

Likely arose from contact between Mesolithic groups and Harappan communities in Saurashtra; used siliceous-stone beads and bone arrowheads alongside early copper.

Late Harappan

c. 2300–1800 BCE

The only site in the region with confirmed Late Harappan material — the basis for arguments that Indus influence extended into the Deccan.

Daimabad culture

c. 1800–1600 BCE

A short, locally distinctive phase named after the site itself, following the occupation gap noted above.

Malwa

c. 1600–1400 BCE

Part of a wider Malwa-culture horizon reaching across central India, arriving in the region around 1600 BCE.

Jorwe

c. 1400–1000 BCE

Named after the type-site of Jorwe; represented at some 200 sites across Maharashtra, with Daimabad and Inamgaon among the largest.

Agriculture · Crafts & Metallurgy

Farming and making, phase by phase

Agriculture

Savalda-phase farmers grew wheat, barley and lentil, and gathered Zizyphus (jujube). By the Malwa phase the range had expanded to include pisum, lathyrus and dolichos alongside the earlier staples.

Crafts & metallurgy

Savalda-phase toolkits combined copper bangles with chalcedony, agate and jasper microliths. By the Jorwe phase, craft had specialized enough for excavators to identify separate potter's, bead-maker's and lime-maker's houses — and metallurgy had advanced to the solid-cast bronze technique behind the famous hoard.

Trade · Regional Trade Map

An inland node in a wider exchange network

Daimabad had no metal or fine-stone sources of its own — its copper, chalcedony, agate, carnelian and steatite point to exchange with communities across the Deccan and beyond.

Later History

Abandonment and rediscovery

Occupation ends with the close of the Jorwe phase around 1000 BCE; the site was not resettled during the historic period, leaving its stratigraphy undisturbed for nearly three millennia.

Today the mound lies within the revenue village of Ladgaon. The 1974 bronze hoard is held at the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya (formerly the Prince of Wales Museum) in Mumbai; the chariot sculpture is recorded as being at the National Museum, New Delhi.
