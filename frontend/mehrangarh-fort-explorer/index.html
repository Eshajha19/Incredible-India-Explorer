🦁 Jodhpur, Rajasthan · Marwar

Mehrangarh Fort Explorer

Rising nearly 120 metres above the Blue City of Jodhpur, Mehrangarh — the "Fort of the Sun" — has guarded the Rathore rulers of Marwar since 1459, its ramparts, palaces and museum telling five centuries of royal history.

The Citadel Above the Blue City

An Introduction to Mehrangarh Fort

Mehrangarh Fort crowns a sheer sandstone hill in Jodhpur, Rajasthan, the capital of the former kingdom of Marwar. Founded in 1459 by Rao Jodha of the Rathore clan, the fort is one of the largest and best-preserved in India — a walled city of palaces, courtyards and galleries that has never been conquered by assault.

Its name means "Fort of the Sun", honouring the Rathores' claimed descent from the sun god Surya. Today Mehrangarh is as famous for its museum — one of India's finest — as for its architecture, drawing visitors from around the world to the heart of the Blue City.

A New Capital on the Rock

Rao Jodha and the Fort's Origins

In 1459, Rao Jodha of the Rathore clan moved his capital from Mandore to a new stronghold — and Jodhpur was born.

🏯

The Founding of Jodhpur

Rao Jodha selected the imposing rock of Bhakurcheeria — "the bird mountain" — for his new capital, laying the foundation of Mehrangarh on 12 May 1459. The city of Jodhpur grew up below the fort's walls and became the greatest commercial and political centre of western Rajputana.

🧙

The Hermit's Curse

Legend holds that the hill's resident hermit, Cheeria Nathji, was displaced by the construction and cursed the fort: "may your citadel suffer scarcity of water". Rao Jodha appeased him with a house and temple — yet the fort's wells have indeed always run dry, and water must be hauled up the hill to this day.

🪦

The Sacrifice of Raja Ram Meghwal

To secure the foundations, tradition records that a man named Raja Ram Meghwal volunteered to be buried alive in the works, and his sacrifice is still commemorated by a shrine within the fort — a reminder of the human cost medieval builders sometimes paid.

🛡️ Defences at a Glance

  • Walls: Ramparts up to ~36 metres high and ~21 metres thick in places.
  • Gates: Seven successive gateways guard the approach.
  • Jai Pol: Victory gate built in 1808 after defeating Jaipur.
  • Fateh Pol: Commemorates victory over the Mughals in 1707.
  • Dedh Kamgra Pol: Still bears cannonball damage from the 1808 siege.
  • Loha Pol: Iron-spiked gate marked by the handprints of sati queens.
Walls That Never Fell

Defensive Architecture

Mehrangarh's defences begin with geography: the fort sits on a cliff-edged hill that rises abruptly from the surrounding plain, making direct assault almost impossible. On top of this natural advantage, its builders raised massive sandstone ramparts — in places up to 36 metres high and 21 metres thick — that have never been breached by force.

Access is controlled by seven successive gates, each a separate defensive work: Jai Pol and Fateh Pol, built to commemorate victories; Dedh Kamgra Pol, whose walls still show the cannonball scars of the 1808 attack by Jaipur; and Loha Pol, the iron-studded inner gate, beside which the handprints of fifteen ranis who committed sati in 1843 are preserved in stone.

Palaces of the Rathores

Royal Palaces and Courtyards

Within the walls, successive maharajas built a sequence of palaces, each adding its own hall, court and private chambers to the citadel.

🫧

Moti Mahal — Pearl Palace

The great durbar hall of the fort, its painted ceiling and mirrored alcoves framing the royal audience chamber where the maharajas held court.

🌺

Phool Mahal — Flower Palace

The pleasure hall of the fort, decorated in gold leaf with painted flowers and scenes of courtly life — the most opulent interior at Mehrangarh.

🪞

Sheesh Mahal — Mirror Palace

A chamber lined with tiny mirrors set into painted plaster, designed to catch lamplight and glitter like stars within the stone walls.

👑

Takhat Vilas

The private apartments of Maharaja Takhat Singh, decorated with vivid wall paintings of his favourite pastimes — from hunting scenes to portraits of his consorts.

🚪

Zenana Deodi

The guarded quarters of the royal women, whose courtyards and screened balconies allowed the ranis to observe court life unseen.

🏺

Daulat Khana — Treasury

The fort's treasury halls, now part of the museum, once stored the wealth of Marwar — and today display its finest surviving objects.

A Palace Turned Museum

The Mehrangarh Museum and Collections

Opened to the public in 1972 by Maharaja Gaj Singh II, the Mehrangarh Museum is one of the finest in India, housed in the fort's own palaces and galleries. Its collections preserve the material world of the Marwar court: elephant howdahs and palanquins, royal cradles, miniature paintings, textiles, musical instruments and one of the world's great collections of turbans.

The armoury is among the most celebrated in the country, displaying swords, daggers, shields and firearms of the Rathore rulers, including weapons of the Mughal emperors and of Rao Jodha himself. Together, the galleries trace five centuries of Rathore patronage of art, craft and war.

🏺 Museum Highlights

  • Howdahs: Ornate elephant seats, including the gilt silver howdah of Maharaja Sawai Madho Singh.
  • Palanquins: Royal litters carried through the city on ceremonial occasions.
  • Armoury: Swords, daggers and firearms of the Rathore rulers and Mughal emperors.
  • Turbans: One of the world's largest collections of royal and regional turbans.
  • Paintings: Miniatures and murals spanning the Marwar school.
  • Textiles: Court costumes, embroideries and ceremonial cloth.
Art in Every Surface

Decorative Interiors

Beyond their scale, Mehrangarh's palaces are celebrated for the richness of their interiors — every ceiling, wall and window treated as a canvas.

🖌️

Painted Ceilings

Ceilings of the Moti Mahal and other halls are covered in dense painted ornament — flowers, birds and geometric patterns in the Marwar style.

✨

Gold Leaf and Mirror Work

The Phool Mahal glows with gold leaf, while the Sheesh Mahal's mirror mosaic turns lamplight into a field of stars.

🪟

Jharokhas and Jali Screens

Carved sandstone balconies and pierced lattice screens filter desert light into the royal chambers while keeping the interiors cool.

📜 Key Moments

  • 1459: Rao Jodha founds Mehrangarh and Jodhpur.
  • 1562–63: Rao Chandrasen holds the fort against Akbar's siege.
  • 1707: Victory over the Mughals, commemorated by Fateh Pol.
  • 1808: Jaipur's attack scars Dedh Kamgra Pol with cannonballs.
  • 1843: The sati handprints are set beside Loha Pol.
  • 1972: The museum opens to the public.
Five Centuries of Marwar

Historical Importance

Mehrangarh has been the stage for much of Marwar's history. In 1562–63, Rao Chandrasen defied the might of the Mughal emperor Akbar, holding the fort through a famous siege before being forced into exile — an episode that made him a hero of Rajput resistance. Later rulers made peace with the Mughals, and the fort became the seat of a powerful and wealthy court.

In the modern era the fort has taken on new life: the museum opened in 1972, the Jodhpur RIFF folk festival has filled its courtyards with music since 2007, and its ramparts have served as the backdrop for major films. Through it all, Mehrangarh remains the living symbol of Jodhpur and of Rathore pride.

Did You Know?

Interesting Facts

Never Conquered

Mehrangarh has never fallen to a direct assault — its cliff-top position and massive walls have kept it invincible for over 560 years.

The Fort of the Sun

"Mehrangarh" honours the Rathores' claimed descent from Surya, the sun god — the clan's emblem is the sun itself.

Water Must Be Hauled

True to the hermit's curse, the fort's wells run dry — water is still carried up the hill, as it has been for centuries.

Handprints of the Ranis

Fifteen handprints beside Loha Pol mark the ranis who performed sati in 1843, following their husband Maharaja Man Singh.

A World-Class Turban Collection

The museum holds one of the world's largest collections of turbans, from royal brocades to regional folk styles.

Hollywood's Fort

Mehrangarh's ramparts have appeared in major films, including The Dark Knight Rises, shot partly within the fort.
