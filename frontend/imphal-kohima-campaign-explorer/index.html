Background

The Burma Campaign and the road to India

By early 1944, World War II's Southeast Asian theatre had turned into a grinding contest for Burma. Japan's 15th Army, alongside Subhas Chandra Bose's Azad Hind Fauj (Indian National Army), planned a bold offensive across the Indo-Burma frontier — codenamed Operation U-Go — aimed at breaking into India itself.

INA–Japanese Alliance

Formed from Indian prisoners of war and expatriates in Southeast Asia, the INA fought under Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose's Azad Hind government, marching alongside Japan's 15th Army under Lieutenant General Renya Mutaguchi as a co-belligerent, not a subordinate force.

Campaign Objectives

The offensive aimed to seize the British supply base at Imphal and the hill town of Kohima, cut the road to Dimapur, and plant the Indian tricolour on Indian soil — opening a path for the INA's larger "Chalo Delhi" march.

Route to the Northeast

Forces advanced from Rangoon and Mandalay, crossed the Chindwin River near Homalin and Sittaung, and pushed through the Naga and Chin Hills toward Tamu, Ukhrul, Imphal, and Kohima.

Siege of Imphal

The battle for the Manipur plain

Imphal, the administrative and supply centre of Manipur, was the campaign's central objective. Japanese and INA columns converged on the town from the south and southwest through March and April 1944, cutting the Imphal–Kohima road and encircling the British-Indian IV Corps garrison.

Bishenpur, Palel, and the surrounding plain saw sustained fighting as the defenders held their perimeter through the monsoon's early weeks, supplied almost entirely by air while ground routes remained severed.

Imphal at a Glance

  • Siege ran roughly from March to June 1944.
  • The Imphal–Kohima road was the lifeline both sides fought to control.
  • Allied forces relied on airlifted supplies through the encirclement.
Battle of Kohima

The fight for the Kohima ridge

North of Imphal, Japanese forces pushed toward Kohima to sever the road linking Imphal to the railhead at Dimapur — a breakthrough that could have opened the way into the Brahmaputra valley. Fighting at Kohima centred on the Garrison Hill area, including the district commissioner's bungalow and tennis court, where opposing trenches lay only metres apart.

The battle, fought through April and into June 1944 in dense monsoon rain, is remembered as one of the fiercest engagements of the Burma Campaign and a turning point that halted the advance toward India's interior.

Kohima at a Glance

  • Fighting for Garrison Hill was fought at extremely close quarters.
  • The siege was broken by relief columns advancing from Dimapur.
  • Kohima and Imphal are jointly regarded as the campaign's decisive battles.
Terrain & Logistics

Monsoon, mountains, and a stretched supply line

Terrain

The route crossed the steep, densely forested Chin and Naga Hills — terrain that slowed columns, artillery, and pack transport to a crawl on narrow jungle tracks.

Monsoon

The southwest monsoon broke in May 1944, turning tracks to mud, flooding river crossings, and spreading malaria and dysentery through both armies' ranks.

Supply Lines

INA and Japanese forces advanced far ahead of their supply base, relying on captured stores and what could be carried overland — a shortfall that became critical once the siege lines stalled.

Jun 1944The Kohima siege is lifted and the Imphal–Kohima road is reopened, easing pressure on the encircled garrison.
Jul 1944Facing exhaustion, disease, and collapsed supply lines, Japanese and INA forces begin a costly retreat across the Chindwin.
Retreat & Consequences

Withdrawal and its cost to the INA

The retreat from Imphal and Kohima, conducted through monsoon-flooded terrain with dwindling supplies, proved as costly as the fighting itself. Disease, exhaustion, and starvation took a heavy toll on retreating columns.

For the INA, the failed offensive was a severe setback — it did not achieve the hoped-for breakthrough into India, and the force's fighting strength was significantly reduced. Even so, the campaign remained central to the INA's wartime narrative and its claim to have carried the fight for independence onto Indian soil.

Historical Significance

Imphal and Kohima are widely regarded by military historians as the point at which the Japanese advance into India was halted and reversed. For the INA, the campaign stands as the closest its forces came to crossing into India during the war — a moment invoked often in the movement's later memory.

References

Learn more

  • The Imphal–Kohima campaign (Operation U-Go, March–July 1944) was fought between Japanese/INA forces and Allied forces of the British Fourteenth Army along India's northeastern frontier.
  • The National Army Museum (UK) and Imperial War Museums document the Battle of Kohima and the Battle of Imphal as part of the wider Burma Campaign.
  • The Netaji Research Bureau, Kolkata, holds primary material on the INA's role in the 1944 offensive under the Azad Hind government.
  • Local memory in Manipur and Nagaland, including war cemeteries at Kohima and the INA memorial at Moirang, preserves the campaign's regional significance.
