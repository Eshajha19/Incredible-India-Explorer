🌧️ Bengal · 23 June 1757

Battle of Plassey Explorer

On a rain-soaked mango grove on the banks of the Bhagirathi, a small East India Company force under Robert Clive routed the vast army of Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah of Bengal. The victory owed less to musketry than to monsoon rains, and above all to the treachery of Mir Jafar. The Battle of Plassey unlocked the wealth of Bengal — and set the British Empire in India firmly on its course.

The Road to Palashi

The Richest Province, the Decisive Field

By the mid-eighteenth century, Bengal was the wealthiest province of the crumbling Mughal Empire — a vast cotton and silk emporium whose treasury funded everything around it. The English East India Company, entrenched in fortified trading posts at Calcutta, Madras and Bombay, traded there alongside French, Dutch and Danish rivals, and its fortune grew fat on Bengal's goods.

The young Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah, who succeeded his grandfather Alivardi Khan in April 1756, resented the Company's growing power, its fortification of Calcutta and its abuse of the trading privileges granted by earlier rulers. When the Company refused to comply with his demands, he marched on Calcutta and captured the city in June 1756 — an event immortalised (and exaggerated) in British memory as the 'Black Hole of Calcutta'. Robert Clive and Admiral Charles Watson recaptured Calcutta in January 1757 and, in a climate of mutual suspicion, the stage was set for a showdown that would decide who controlled Bengal.

🏛️ At a Glance

  • 23 June 1757 — Fought at Plassey (Palashi), on the Bhagirathi river, about 150 km north of Calcutta.
  • ~3,000 against ~50,000 — A small Company force faced the Nawab's vast army and artillery park.
  • Rain and Treachery — A monsoon downpour soaked the Nawab's powder, while Mir Jafar's army stood idle.
  • British Victory — Siraj-ud-Daulah fled; Bengal fell under Company control.
  • Birth of British Rule — The decisive first step toward the British Empire in India.
Forces in the Field

The Belligerents

A compact, disciplined and well-supplied Company army against the enormous but ill-led forces of the Nawab of Bengal.

⚓

British East India Company

  • Commander — Robert Clive
  • Strength — ~3,000 total (roughly 950 Europeans, ~2,100 Indian sepoys, ~100 gunners)
  • Artillery — ~10 field guns, plus naval guns from warships on the river
  • Advantages — Superior discipline, drilled volley fire, and dry gunpowder
🏹

Nawab of Bengal

  • Ruler — Siraj-ud-Daulah
  • Strength — ~50,000 soldiers (infantry, cavalry) with ~50 heavy cannon
  • Commanders — Mir Madan, Mohan Lal, Rai Durlabh, Mir Jafar (who did not fight)
  • Weaknesses — Rival factions within the army, wet powder, and divided loyalties

Why it mattered: Plassey was decided before a shot of the main action was fired. Clive had signed a secret treaty with Mir Jafar, commander of the Nawab's army, promising him the throne of Bengal in exchange for his betrayal. When the moment came, Mir Jafar's troops simply refused to fight.

Men Who Decided the Day

The Commanders

On one side a resolute imperial soldier and a treacherous general; on the other a young Nawab with a vast but divided army.

Company · Commander-in-Chief

Robert Clive

A former Company clerk turned brilliant soldier, Clive had captured Arcot in 1751 and recaptured Calcutta in 1757. At Plassey he gambled a handful of men against a kingdom — securing the conspiracy with Mir Jafar, weathering the Nawab's bombardment, and launching the decisive attack once the rain had crippled the enemy's powder. He later became Baron Clive of Plassey.

Nawab · Army Commander · Traitor

Mir Jafar

Mir Jafar Khan, commander-in-chief of Siraj-ud-Daulah's army, had long been courted by both the British and the French. In the secret treaty of 1757 he agreed to stand aside at Plassey in return for the Nawabship. He kept his word — and his soldiers did not fire a shot. Installed as Nawab after the battle, he remained a puppet of the Company.

Nawab of Bengal

Siraj-ud-Daulah

The young and impetuous Nawab of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, grandson of Alivardi Khan. His capture of Calcutta in 1756 brought the Company to war. At Plassey, after his loyal general Mir Madan was killed and Mir Jafar refused to engage, his vast army disintegrated. He fled to Murshidabad, was captured and put to death in July 1757 — the last independent Nawab of Bengal.

Nawab · Loyal General

Mir Madan

Siraj-ud-Daulah's most loyal commander, Mir Madan led a spirited counter-attack against the Company's position. He was struck down by a cannonball as he advanced. His death broke the Nawab's resolve and gave the traitors in his camp their moment — the turning point of the battle.

⚔️ The Verdict of Plassey

  • Decisive Company Victory — The Nawab's army broke and fled; the battle cost the Company barely two dozen lives.
  • Mir Madan Killed — Siraj's loyal general fell to cannon fire early in the action.
  • Siraj-ud-Daulah Deposed — Captured fleeing at Murshidabad, he was executed in July 1757.
  • Mir Jafar Installed — The traitor-general was placed on the throne as Nawab under Company influence.
The Treasure of Bengal

A Victory of Diplomacy as Much as Arms

The fighting at Plassey lasted only a few hours and was decided less by musketry than by the monsoon and by treason. Yet its consequences were immense. The Company, through Mir Jafar, secured the forts and mints of Bengal, the right to trade freely without duties, the zamindari of the Twenty-Four Parganas around Calcutta, and enormous indemnities paid from the Bengal treasury.

Within days the new Nawab's coffers yielded Clive and the Company a fortune. Bengal — the richest province in India — was now effectively in British hands, and its revenue would fund the conquest of the rest of the subcontinent.

The Long Shadow

Why Plassey Changed Everything

The consequences of one rainy afternoon stretched across two centuries of Indian history.

💰

Control of Bengal's Wealth

Plassey placed the revenues of the richest province at the Company's disposal. Bengal's taxes, trade and treasuries financed the wars, armies and diplomacy that carried British power across India.

👑

From Merchant to Master

For the first time the East India Company set and removed a ruler of an Indian state. Plassey transformed the Company from a trading corporation into a territorial power — a process completed at Buxar in 1764 and the Diwani of 1765.

🌏

The Road to Empire

By funding the conquests that followed, Plassey is widely regarded as the foundation of the British Empire in India. Over the next century, Company rule spread to become the British Raj, shaping modern India's politics, railways, cities and institutions.

Milestones

Chronology of the Battle of Plassey

From Siraj-ud-Daulah's seizure of Calcutta to the conspirators' reward on the throne of Bengal.

April 1756

Siraj-ud-Daulah Becomes Nawab

The young Siraj-ud-Daulah succeeds his grandfather Alivardi Khan as Nawab of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, inheriting a court riven by factions and resentful of Company power.

June 1756

The Fall of Calcutta

Angered by the Company's fortifications and defiance, Siraj-ud-Daulah besieges and captures Calcutta. British prisoners are held overnight — the 'Black Hole of Calcutta'.

January 1757

Calcutta Recaptured

Robert Clive and Admiral Charles Watson, sent from Madras, retake Calcutta and sign the Treaty of Alinagar with the Nawab, restoring the Company's privileges.

Spring 1757

The Conspiracy Forms

With war against France looming in Bengal, Clive negotiates a secret treaty with Mir Jafar — the Nawab's commander-in-chief — who agrees to desert Siraj in return for the throne. The merchant Omichand is drawn in and then deceived.

13–22 June 1757

The March to Plassey

Clive's army, joined by Mir Jafar's promises, marches from Chandernagore toward Murshidabad. On 22 June it crosses the Bhagirathi and takes position in the mango grove at Plassey.

23 June 1757 · morning

The Battle Opens

Siraj's army advances with massed artillery. The Company's small force huddles in the grove under bombardment; the Nawab's heavy guns fail to dislodge it.

23 June · afternoon

The Turning of the Tide

A sudden monsoon storm soaks the battlefield. The Nawab's troops and powder are drenched, while the British had protected their ammunition. Mir Madan is killed by a cannonball, and as the Nawab's morale collapses, Mir Jafar's divisions stand idle.

23 June · evening

The Rout

Clive launches his guns and infantry against the shaken enemy. The Nawab's army dissolves; Siraj flees on a fast camel toward Murshidabad. The Company loses barely two dozen men.

28 June – July 1757

The Throne Changes Hands

Mir Jafar is installed as Nawab of Bengal. Siraj-ud-Daulah is captured and executed. The Company secures the mints, forts, trade privileges and the Twenty-Four Parganas, and huge payments from the Bengal treasury.
