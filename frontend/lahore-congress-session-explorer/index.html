Lahore Congress Session & Purna Swaraj

On the banks of the Ravi, under Jawaharlal Nehru's presidency, the Congress declared Purna Swaraj — complete independence — as its goal, setting India on the road to Civil Disobedience.

Political Background

From Dominion Status to complete independence

By the late 1920s, the Congress's demand for Dominion Status — self-government within the British Empire — had run out of patience. The all-White Simon Commission of 1928, the ambiguous response to the Nehru Report's proposals, and a viceregal statement seen as evasive convinced a younger generation of leaders that nothing short of full independence would do.

Congress Debates

At the Calcutta session of December 1928, the Congress gave the British government a year's ultimatum to grant Dominion Status — after which it would launch a campaign for Purna Swaraj. Younger leaders like Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose pushed for immediate independence rather than a deadline.

Nehru's Presidency

Jawaharlal Nehru was elected Congress President for the Lahore session on Gandhi's recommendation — a deliberate handing of the movement's leadership to a younger, more radical generation as the independence demand hardened.

The Year's Deadline Expires

With the British government's response falling short of Dominion Status by December 1929, the Congress's self-imposed deadline lapsed — clearing the way for a formal declaration of complete independence at Lahore.

Purna Swaraj

The declaration of complete independence

On the night of 31 December 1929, on the banks of the Ravi, the Congress adopted a resolution declaring Purna Swaraj — complete independence — as its goal, formally abandoning the earlier demand for Dominion Status. Jawaharlal Nehru hoisted the tricolour flag of independence before the assembled crowd at midnight, marking the moment symbolically as well as politically.

The Congress Working Committee was authorised to launch a civil disobedience campaign, including non-payment of taxes, whenever it judged the moment right — laying the groundwork for the movement that would follow within months.

Purna Swaraj at a Glance

  • Resolution adopted on the night of 31 December 1929.
  • Replaced the demand for Dominion Status with complete independence.
  • Authorised the Congress Working Committee to launch civil disobedience.
26 January 1930

Independence Day observance

The Lahore session called on Congress committees across India to observe 26 January 1930 as "Independence Day," with public meetings pledging to work for Purna Swaraj. The observance drew large crowds in towns and villages nationwide, giving ordinary people a direct, participatory role in declaring independence as the movement's goal.

The date's symbolic power endured well beyond 1930 — independent India would later choose 26 January as the day its Constitution came into force in 1950, deliberately echoing the Lahore pledge.

Why 26 January

  • Chosen by the Lahore session as a nationwide pledge day for Purna Swaraj.
  • Observed with public meetings and flag hoistings across India in 1930.
  • Later adopted as India's Republic Day in 1950.
Nationalist Mobilization

Carrying the pledge to the country

Provincial Committees

Provincial and local Congress committees organised public meetings and flag hoistings to mark 26 January 1930, translating a resolution passed in Lahore into a nationwide event.

The Independence Pledge

Crowds at these meetings recited a pledge affirming the right to freedom and the wrongs of British rule, rehearsing arguments the Civil Disobedience Movement would soon put into action.

Preparing the Ground

The mobilisation gave the Congress organisational reach and public momentum it would draw on just weeks later, when Gandhi announced the Salt March.

Connection to Civil Disobedience

From resolution to movement

The Purna Swaraj resolution did more than restate a demand — it authorised the Congress Working Committee to launch mass civil disobedience whenever conditions were judged ready. That authorisation was exercised within months: Gandhi's Salt March in March 1930 opened the Civil Disobedience Movement, turning the Lahore pledge into direct nationwide action.

Historians regard the Lahore session as the hinge between the Congress's earlier constitutional strategy and the mass movements that defined the freedom struggle's final two decades.

Why It Mattered

Lahore reframed the goal of the national movement from securing better terms within the empire to ending colonial rule outright — a shift in language and ambition that shaped every Congress campaign that followed.
