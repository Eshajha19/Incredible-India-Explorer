🏴 Underground Administrations · 1942-1944

Parallel Governments of Quit India

When British repression drove the Quit India Movement underground in August 1942, ordinary Indians in a handful of regions didn't just protest colonial rule - they replaced it. From the deltas of Midnapore to the hill forts of Satara, local leaders ran their own courts, police, postal systems, and even currency, openly defying the Raj for months and, in Satara's case, years.

Roots of the Movement

Why Parallel Administrations Emerged

The "Do or Die" call of August 1942 was met with mass arrests of the entire Congress high command within hours. With no central leadership left to direct the movement, local organisers stepped into the vacuum.

🚨

Leadership Vacuum

Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, and the entire Congress Working Committee were arrested on 9 August 1942, leaving the movement leaderless at the national level and forcing regional cadres to organise independently.

📡

Collapsed Communication

With telegraph lines cut, railways sabotaged, and newspapers censored, regions that rose in revolt were effectively isolated - and had to govern themselves out of necessity, not just ideology.

🌾

Strong Local Networks

Areas like Midnapore and Satara already had dense networks of Congress volunteers, socialist organisers, and village-level sympathisers capable of quickly filling administrative gaps.

⚔️

Retreat of British Authority

In some rural pockets, British police and revenue officials were driven out or withdrew under pressure, leaving a genuine administrative gap that local Sarkars moved to fill.

🎯

A Deliberate Symbol

Beyond necessity, running a functioning "national government" - however small - was a powerful propaganda tool, proving Indians could administer themselves the moment British control lapsed.

🤝

Socialist & Gandhian Convergence

Many parallel governments blended Gandhian constructive-work ideals with the more militant, socialist-influenced tactics of underground leaders like Jayaprakash Narayan and Achyut Patwardhan.

Side by Side

Comparing the Two Major Sarkars

🌊 Tamralipta Jatiya Sarkar

  • Region: Tamluk subdivision, Midnapore, Bengal
  • Founded: December 1942
  • Key Leaders: Satish Chandra Samanta, Ajoy Mukherjee, Sushil Kumar Dhara, Matangini Hazra (martyred leading a procession)
  • Administration: Bidyut Vahini volunteer corps for law and order, parallel courts, agricultural and cyclone relief
  • Dissolved: September 1944, ahead of the release of national leaders

⛰️ Prati Sarkar, Satara

  • Region: Satara district, Maharashtra
  • Founded: 1943
  • Key Leaders: Nana Patil, Yashwantrao Chavan (early involvement), leaders of the "Toofan Sena"
  • Administration: Village-level "nyayadan mandals" (people's courts), local revenue collection, village committees
  • Dissolved: 1945, as the wider movement wound down after the war's end
Who Ran Them

Leadership Network

The parallel governments were held together by local organisers, many operating entirely underground.

🧑‍⚖️

Satish Chandra Samanta

President of the Tamralipta Jatiya Sarkar, coordinating its courts and relief operations from hiding while evading British capture for much of its existence.

🛡️

Nana Patil

Founder and central figure of the Satara Prati Sarkar, known for organising the "Toofan Sena" and mobile people's courts that dispensed swift local justice.

✊

Ajoy Mukherjee

A key organiser of the Tamralipta Sarkar's administrative machinery, later a prominent post-independence Bengal politician.

🎖️

Chittu Pandey

Led the short-lived provisional government at Ballia, freeing political prisoners before British forces retook the town within days.

🕊️

Matangini Hazra

A 73-year-old Gandhian who was shot and killed while leading a procession toward the Tamluk police station carrying the national flag - becoming a lasting symbol of the Midnapore uprising.

📢

Jayaprakash Narayan

Though based in the wider underground Congress network, JP's socialist organising style influenced coordination between scattered regional Sarkars, including support networks reaching Satara.

Rise and Fall

Timeline of the Parallel Governments

9 August 1942

Quit India Launched, Leaders Arrested

Gandhi's "Do or Die" call is followed within hours by the arrest of the entire Congress high command, leaving the movement without central direction.

19-22 August 1942

Ballia Rises

Chittu Pandey leads a takeover of Ballia town, briefly declaring a provisional government before British forces regain control.

September 1942

Midnapore Uprising and Repression

Mass protests sweep Tamluk and Contai; Matangini Hazra is killed leading a procession, hardening local resolve to organise independently.

December 1942

Tamralipta Jatiya Sarkar Formed

Local leaders formally establish the Jatiya Sarkar, launching volunteer policing, parallel courts, and relief work after a devastating cyclone.

1943

Prati Sarkar Established in Satara

Nana Patil and allied organisers set up village-level people's courts and administration across rural Satara, evolving into the movement's longest-lived Sarkar.

September 1944

Tamralipta Sarkar Dissolves

With British pressure mounting and national leaders' release anticipated, the Jatiya Sarkar formally winds down after nearly two years of parallel rule.

1945

Prati Sarkar Winds Down

As the Second World War ends and national political activity resumes, Satara's parallel administration is gradually folded back into the mainstream Congress movement.

Running a Shadow State

Administrative Activities

What set these movements apart from ordinary protest was governance. The Tamralipta Sarkar's "Bidyut Vahini" acted as both police and disaster-relief corps, coordinating flood and cyclone relief when the official administration had collapsed. It also ran informal courts that heard local disputes and delivered rulings without any reference to British law.

In Satara, the Prati Sarkar's "nyayadan mandals" (justice-giving councils) handled everything from land disputes to social offences at the village level, while volunteer squads collected revenue and enforced boycotts of British goods and courts - creating, in effect, a functioning shadow government across large parts of the district.

Crackdown

British Response

The colonial government treated these Sarkars as a direct challenge to its sovereignty. In Midnapore, British forces imposed collective fines, conducted punitive military operations, and were accused of using ordinary policing powers with extraordinary severity to break the Jatiya Sarkar's hold.

In Satara's more dispersed, rural terrain, repression proved harder to sustain - British forces struggled to permanently dislodge the Prati Sarkar's network, which is part of why it survived far longer than most other regional risings of 1942.

Wider Context

Related Quit India Events

The parallel governments were part of a much larger nationwide upheaval in 1942-44.

📻

Congress Radio

Usha Mehta's secret broadcast station kept the country informed of the movement's progress, including news from the parallel governments, until it was tracked down in November 1942.

🚂

Railway & Communication Sabotage

Widespread attacks on railway lines, telegraph wires, and police stations across India both triggered and sustained the conditions that allowed regional Sarkars to operate.

⛓️

Mass Arrests & Repression

Over 100,000 people were arrested nationwide during Quit India, with harsh reprisals in the very districts - Midnapore, Satara, Ballia - where parallel governments took root.

🕯️

Martyrs of the Movement

Figures like Matangini Hazra and Kanaklata Barua became rallying symbols whose sacrifice hardened local resolve to build independent administration rather than simply protest.

Why It Mattered

Historical Significance

The parallel governments of Quit India proved something no amount of protest alone could: that Indians were capable of self-governance the moment colonial authority lapsed, even at the village level and even under military pressure. Though geographically limited, Tamralipta and Satara's Sarkars demonstrated durable local institutions - courts, policing, and revenue systems - that functioned for months and, in Satara's case, years without British sanction.

Their legacy fed directly into post-independence local governance debates, and leaders who cut their teeth running these shadow administrations - including Nana Patil - went on to shape regional politics for decades afterward.
