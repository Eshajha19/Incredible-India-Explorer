📖 Historical Background

The City of Moon God's Fort

Location & Significance

Chandraketugarh is located at Berachampa in North 24 Parganas district, West Bengal, approximately 35 km northeast of Kolkata. The site sits on the banks of the Vidyadhari River, a tributary that once connected directly to the Bay of Bengal through a network of channels that have since silted up.

The name "Chandraketugarh" translates to "Fort of Chandraketu" (Moon-Bannered), possibly referring to a legendary king mentioned in regional folklore. Archaeological evidence suggests the site was continuously occupied from the 4th century BCE through the 12th century CE — an extraordinary span of 1,500 years.

The Gangaridai Connection

Greek and Roman sources describe the powerful Gangaridai kingdom in the Ganges delta region, whose war elephants reportedly deterred Alexander the Great from advancing further east. Chandraketugarh is widely identified by historians as one of the major urban centers of this kingdom.

Megasthenes' Indica (4th century BCE) describes a prosperous land with fortified cities, river ports, and sophisticated administration. The archaeological finds at Chandraketugarh — including ramparts, terracotta art, coins, and imported goods — match these ancient descriptions remarkably well.

🏺 Historical Periods

Fifteen Centuries of Civilization

4th–2nd BCE

Mauryan Period

Earliest settlement phase with Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) pottery. Fortification walls constructed. Urban planning evident. Part of the Mauryan empire's eastern trade network.

2nd BCE–2nd CE

Shunga-Kushana

Golden age of terracotta art. Exquisite plaques depicting daily life, deities, and royal processions. Trade connections with Roman empire evidenced by coin finds. Sophisticated drainage systems.

3rd–6th CE

Gupta Period

Continued prosperity under Gupta influence. Buddhist monasteries constructed. Gold coins of Kumaragupta and Skandagupta discovered. Temple architecture begins to emerge.

7th–12th CE

Pala-Sena Period

Buddhist monastery expanded under Pala patronage. Terracotta temple construction peaks. Gradual decline begins as river channels silt. Final abandonment by late 12th century.

🔍 Archaeological Discoveries

Unearthing an Ancient Metropolis

Major Excavations

Systematic excavations began in the 1950s under the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), with major campaigns in 1957, 1964-65, and subsequent decades. The site revealed a well-planned urban complex spanning multiple historical periods.

Key structural discoveries include a massive brick-built fortification wall with bastions, a Buddhist monastery complex (vihara), temple foundations, residential quarters with advanced drainage, and a sophisticated water management system with brick-lined wells and soak pits.

Significant Finds

  • Over 10,000 terracotta plaques and figurines — the largest collection from any Bengal site
  • Coins spanning Mauryan punch-marked to Pala copper issues
  • Roman amphorae shards and Mediterranean glass beads
  • Ivory seals with Brahmi inscriptions
  • Iron tools, copper implements, and bronze artifacts
  • Ceramic types from multiple cultural periods
💰 Trade Importance

Gateway of Eastern Commerce

🚢

River Port Function

Chandraketugarh served as a crucial inland river port connecting the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta to maritime trade routes. Ships could navigate from the Bay of Bengal via the Vidyadhari to reach this commercial hub, then continue upstream to Pataliputra and beyond.

📦

Export Commodities

Major exports included fine muslin textiles (for which Bengal was renowned), ivory products, pearls, tortoise shell, precious stones, timber, and agricultural produce. Bengal's famous cotton was particularly prized in Mediterranean markets.

🪙

Roman Trade

Direct trade with the Roman Empire is confirmed by finds of Roman coins (1st-2nd century CE), Mediterranean amphorae, and coral. Pliny complained about Roman gold draining eastward to purchase Indian luxuries — much flowed through Gangaridai ports.

🗺️

Regional Networks

Connected to Tamralipta (Tamluk) on the coast, Pataliputra (Patna) upstream, and Southeast Asian kingdoms. Part of the larger "Southern Silk Route" linking China, Burma, Bengal, and the Mediterranean world.

🏛️

Administrative Center

Seals and inscriptions reveal sophisticated administrative structure. Merchant guilds (sreni) operated from the city. Customs duties and trade taxes funded urban infrastructure including fortifications and public works.

⚓

Maritime Technology

Archaeological evidence indicates boat-building yards along the riverbank. Terracotta plaques depict various vessel types from small river boats to ocean-going ships, showing advanced naval architecture knowledge.

📅 Timeline

Chronological Development

4th BCE

Mauryan Foundation

Initial settlement established. Northern Black Polished Ware culture. Fortification walls constructed. Becomes part of the Mauryan empire after conquest of Bengal region.

3rd BCE

Megasthenes' Account

Greek ambassador to Chandragupta Maurya's court describes Gangaridai as a powerful kingdom with 4,000 war elephants. Chandraketugarh identified as a major urban center.

2nd BCE

Shunga Flourishing

Golden age of terracotta art begins. Exquisite plaques depicting scenes from daily life, mythology, and courtly entertainment. Trade with Southeast Asia expands.

1st CE

Roman Trade Peak

Direct maritime commerce with Roman empire reaches height. Roman coins, amphorae, and Mediterranean goods discovered in excavations. Prosperity evidenced by monumental architecture.

4th CE

Gupta Integration

Under Gupta imperial control. Buddhist monastery constructed. Gold coins of Kumaragupta and Skandagupta in circulation. Temple architecture begins.

8th CE

Pala Patronage

Buddhist monastery expanded under Pala dynasty. Terracotta temple construction peaks. City remains important regional center though river channels beginning to silt.

12th CE

Decline & Abandonment

River course changes and channel silting lead to economic decline. Gradual abandonment under Sena dynasty. Site eventually buried under alluvial deposits until modern rediscovery.

🏺 Notable Artifacts

Treasures from the Past

Terracotta Yakshi

Exquisite figurine of a fertility goddess with elaborate jewelry and headdress, dating to Shunga period (2nd century BCE). Represents the finest example of Bengal terracotta art and is displayed in the Indian Museum, Kolkata.

Royal Procession Plaque

Large terracotta plaque depicting a royal chariot procession with soldiers, elephants, and attendants. Provides invaluable evidence of Mauryan-era military organization and courtly ceremonial.

Seated Buddha Bronze

Rare bronze image of Buddha in dhyana mudra from Pala period (9th century CE). Shows sophisticated metal-casting techniques and artistic refinement characteristic of Pala Buddhism.

Ivory Seal Collection

Multiple carved ivory seals with Brahmi and later scripts, bearing merchant guild names and administrative titles. Critical evidence for understanding ancient commercial organization.

Roman Coin Hoard

Collection of 1st-2nd century CE Roman silver denarii of emperors Augustus, Tiberius, and Nero. Direct evidence of Indo-Roman maritime trade reaching deep into the Ganges delta.

Mother-and-Child Figurines

Numerous terracotta sculptures depicting mothers with children, representing domestic religion and fertility worship. Among the most beloved artifacts from the site.
