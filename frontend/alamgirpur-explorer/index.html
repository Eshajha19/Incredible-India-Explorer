01 — Overview

A river-side outpost at the edge of a civilisation

Locally known as Parshuram ka Tila (Parshuram's Mound), the site is small — roughly 60 by 50 metres, rising just 6 metres above the surrounding sugarcane and wheat fields — yet it rewrote assumptions about how far Harappan culture actually travelled.

Geography & river environment

Alamgirpur sits on the left bank of the Hindon River, a tributary of the Yamuna, within the upper Ganga–Yamuna Doab — a landscape of Quaternary alluvium quite unlike the Indus basin itself. In antiquity the surroundings were open grassland with limited wood cover, so inhabitants supplemented timber with other fuel sources. The Hindon–Yamuna corridor likely served as the physical link connecting this outpost back to the Harappan world to the west.

Discovery

First probed in 1958 by the Regional Camp Committee of the Bharat Sewak Samaj, which recovered pottery and beads. Y.D. Sharma of the Archaeological Survey of India then confirmed the site's Harappan affiliation and defined a four-fold cultural sequence — each period separated by a break in occupation. A 2008 re-excavation by Banaras Hindu University applied AMS radiocarbon dating, archaeobotany, and geoarchaeology to re-test that chronology.

Why it matters

Until Alamgirpur's excavation, scholars assumed the Harappan world was confined to the Indus Valley and its immediate surrounds. Finding unmistakably Harappan pottery, terracotta cakes and faience bangles across the Yamuna — hundreds of kilometres from the nearest known site — proved the civilisation's cultural and economic reach extended into the Ganga plains.

A revised timeline

Alamgirpur was originally filed away as purely "Late Harappan," which conveniently supported the old theory that Harappans declined in the west and migrated east. But the 2008 AMS dates push the earliest layer back to roughly 2600–2200 BCE — overlapping the Mature (urban) Harappan phase — complicating that tidy migration story.
