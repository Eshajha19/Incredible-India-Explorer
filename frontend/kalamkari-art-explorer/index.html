Kalamkari Heritage

The ancient storytelling art of hand-painted and block-printed textiles.

The Pen and the Craft

'Kalamkari' is derived from the Persian words 'Kalam' (pen) and 'Kari' (craftsmanship). This ancient art form flourished in the temples of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, where artists created large scrolls and temple hangings depicting Hindu epics.

Historically, groups of traveling artists known as 'Chitrakattis' would move from village to village, singing ballads and illustrating them on the spot using Kalamkari scrolls.

Two Distinct Styles

Srikalahasti Style

Technique: Entirely hand-painted using a 'kalam' (bamboo pen).

Themes: Strictly religious and mythological, often created as temple hangings or scrolls.

Flourished: Under the patronage of local temples and royalty.

Machilipatnam Style

Technique: Uses hand-carved wooden blocks for outlines and printing, with hand-painting for details.

Themes: Floral patterns, creepers, and secular motifs designed for textiles and garments.

Flourished: Under the Golconda Sultanate and later for export trade.

Fabric Types

Kalamkari is traditionally done on pure cotton fabric. The cloth is treated with a mixture of cow dung and bleach to achieve a uniform off-white base, then soaked in buffalo milk to prevent the dyes from smudging.

Design Motifs

  • The Tree of Life: A central motif symbolizing the connection between heaven, earth, and the underworld.
  • Peacocks & Parrots: Frequently depicted amidst floral creepers, symbolizing beauty and fidelity.
  • Lotus Medallions: Used as borders and corner pieces, representing purity.
  • Mythological Epics: Scenes from the Ramayana, Mahabharata, and Puranas.

Cloth Treatment

The cotton is soaked in a mixture of buffalo milk and myrobalan (astringent fruit) to prepare the fibers for dye absorption. It is then washed and sun-dried multiple times.

Charcoal Sketching

Artists draw the intricate outlines directly onto the fabric using a piece of burnt tamarind wood or charcoal. No stencils are used in the Srikalahasti style.

Applying Mordant

Iron (for black) and Alum (for red and other colors) are applied using the kalam. These metallic salts react with the natural dyes to fix the color permanently.

Natural Dyeing

The fabric is boiled in vats of natural dyes. Red comes from madder root, yellow from pomegranate rinds or mango bark, blue from indigo, and black from rusted iron and jaggery.
