Deccan Riots of 1875

🌾 The Uprising

What were the Deccan Riots?

Context

The Deccan Riots (May–September 1875) were a series of agrarian uprisings by the kunbi peasant caste in the districts of Pune, Satara, and Ahmednagar in the Bombay Presidency. Unlike the Indigo revolts of Bengal or the tribal huls of central India, these were class-based movements targeting moneylenders (mostly Marwari and Brahmin sahukars) rather than the colonial state directly.

The riots were not random violence but targeted attacks on the bonds, account books, and mortgage deeds that enslaved peasants in debt. The underlying cause was the colonial revenue system colluding with moneylender capitalism.

Immediate Trigger

In 1874, a moneylender named Kalaram in the village of Supa (Pune district) refused to accept a partial debt repayment and demanded the full bond amount. The villagers, led by local patels, collectively decided to boycott him.

This act of solidarity spread rapidly: social boycott became the main weapon. Moneylenders were denied grain, water, and services. Those who persisted in recovery were met with organized resistance. Within weeks, the movement had spread across dozens of villages.

💰 Economic Context

The Deccan Agrarian Economy

Kunbi Peasantry

The kunbis were the backbone of the Deccan — Maratha-speaking cultivators who owned small holdings. Most lived at subsistence level, vulnerable to poor monsoons, cattle deaths, and marriage expenses.

Cash-Crop Shift

After the American Civil War (1861–65), British demand for Indian cotton skyrocketed. Peasants switched from food crops to cotton, becoming dependent on volatile global markets. When prices crashed post-1865, they were left with debts.

Revenue Settlement

The ryotwari settlement imposed heavy cash revenue demands, payable on fixed dates regardless of harvest quality. Peasants were forced to borrow from moneylenders just to pay taxes.

The Debt Trap

Interest rates ranged from 25% to 50% annually. Compounded over generations, a small loan became an unpayable burden. Peasants became virtual serfs — their lands mortgaged, their labour pledged.

Civil Courts

British civil courts consistently upheld moneylender claims based on signed bonds. Illiterate peasants who had signed blank papers or documents they couldn't read lost their lands in court.

Railway & Market Disruption

The new railways brought outside moneylenders (Marwaris, Gujaratis) into the Deccan, replacing traditional village patrons. These outsiders had no social ties to the community and aggressively enforced debts.

Revenue Pressures

Ryotwari settlements set revenue at 50% of crop value, payable in cash. A single bad monsoon pushed peasants into debt. Revenue was fixed rigidly, without regard for actual harvests.

Indebtedness & Bond Fraud

Illiterate peasants were tricked into signing blank papers or bonds for more than they borrowed. Moneylenders added fictitious interest and fees, inflating debts many times over.

Moneylender Dominance

Outside moneylenders (Marwaris, Brahmins, Gujaratis) lacked village social ties and exploited their legal advantages. They used courts aggressively to foreclose on land.

Colonial Legal System

Civil courts favoured moneylenders, treating signed bonds as sacred. Peasants had no access to lawyers, and court fees were prohibitive. Justice was inaccessible and hostile.

Loss of Traditional Patrons

Earlier, village sahukars had community ties and adjusted debts during bad years. New commercial moneylenders had no such obligations and demanded full repayment.

Post-Cotton Crash

The American Civil War cotton boom (1861–65) had drawn peasants into debt to expand cultivation. When prices collapsed in 1866, they were trapped in loans they could never repay.

⚔️ Forms of Protest

How the Peasants Fought

Social Boycott

The most effective weapon: villages collectively refused to supply moneylenders with grain, water, milk, or any services. Barbers, washermen, and labourers refused service. Without village support, moneylenders could not operate and fled.

Bond Burning

Peasants seized account books, bonds, and mortgage deeds from moneylenders' homes and burned them publicly. The destruction of paper evidence destroyed the moneylenders' legal claims in court.

Non-Cooperation

Peasants refused to appear in court when summoned by moneylenders. They would rather face fines and imprisonment than acknowledge debts they considered fraudulent.

Peaceful Resistance

Remarkably, the riots were largely non-violent. Few deaths were recorded. The focus was on economic pressure, not physical harm. This discipline distinguished the Deccan Riots from other agrarian revolts.

📅 Chronology

Timeline of the Riots

1865

Cotton prices crash

After the American Civil War, global cotton demand collapses. Deccan peasants who borrowed heavily during the boom are left with unpayable debts.

1874

Supa boycott begins

In Supa village (Pune district), peasants boycott moneylender Kalaram after he refuses partial debt repayment. The social boycott strategy is born.

May 1875

Movement spreads

The boycott strategy spreads to neighbouring villages. In Parsi (Pune), peasants forcibly recover bonds and burn them publicly.

Jun 1875

Widespread unrest

Riots erupt across Pune, Satara, and Ahmednagar districts. At least 33 villages are affected. Moneylenders' homes are raided and bonds burned.

Jul 1875

Government intervenes

The Bombay government, alarmed by the scale, deploys police. Several peasant leaders are arrested. However, the social boycott continues.

Aug 1875

Deccan Riots Commission

The British appoint a commission under Sir William Hunter to investigate the causes. The commission interviews thousands of peasants and moneylenders.

1876

Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act

Based on the commission's findings, the Act is passed. It allows courts to revise "unconscionable" debts, requires moneylenders to maintain proper accounts, and protects cultivators' essential land from sale.

1879

Act amended

The Act is amended to strengthen protections. However, enforcement remains weak, and moneylenders find new ways around the law.

📜 Legislative Response

British Response & Legacy

Deccan Riots Commission (1875)

The British government commissioned an extensive inquiry. Its report documented systematic exploitation by moneylenders, including fraudulent account-keeping, inflated debts, and coercive legal tactics. The report was unusually sympathetic to peasant grievances.

Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act (1879)

The Act empowered courts to reopen past debt cases and revise unconscionable interest. It required moneylenders to register and keep accurate accounts. Certain essential assets (ploughs, cattle, seed) were protected from attachment.

Precedent for Future Reforms

The Act became the model for later agricultural debt relief laws across British India: Punjab Land Alienation Act (1900), Bengal Tenancy Act amendments, and eventually the post-independence agrarian reforms.

Nationalist Critique

The riots became a key example used by early Indian nationalists like M.G. Ranade, R.C. Dutt, and Dadabhai Naoroji to argue the "drain of wealth" theory — that colonial policies impoverished India's peasantry.

Historical Significance

  • First major class-based peasant movement in colonial India, distinct from tribal or religious revolts
  • Demonstrated the effectiveness of social boycott as a non-violent weapon — a tactic later adopted by Gandhi
  • Forced the colonial state to admit systemic injustice and legislate protections
  • Influenced nationalist economic thought: the "drain of wealth" theory gained empirical support
  • Set the template for post-independence debt relief legislation in Maharashtra and beyond
  • Studied by historians like Charlesworth, Hardiman, and Guha as a model of subaltern resistance
