Mountbatten Plan Explorer

On 3 June 1947, Viceroy Lord Mountbatten announced the plan that finally settled how power would be transferred — partitioning British India into India and Pakistan and moving Independence forward by nearly a year. This explorer walks through the political decisions that led there, and the consequences that followed.

🌍 Political Situation in Early 1947

By early 1947, the Interim Government formed after the Cabinet Mission Plan was barely functioning — Congress and the Muslim League clashed on almost every decision, and the two sides no longer trusted each other enough to govern jointly. Communal violence that had begun with Direct Action Day in Calcutta (August 1946) had since spread to Noakhali, Bihar, and parts of Punjab, leaving thousands dead and deepening the political deadlock.

Facing a deteriorating law-and-order situation and a British public with little appetite for prolonged rule, Prime Minister Clement Attlee announced on 20 February 1947 that Britain intended to transfer power to Indian hands by June 1948 at the latest — with or without an agreement between Congress and the League. Attlee also announced that Lord Wavell would be replaced as Viceroy by Lord Louis Mountbatten, tasked specifically with overseeing this handover.

This declaration was a turning point: for the first time, Britain committed to a firm deadline for withdrawal, regardless of whether Indian leaders reached a unity settlement — placing enormous pressure on Congress and the League to resolve their differences quickly, or risk a chaotic, ungoverned transfer of power.

🎖️ Lord Mountbatten's Arrival

Lord Louis Mountbatten arrived in New Delhi and was sworn in as India's last Viceroy on 24 March 1947. A decorated naval commander and the last Supreme Allied Commander of South East Asia, he brought a reputation for decisive action and was given unusually wide personal authority to negotiate a settlement.

  • Rapid consultations: Within weeks, Mountbatten held a series of one-on-one talks with Jawaharlal Nehru, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Sardar Patel, Mahatma Gandhi, and Sikh leaders to gauge whether a united India was still possible.
  • Verdict on unity: He concluded that Jinnah would not budge from the demand for a separate Pakistan, and that continued deadlock risked wider civil war.
  • Accelerated timetable: Rather than wait until June 1948, Mountbatten decided an early, clean transfer of power — with Partition — was the fastest way to stop the violence from spreading further.

His plan for partition was drafted through April and May 1947, refined after London's approval, and finally announced as the 3 June Plan.

⚖️ Congress, Muslim League & Sikh Positions

Jawaharlal Nehru and Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel had grown convinced that a weak, divided centre under compulsory grouping (as in the Cabinet Mission Plan) was unworkable, and that a smaller but strong, unified India was preferable to endless deadlock with the League. The Congress Working Committee reluctantly accepted Partition in early June 1947 as the price of a swift, orderly transfer of power.

Muhammad Ali Jinnah and the Muslim League had demanded a fully sovereign Pakistan since the Lahore Resolution of 1940. The League accepted the 3 June Plan as it finally guaranteed a separate Muslim-majority state, though Jinnah was unhappy that Bengal and Punjab would themselves be partitioned rather than transferred to Pakistan whole.

Sikh leaders, notably Master Tara Singh, were alarmed that Partition would split Punjab — the Sikh community's homeland — down the middle. They pressed for safeguards and a fair boundary award, but ultimately could not prevent Punjab's division, which triggered some of the worst violence and displacement of 1947.

Mahatma Gandhi opposed Partition to the last, viewing it as a betrayal of a united, pluralistic India, and unsuccessfully proposed alternatives such as offering Jinnah the leadership of an undivided interim government. He was overruled by the Congress Working Committee, which judged Partition the only realistic way to end the deadlock and stop the violence.

📜 The 3 June Plan

Mountbatten announced the plan in a radio broadcast on 3 June 1947, alongside Nehru, Jinnah, and Sikh representative Baldev Singh, who each endorsed it on air. Its key decision points were:

1

Partition Accepted

British India would be partitioned into two independent dominions — India and Pakistan — abandoning the earlier goal of a single united successor state.

2

Punjab & Bengal Vote

The provincial legislatures of Punjab and Bengal would each vote, split by district, on whether their province should be partitioned between India and Pakistan.

3

Referendums

The North-West Frontier Province and the Sylhet district of Assam would hold referendums to decide which dominion to join.

4

Advanced Timetable

The transfer of power was moved forward from the planned June 1948 deadline to 15 August 1947 — barely ten weeks away.

The plan also set up a Boundary Commission to draw the actual borders in Punjab and Bengal, and left the roughly 565 princely states free to accede to either dominion (or, nominally, remain independent).

✂️ Partition Proposal & the Boundary Commission

With Punjab and Bengal both voting to partition, Mountbatten appointed British barrister Sir Cyril Radcliffe — who had never previously visited India — to chair two Boundary Commissions and draw the actual dividing lines, working from census, religious-majority, and other data with just over a month to decide.

  • Punjab Boundary Commission: Divided Punjab between West Punjab (Pakistan) and East Punjab (India).
  • Bengal Boundary Commission: Divided Bengal between East Bengal (Pakistan, later Bangladesh) and West Bengal (India).
  • The Radcliffe Line: Completed by mid-August 1947 but only made public on 17 August 1947 — two days after Independence — leaving millions unsure which country they now lived in.

The delayed, hurried announcement of the Radcliffe Line is widely seen as one of the most consequential decisions of the transfer of power: it triggered one of the largest mass migrations in human history, as an estimated 10–15 million people crossed the new border in fear, and communal violence claimed an estimated 1–2 million lives.

🏛️ The Indian Independence Act, 1947

To give the 3 June Plan legal force, the British Parliament passed the Indian Independence Act 1947, introduced on 4 July and receiving royal assent on 18 July 1947. Its key provisions:

  • Created two independent dominions — India and Pakistan — effective from 15 August 1947.
  • Ended British paramountcy over the princely states, leaving each free to accede to either dominion.
  • Abolished the title "Emperor of India" held by the British monarch.
  • Empowered the legislatures of each new dominion to act as constituent assemblies until their own constitutions were adopted.
  • Appointed Mountbatten as the first Governor-General of independent India, while Jinnah became Governor-General of Pakistan.

The speed of the Act — barely a month between passage and the actual transfer of power — left enormous administrative work (dividing the army, civil service, and government assets between the two dominions) to be completed in a matter of weeks.

🏁 Consequences of the Mountbatten Plan

  • Mass migration: An estimated 10–15 million people crossed the new India–Pakistan border in the months around Partition, making it one of the largest forced migrations in history.
  • Communal violence: Widespread killings, particularly in Punjab and Bengal, are estimated to have claimed 1–2 million lives.
  • Kashmir dispute: The ambiguous status of princely states, especially Jammu and Kashmir, sowed the seeds of a territorial dispute between India and Pakistan that continues today.
  • Divided institutions: The army, civil service, railways, and government assets were split between the two new dominions in a matter of weeks, straining both new states.
  • Long-term legacy: The 3 June Plan's compressed timetable is widely debated by historians as a trade-off between ending colonial rule and communal deadlock quickly, versus the human cost of an unprepared, rushed Partition.
