🏛️ Hellenistic Rule in Northwestern India

Indo-Greek Kingdom Explorer

The Indo-Greek Kingdom flourished from around 200 BCE to 10 CE across parts of Afghanistan, Pakistan, and northwestern India. Descended from Alexander the Great's Hellenistic successors in Bactria, more than thirty Indo-Greek kings left behind a striking legacy of bilingual coinage and East-meets-West cultural fusion.

📜 Historical Overview

The Indo-Greek Kingdom traces its roots to the Hellenistic Greco-Bactrian kingdom established in the wake of Alexander the Great's conquests. Around 200–180 BCE, the Greco-Bactrian king Demetrius I crossed the Hindu Kush mountains and pushed into northwestern India, founding what became a distinct Indo-Greek realm centered on Gandhara and the Punjab.

Over the next two centuries, the kingdom was ruled by more than thirty kings, often several at once, split across two rival lines — the Euthydemid branch (descended from Demetrius) and the Eucratid branch (descended from the usurper Eucratides I). Territory and power shifted constantly between them, with capitals at Taxila and later Sagala (modern Sialkot).

The kingdom reached its peak under Menander I ("Milinda"), whose realm stretched from the Kabul Valley to Mathura and who is remembered as much for his reported conversion to Buddhism as for his conquests. After Menander, the kingdom fragmented into smaller, competing principalities. The last known Indo-Greek king, Strato II, vanished from the historical record around 10 CE as the invading Indo-Scythians (Sakas) absorbed the remaining Greek territories — bringing over two centuries of Hellenistic rule in India to a quiet end.

🕰️ Timeline

c. 200 BCE

Demetrius I crosses the Hindu Kush from Bactria, founding Indo-Greek rule in northwestern India.

c. 180 BCE

Apollodotus I extends Indo-Greek territory deep into Sindh and Gujarat, with his coinage circulating widely.

c. 155 BCE

Menander I becomes king, expanding the realm to its greatest extent and pushing east as far as Mathura.

c. 130 BCE

Death of Menander I; his reported dialogue with the Buddhist monk Nagasena is later recorded in the Milinda Panha.

c. 10 CE

Strato II, the last known Indo-Greek king, disappears as the Indo-Scythians take control of the remaining territory.

👑 Major Rulers

Demetrius I (c. 200–180 BCE)

The Greco-Bactrian king who crossed the Hindu Kush into India, founding Indo-Greek rule and beginning the Euthydemid line of kings.

Apollodotus I (c. 180–160 BCE)

Extended Indo-Greek rule south into Sindh and Gujarat. His widely circulated coins were still in use at the port of Barygaza a century after his death.

Menander I "Milinda" (c. 165/155–130 BCE)

The most celebrated Indo-Greek king. He repelled rival Eucratid forces, expanded the kingdom to Mathura, and is remembered in the Buddhist text Milinda Panha for his philosophical dialogue with the monk Nagasena.

Eucratides I (c. 170–145 BCE)

A rival Greco-Bactrian usurper whose line contested Indo-Greek territory in the west, giving rise to the separate Eucratid branch of kings.

Strato II (c. 25 BCE–10 CE)

The last known Indo-Greek king. His disappearance around 10 CE marks the traditional end of Hellenistic rule in India.

🏺 Cultural Legacy

  • Bilingual coinage: Indo-Greek coins paired a Greek legend and royal portrait on one face with Kharosthi or Prakrit script on the other — a numismatic innovation and a rich historical record of the kingdom's kings
  • Hellenistic-Indian artistic fusion: Realistic Greek-style portraiture and sculptural technique blended with Indian subject matter, laying groundwork later built upon by the Gandhara school of art under the Kushans
  • Greco-Buddhism: Several Indo-Greek rulers, most famously Menander I, engaged deeply with Buddhism; the Milinda Panha remains a significant text in the Theravada Buddhist tradition
  • Cross-cultural exchange: The kingdom acted as a bridge between the Hellenistic Mediterranean world and the Indian subcontinent, carrying Greek artistic, philosophical, and administrative ideas eastward
  • Scientific and linguistic influence: Greek astronomical and astrological concepts, along with Greek loanwords, filtered into later Indian texts on astronomy and astrology
  • Urban centres: Cities such as Taxila and Sagala flourished as centres of trade and learning where Greek and Indian populations lived side by side
